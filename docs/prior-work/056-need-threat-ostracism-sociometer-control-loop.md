**Headline.**
1. Williams's temporal need-threat model is a **verbal state machine with no equations**. The version in print that I could read (2007) names the late stage "acceptance", not "resignation"; "resignation" is the 2009 label (confirmed only through secondary descriptions, Hartgerink's wording, and file 42). The late stage is untested experimentally.
2. The **reflexive effect is large and robust** (Hartgerink: first-measure d = -1.36, 95% CI [-1.54, -1.18], k = 120) but the model's **signature moderation claim failed**: first measures were moderated at least as much as last measures. And there is a **second published meta-analysis family that disagrees about the size** of the effect on mood and self-esteem (Gerber & Wheeler 2009 versus Blackhart et al. 2009).
3. The **dACC "social pain = physical pain" claim is unresolved**: the founding result is N = 13, a single exclusion block, fixed condition order, and peak-voxel correlations of 0.88 and 0.75; the best pattern-classifier evidence (Woo 2014, Wager 2013) says the representations are separate. For the algebra this does not matter: the control-loop claim does not need shared neural substrate.

## 1. Williams (2007, 2009): the temporal need-threat model

**Citations and verified DOIs (Crossref-checked this session).**
- Williams, K. D. (2007). Ostracism. *Annual Review of Psychology* 58, 425-452. DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.58.110405.085641. Resolves. **[read in full]**
- Williams, K. D. (2009). Ostracism: a temporal need-threat model. In M. P. Zanna (Ed.), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 41, 275-314 (Crossref lists 275-314; some citing papers say 279-314). DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(08)00406-1. Resolves (Crossref title "Chapter 6 Ostracism"). **[not read]**
- Williams, K. D., Cheung, C. K. T. & Choi, W. (2000). Cyberostracism: effects of being ignored over the Internet. *JPSP* 79, 748-762. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.748. Resolves. Cited from Williams 2007 `[read]` only.

**Their vocabulary.** Ostracism (being ignored and excluded), social exclusion, rejection; **reflexive** stage; **reflective** stage; **acceptance** stage (2007) / **resignation** stage (2009, secondary); **fundamental needs**: belonging, self-esteem, control, meaningful existence; **need threat**; **need fortification**; Cyberball; "social monitoring system"; "sociometer".

**Williams 2007, the sequence as stated `[read]`** (p. 431; paraphrased in our own words, the passage is publisher copyright and is not reproduced):
Williams proposes three steps (citing Williams 1997, 2001 and Williams & Zadro 2005): (a) a reflexive, painful response to any form of ostracism, not moderated by situation or individual differences; (b) threats to the needs for belonging, self-esteem, control and meaningful existence, with more sadness and anger; (c) a reflective stage, shaped by appraisals of the situation, the source and the reasons for the ostracism, and by dispositions of the target, in which the person works to fortify whichever needs are most threatened. If relational needs (belonging, self-esteem) are the most threatened, responses tend to be prosocial; if efficacy and existence/recognition needs are, responses may be controlling, provocative or antisocial. With repeated or long-lasting ostracism the capacity to fortify needs is said to erode, giving helplessness, alienation and despair. The original passage is described as the model's whole formal content.

**The late stage, 2007 wording `[read]`.** Section "Acceptance stage: responses to chronic ostracism": "individuals' resources are depleted because they have had to endure long-term ostracism"; "there is not much research on this stage yet". Evidence offered: Allen & Badcock (2003) social risk theory of depression; Cacioppo & Hawkley (2005) on loneliness and learned helplessness; and Zadro (2004), 28 coded interviews with long-term targets of the silent treatment, who "accepted alienation and isolation", "accepted low self-worth", "expressed helplessness", "became depressed". Williams himself flags the sample as self-selected and causation as unknowable. He also says the life-alone paradigm and highly lonely people "may be tapping into this third temporal stage".

**The competing-needs mechanism `[read]`** (pp. 443-444): "Although belonging and self-esteem threats may motivate individuals to please others, control and meaningful existence threats might motivate aggressive and provocative responses. When these motives compete, there may be ambivalent response tendencies (Warburton & Williams 2005)." Also: "In this case, a desire to be noticed may supplant a desire to be liked." Williams labels this "speculative" in the text.

**Effect-size statements in Williams 2007 `[read]`.** "The typical effect size of ostracism on self-reported distress (as measured by moods and need threat) is high, between 1.0 and 2.0." Dose-response: Williams et al. (2000) found distress "linearly associated with the amount of ostracism" (included only twice then never again > included one-sixth of throws > inclusion > over-inclusion). Recalled social-pain episodes, especially ostracism, scored on the McGill pain inventory at levels "comparable to" meta-analytic chronic back pain and childbirth (Williams & Fitness 2004; secondary to this review).

**Resistance to moderation, as Williams argued in 2007 `[read]`.** No moderation of distress by trait self-esteem, individualism-collectivism, introversion-extraversion, gender, loneliness, need for belonging, social anxiety. Distress is no lower if told the ostracisers are a script, or a computer (Zadro et al. 2004); no moderation by despised outgroup membership (Gonsalkorale & Williams 2006), by cost of a throw, or ball versus bomb (van Beest & Williams). Counter-evidence he reports: Baumeister/Twenge life-alone studies find no mood effect, which he reads as helplessness-induced "cognitive deconstruction"; a Cyberball-like game with the human features removed and no visualisation instruction produced no distress (Law & Williams 2006). Summary point 3: "Cognitive factors … and personality factors … appear to have little influence in determining the detection of ostracism or the pain that it initially brings."

**Operators / equations.** None. `[read]` (2007). 2009 `[not read]`; secondary descriptions and file 42 also report none. The model is: ordered stages; a four-component need vector that is lowered; a fork (prosocial versus antisocial) set by which components are lowest; a late absorbing state of depleted resources.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** (i) Reflexive distress is unmoderated; (ii) delayed responses are moderated; (iii) lowest-need determines pro- versus antisocial fortification; (iv) chronic exposure depletes fortification. Items (i) and (ii) are tested by Hartgerink; (iii) by Gerber & Wheeler (below); (iv) not experimentally.

## 2. Hartgerink, van Beest, Wicherts & Williams (2015): the meta-analytic test

**Citation.** Hartgerink, C. H. J., van Beest, I., Wicherts, J. M. & Williams, K. D. (2015). The ordinal effects of ostracism: a meta-analysis of 120 Cyberball studies. *PLoS ONE* 10(5), e0127002. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0127002. Resolves. Open access.

All numbers below were re-checked at validation against the full Europe PMC text (PMC4449005) `[read]`. Note that the abstract rounds the first-measure effect as "d > |1.4|" while the Results give -1.36.

- Corpus: 98 papers, 120 studies, N = 11,869; 52 factorial studies for moderation. One outlier (Oaten et al., d > 15) excluded. Hedges' g (reported as d); `metafor` 1.9-5 in R; random-effects and mixed-effects meta-regression, REML.
- **First measure** (k = 120): d = -1.36, 95% CI [-1.54, -1.18]; I² = 92.99%; τ² = 0.90 [0.70, 1.24]; Egger Z = -6.14, p < .001 (small-study asymmetry present).
- **Last measure** (k = 95): mixed-effects model with elapsed time as predictor, intercept d = -0.76, 95% CI [-0.91, -0.61]; the paper's summary of the average last-measure effect is about -0.73, 95% CI [-0.86, -0.59]. Both figures are in the Results; they come from two different models (with and without the time predictor), so this is not an internal inconsistency (corrected at validation). Mean elapsed time 4.85 minutes; time did not predict the effect (b = 0.0069, p = .187, 95% CI [-0.0034, 0.0172]).
- **Moderation (interaction) effects.** First measure (k = 52): Δd = -0.46 [-0.64, -0.28]. Last measure (k = 46): Δd = -0.20 [-0.402, 0.002], p = .052 from the mixed-effects model with time; Table 3 (no time predictor) gives -0.19 [-0.40, 0.02], p = .069, and the Discussion quotes -.19 (two models, not an error); after removing three outliers Δd = -0.32 [-0.60, -0.03], p = .029. Time since ostracism: b = 0.011, p = .159.
- **Fundamental needs.** Interaction, restricted to measures referring to feelings during or after the game: first measure Δd = -0.37 [-0.60, -0.14] (k = 29); last measure Δd = -0.13 [-0.53, 0.27] (k = 8). Composite need satisfaction: immediate Δd = -0.18 [-0.47, -0.11] (k = 15), delayed Δd = -0.93 [-1.67, -0.19] (k = 3, fragile). The fetch says simple need effects are only in Figure 3; mood estimates are not reported separately.
- Homogeneous subset (k = 19): d = -2.05 [-2.44, -1.65].
- Sample composition: about 39% male, mean age about 20.5, 52% US, 45% other Western, 3% Asian.
- **Power:** 2,186 participants ("2186", G*Power, power .8) would be needed to detect the interaction at both time points; typical factorial study mean n ≈ 110; power ≈ .08 for the last-measure interaction.

**Test of the model, stated carefully.** Williams predicted moderation is "less likely to occur for reflexive measures than for reflective measures". Across all outcomes, first measures were moderated **more** than last, so the proposition lacks support overall. For the need-satisfaction composite the predicted pattern did appear (delayed more moderated), but k = 3. The authors argue many "first" measures were not truly reflexive ("Anything taken after the game, or assessed by wording indicating present state" should not be assumed reflexive), and that no fixed window defines either measure. They propose: (1) reflexive-stage moderation is possible (acetaminophen, TMS, schizotypal traits); (2) time-to-measurement matters, with recovery varying from 5-10 minutes in most studies to 45 minutes in socially anxious participants.

**Empirical standing, blunt.** The immediate effect is as large and as well-replicated as anything in social psychology, but: it is mostly self-report; the sample is young, Western, and student; asymmetry (Egger) and heterogeneity (I² 93%) are both high; and the temporal claim that makes it a *dynamics* model is untested by design, because "last" means about five minutes. The resignation stage has no experimental test.

**Recent boundary test.** Fairbairn, C. E., Creswell, K. G., Hales, A. H., Williams, K. D. & Wilkins, K. V. (2022). Mixing misery and gin: the effect of alcohol administration on ostracism response. *Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 48(8), 1269-1283 (online 2021). DOI 10.1177/01461672211038450 (Crossref-resolved at validation) `[abstract read]`: three studies, N = 438, alcohol did not reduce ostracism effects on mood or need satisfaction in intoxicated versus sober participants. Supports "difficult to diminish", the claim Hartgerink questions.

## 3. Two other meta-analyses, which disagree

These are not in the seeds. They matter because they test what the control-loop reading needs: effect sizes on self-esteem and the pro/antisocial fork.

- **Gerber, J. & Wheeler, L. (2009).** On being rejected: a meta-analysis of experimental research on rejection. *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 4, 468-488. DOI 10.1111/j.1745-6924.2009.01158.x. Resolves. `[abstract read]`: 88 studies. Rejection lowers mood (d = -0.50) and self-esteem (d = -0.70); no decrease in arousal and no flattened affect ("needs account", not "numbness account"). Belonging (d = 0.69) and control (d = 1.16) frustrated. Measures that contrast belonging and control (d = -1.17) produce antisocial responding; measures that "do not allow for control to be restored cause prosocial responding (d = 1.21)". Their gloss: people "will prioritize restoring control even if it requires being antisocial." Signs follow the abstract's own convention; I have not seen how d is signed in the tables.
- **Blackhart, G. C., Nelson, B. C., Knowles, M. L. & Baumeister, R. F. (2009).** Rejection elicits emotional reactions but neither causes immediate distress nor lowers self-esteem: a meta-analytic review of 192 studies on social exclusion. *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 13, 269-309. DOI 10.1177/1088868309346065. Resolves. `[abstract read]`: rejection causes "a significant shift toward a more negative emotional state" but "typically … an emotionally neutral state marked by low levels of both positive and negative affect"; "Self-esteem among rejected persons was no different from neutral controls"; acceptance raised self-esteem moderately.

**Conflict, not reconciled here.** Self-esteem after rejection: d = -0.70 (88 studies) versus "no different from neutral controls" (192 studies). Both use the term meta-analysis on overlapping literatures. The 192-study set evidently includes the life-alone and similar paradigms where, per Williams 2007, mood effects are absent; Gerber & Wheeler's narrower sample may exclude them, but I have not read either inclusion criteria, so this is a hypothesis, not a finding. Neither abstract gives CIs. This is directly relevant to the sociometer because Blackhart's result (rejection does not lower state self-esteem relative to a *neutral* control) is a challenge to the sociometer's central prediction.

## 4. The sociometer: Leary et al. (1995), Leary & Baumeister (2000)

**Citations (Crossref-resolved).**
- Leary, M. R., Tambor, E. S., Terdal, S. K. & Downs, D. L. (1995). Self-esteem as an interpersonal monitor: the sociometer hypothesis. *JPSP* 68(3), 518-530. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.68.3.518.
- Leary, M. R. & Baumeister, R. F. (2000). The nature and function of self-esteem: sociometer theory. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 32, 1-62. DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(00)80003-9.

**Not read at source.** What follows is `[secondary]` from search summaries of the 1995 abstract and descriptions of the 2000 chapter, plus Williams 2007's one-sentence statement `[read]`: sociometer theory "asserts that self-esteem is a gauge of relational valuation that, when low, signals the individual that changes must be made to improve inclusionary status."

**Their vocabulary.** Sociometer; relational value / relational evaluation; inclusionary status; state versus trait self-esteem; "indicative" and "imperative" functions (the latter from a 2016 review described in search results; not checked).

**Drives/operands.** One regulated variable: perceived relational value. State self-esteem is the readout; trait self-esteem is described `[secondary]` as the accumulated or resting level of the readout. The behavioural motive is not "maintain self-esteem" but "avoid social devaluation and rejection".

**Operators / equations.** No equation in anything I could see. `[secondary]` Five-study design (1995): Study 1 state self-esteem changes parallel assumed acceptance/rejection implications; Studies 3-4: exclusion lowered self-esteem when the reason was personal but not when random (no self-presentation mediation); Study 5: trait self-esteem correlates highly with how generally included one feels.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** (i) State self-esteem tracks perceived inclusion more closely than performance or other private evaluation; (ii) low readout motivates repair; (iii) exclusion lowers readout when attributable to the self. Prediction (ii), the control action, is the part search summaries rated "mixed" (2016 review).

**Empirical standing.** Supportive correlational and scenario evidence (Leary et al.); but the 1995 effects are mostly scenario and self-report, and Blackhart et al. 2009 report no self-esteem drop versus neutral control. Williams 2007 reports that Leary et al. (1998) found trait self-esteem did not moderate reactions to rejection `[read]`. So: a plausible and widely cited **gauge** model whose cleanest quantitative test is split.

## 5. Twenge et al. (2001): exclusion and aggression

**Citation.** Twenge, J. M., Baumeister, R. F., Tice, D. M. & Stucke, T. S. (2001). If you can't join them, beat them: effects of social exclusion on aggressive behavior. *JPSP* 81(6), 1058-1069. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.81.6.1058 (Crossref-resolved).

`[abstract read]` (EPMC): exclusion manipulated by telling people they would end up alone later in life, or that other participants had rejected them. Excluded participants gave a more negative job evaluation to someone who insulted them (Exps 1-2), and blasted a target with more aversive noise whether the target had insulted them (Exp 4) or was a neutral stranger with no interaction (Exp 5). **Not** more aggressive toward someone who praised them (Exp 3). "These responses were specific to social exclusion (as opposed to other misfortunes) and were not mediated by emotion." Sample sizes not obtained.

**Standing.** The fork "exclusion → antisocial" in the model is mostly supported by this lab's life-alone work (which reports no mood change, an anomaly for a need-threat reading) and by Gerber & Wheeler's control-priority finding. The 2001 result is a single-lab, five-experiment paper; I did not find a registered replication. Note the key tension: Twenge finds aggression **without** mood change; Williams reads Cyberball as mood and need threat **with** both pro- and antisocial outcomes. These are two mechanisms, not one.

## 6. The dACC claim: Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams (2003) onward

**Citation.** Eisenberger, N. I., Lieberman, M. D. & Williams, K. D. (2003). Does rejection hurt? An fMRI study of social exclusion. *Science* 302(5643), 290-292. DOI 10.1126/science.1089134 (Crossref-resolved). **[read in full]**

**What was done `[read]`.** Three scans, fixed order: implicit exclusion (ISE; watching the other two "players" because the link was said to be down); inclusion; explicit social exclusion (ESE; 7 throws, then excluded for the rest of the scan, ~45 throws). Participants believed the other players were real. A single ESE period; "the conditions were always implemented in the same order". Sample size is not in the text of the report I extracted (N = 13 comes from a search result `[secondary]`).

**Equations / statistics `[read]` (verbatim values):**
- ESE vs inclusion, dorsal ACC (x = -8, y = 20, z = 40): t = 3.36, r = 0.71, P < 0.005.
- Distress correlated with ACC activity in that contrast: (x = -6, y = 8, z = 45) r = 0.88, P < 0.005; (x = -4, y = 31, z = 41) r = 0.75, P < 0.005.
- Anterior insula (x = 42, y = 16, z = 1): t = 4.07, r = 0.78; **not** associated with distress.
- Right ventral PFC (RVPFC): (x = 42, y = 27, z = -11) t = 4.26, r = 0.79; (x = 37, y = 50, z = 1) t = 4.96, r = 0.83. RVPFC vs distress: (30, 34, -3) r = -0.68. RVPFC vs ACC: r = -0.81.
- Mediation: ACC mediates RVPFC → distress, Sobel Z = 3.16, P < 0.005; residual direct path β = -0.17, P > 0.5.
- ISE vs inclusion, ACC: (x = -6, y = 21, z = 41) t = 4.34, r = 0.78; no RVPFC even at P = .05. Interpretation in the paper: ACC registered exclusion but "did not generate a self-regulatory response"; explicit awareness may be needed to attribute and regulate.
- Threshold: uncorrected P = 0.005 with cluster size 10 (their footnote 23: per-voxel false positive probability < 0.000001, citing Forman et al. 1995).
- Behavioural: "participants felt ignored and excluded during ESE (t = 5.33, P < 0.05)".

**Their argument `[read]`.** The social attachment system "may have piggybacked onto the physical pain system"; dACC is a neural "alarm system" or conflict monitor; dACC signals the affective, not sensory, component of pain; RVPFC regulates by disrupting ACC. The authors themselves flag the competing attention account and answer it with (1) the ACC-distress correlation and (2) greater ACC activity in ISE than inclusion despite lower attention demand. They acknowledge missing controls.

**Observations, mine.** The reported correlations are at peak voxels chosen for the contrast, a design known to inflate correlation (`[standard account]`: the 2009 "voodoo correlations" critique). The report gives no CIs. The ACC-distress correlation rests on a single ESE block in a fixed order; with a small N, r = 0.88 is not a stable estimate.

**Eisenberger (2012)** (The neural bases of social pain. *Psychosomatic Medicine* 74(2), 126-135, DOI 10.1097/psy.0b013e3182464dd1, Crossref-resolved; PMC3273616, first 100,000 characters read via fetch). Claims: dACC and anterior insula track the affective component of physical pain; social exclusion, rejection feedback, rejection cues and bereavement activate them; people with overlapping responses to rejection recall and heat pain; pain sensitivity should correlate across domains, and factors that alter one should alter the other (baseline heat sensitivity, OPRM1 genotype, endotoxin, social support, acetaminophen). Concedes "it is not yet clear if the activations observed in the studies reviewed here are indicative of pain per se", and that the two pains do not fully overlap. Evolutionary argument: caregiver dependence led the attachment system to co-opt "the pain system, borrowing the pain signal" (Panksepp 1998). Samples cited (re-checked at validation `[read]`): OPRM1 study n = 125 genotyped, n = 30 scanned; endotoxin n = 39; test statistics only, no effect sizes.

**The controversy, with verified sources.**
- **Somerville, Heatherton & Kelley (2006)**: dACC sensitive to expectancy violation, ventral ACC to social acceptance (described in the SCAN editorial `[read]`; the paper itself not retrieved).
- **Rotge, Lemogne, Hinfray, Huguet, Grynszpan, Tartour, George & Fossati (2015; online 2014)**. A meta-analysis of the anterior cingulate contribution to social pain. *SCAN* 10(1), 19-27. DOI 10.1093/scan/nsu110 (Crossref-resolved). `[abstract read]`: 46 articles, 940 subjects, mostly Cyberball. Significant activation likelihood in **both** ventral and dorsal ACC for social pain and for self-reported distress; distress involves "more specifically the subgenual and pregenual ACC"; Cyberball engages anterior midcingulate "to a lesser extent than other experimental tasks"; children subgenual > adults; ventro-dorsal gradient related to exclusion-phase length. Eisenberger's editorial `[read]` (Meta-analytic evidence for the role of the anterior cingulate cortex in social pain, SCAN 10(1), 1-2, DOI 10.1093/scan/nsu120) calls the dACC-versus-vACC debate a "pendulum" over decades and cites Kawamoto 2012 and Cooper 2014 as controlling expectancy violation with dACC still responsive, versus Masten 2009 and Bolling 2011 as showing vACC.
- **Kross, Berman, Mischel, Smith & Wager (2011)**. PNAS 108, 6270-6275, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1102693108 (resolves). `[abstract read]`: strong-rejection manipulation (viewing an ex-partner photo after an unwanted break-up) activated secondary somatosensory cortex and dorsal posterior insula, regions supporting the *sensory* part of pain; diagnostic of physical pain with PPV up to 88% in a database of 500+ studies.
- **Wager, Atlas, Lindquist, Roy, Woo & Kross (2013)**. An fMRI-based neurologic signature of physical pain. NEJM 368(15), 1388-1397, DOI 10.1056/nejmoa1204471 (Crossref-resolved). `[abstract read]`: four studies, 114 participants; pain signature discriminated heat from warmth at 93-94%; **social pain versus physical pain at 85% sensitivity (CI 76-94) and 73% specificity (CI 61-84)**, and 95% in a forced-choice test of which condition was more painful.
- **Woo, Koban, Kross, Lindquist, Banich, Ruzic, Andrews-Hanna & Wager (2014)**. Separate neural representations for physical pain and social rejection. Nature Communications 5, 5380 (ncomms6380; DOI 10.1038/ncomms6380, Crossref-resolved) `[abstract read]`: 60 participants, classifiers 92% (pain) and 80% (rejection) out-of-sample; each at chance on the other; uncorrelated even within dACC; distinct connectivity (N = 91 resting-state). "Rather than co-opting pain circuitry, rejection involves distinct affective representations in humans."
- **Lieberman & Eisenberger (2015)**. PNAS 112, 15250-15255. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1515083112 (resolves). `[abstract read]`: Neurosynth reverse inference "of over 10,000" fMRI studies; concludes the "best psychological description of dACC function was related to pain processing--not executive, conflict, or salience processing".
- **Yarkoni's rebuttal** (blog, talyarkoni.org, Dec 2015 `[secondary, read via fetch]`; a blog, not peer-reviewed, and I have not seen L&E's reply in full): z-scores are not probabilities; "pain" base rate in Neurosynth about 3.5%, "memory" about 16%, "motor" about 18%; empirical-prior pain posteriors peak about 15-20% (posterior insula), most dACC voxels 10% or less; "motor" posterior higher than "pain" throughout dACC; "fear" no voxels above 5%.

**Weight of evidence on the dACC claim (my reading).** Against *identical* substrate: Woo 2014, Wager 2013 (73% specificity is far from chance classifier overlap, and it separates the two), Kross 2011 (overlap is in sensory regions only for strong rejection), Rotge 2015 (the effect is not dACC-specific), Yarkoni on reverse inference. For *overlapping alarm function*: dACC and insula responses to rejection are replicated across many studies, per Rotge's meta-analysis, and acetaminophen reduced both self-reported social pain over three weeks (Exp 1) and dACC/anterior-insula responses to rejection (Exp 2) in DeWall et al. 2010 (Psychol Sci 21, 931-937, DOI 10.1177/0956797610374741, Crossref-resolved, `[abstract read]`); sample sizes not retrieved, and I found no preregistered replication. Fairbairn et al. 2022 found alcohol did not diminish the response, which sits oddly with a simple "borrowed pain signal that anaesthetics blunt" story.

## 7. Operand/operator translation: the control loop, what the sources permit

All items below are my construction from the sources. No cited author states them as equations.

**What the sources license.**
- **Operand: a need vector**, four components (belonging B, self-esteem S, control C, meaningful existence M), Williams `[read]`. Williams 2007 says the measures "usually show high levels of intercorrelation" and that there are "few compelling reasons to separate" them, which argues for a low-dimensional or single factor at the measurement level; the model's fork requires them to separate in the *response*. This tension is unresolved in the sources.
- **Operator: `exclude(a, b)`** lowers the need vector of b. Dose-response is linear in the proportion of throws (Williams et al. 2000 as reported in the 2007 review). Effect sizes: d about -1.36 (first measure, 95% CI [-1.54, -1.18], Hartgerink); decays toward d about -0.76 within about five minutes (last measure, a decay *rate* is not identified because time did not predict the effect, b = 0.0069 per minute, p = .187).
- **Set point.** The sociometer gives a regulated variable, perceived relational value, with a readout (state self-esteem) and trait level as resting value. No numerical set point, gain or time constant is stated in any source I read.
- **Control action / error response.** Williams's fortification is the closest to a control action: the *lowest need* selects the action. Gerber & Wheeler's d = 1.16 (control frustrated) versus 0.69 (belonging frustrated) fits the weak claim that control is hit harder than belonging, and the d = 1.21 versus -1.17 pair fits "when control can be restored, antisocial; when not, prosocial". A minimal rule that fits both: act to raise argmin_i (n_i - n_i*) where n* is a per-need set point, with the action class determined by i (relational → affiliative, efficacy → provocative). This is a hypothesis; Gerber & Wheeler's moderators are measurement-type categories, not within-person tests of an argmin rule.
- **Accumulation / absorbing state.** Chronic exposure depletes coping resources (Williams 2007), a one-way state. The sources give no rate, threshold or reversibility; Zadro's interviews are self-selected.
- **Coupling to other drives.** Williams 2007 cites tend-and-befriend (Taylor et al. 2000) as an alternative to fight/flight/freeze for exclusion, so the ostracism literature already sits next to the fear→affiliation question (D162).

**What the sources do not license.** A single loop gain; a time constant for the reflexive-to-reflective transition; a threshold for the resignation transition; any claim that dACC activity *is* the error signal; any group-level variable (all studies are one target and a few perpetrators).

## Exists versus does not exist

**Exists**
- Large, replicated reflexive effect of Cyberball-type ostracism on self-reported need satisfaction and mood (Hartgerink: k = 120, N = 11,869, d = -1.36).
- A published four-component need vocabulary and a verbal three-stage model with a pro/antisocial fork (Williams 2007, 2009).
- Two further meta-analyses on mood and self-esteem, which disagree (Gerber & Wheeler 2009; Blackhart et al. 2009).
- A gauge model of belonging (sociometer) with correlational and scenario support.
- A fifteen-year neuroimaging dispute about the dACC with meta-analyses on both sides and one open peer-reviewed refutation of the "identical pattern" claim (the phrase is from Woo et al. 2014, *Nature Communications*).

**Does not exist (or was not found)**
- Any equation, gain, time constant or set-point value for the belonging loop.
- A within-person test of the "lowest-need determines response" rule.
- Experimental evidence on the resignation / acceptance stage; chronic ostracism cannot be run in the lab. The best evidence is 28 self-selected interviews.
- A multi-agent version: every study has one target; no group-level state; no model of the perpetrators' side.
- A reconciliation of Gerber & Wheeler with Blackhart et al.
- A preregistered, adequately powered replication of the acetaminophen effect or the Twenge aggression paradigm (none found).
- A time-resolved Cyberball study that maps recovery at fixed intervals in one sample (Hartgerink calls for it).
