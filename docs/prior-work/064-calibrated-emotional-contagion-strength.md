## Headline findings

1. **The d of about 0.001 is not Kramer's headline and is internally inconsistent.** Kramer et al. report four effects. The two same-valence effects (positive posts reduced, positive words fall; negative posts reduced, negative words fall) are d = 0.02 each. The two cross-valence effects are d = 0.001 and d = 0.008. "As small as d = 0.001" is the smallest of the four and belongs to the effect on negative words when positive posts were cut. Its own test statistic, t = 2.71 on 310,044 df, implies d of about 0.0097 [computed], not 0.001. The other three reported d values reproduce from their t values to within rounding (0.0202, 0.0198, 0.0079). So the 0.001 is most likely a typo for 0.01, an inference I cannot confirm without the authors' data. Files 18 and 27 carry the figure forward as a headline ("as small as d = 0.001" labelled [from abstract]); it is not in the abstract (it is in the Discussion), and the right headline is d = 0.02.
2. **Every credible estimate of a text-based, expression-level contagion effect is small, and the large ones are observational.** Experimental platform estimate d about 0.02 per week-long exposure shift; natural-experiment estimate 1.3 to 1.75 additional posts per post [summary-fetch] (a different unit, section 5); observational estimates of persistence across years (25% for a nearby friend) are the largest and the least identified.
3. **The homophily critique is a theorem, not a quibble.** Shalizi and Thomas: latent homophily, contagion and covariate effects are generically confounded in observational network data. That means the observational layer (Framingham, Twitter exposure studies) cannot supply the algebra's gain on its own; only randomisation or an instrument can. Of the sources here, only Kramer (randomised), Coviello (rainfall instrument) and Muchnik (randomised ratings) identify a causal transmission effect, and none of those measures a drive or a mood; they measure expression.

## 1. Kramer, Guillory and Hancock (2014): the platform experiment

**Citation.** Kramer, A. D. I., Guillory, J. E., Hancock, J. T. (2014). Experimental evidence of massive-scale emotional contagion through social networks. *PNAS* 111(24), 8788-8790. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1320040111 (Crossref confirmed). Editorial Expression of Concern (Verma), PNAS 111(29), 10779, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1412469111, and a correction of the affiliation lines on the same page. Read: Stanford Social Media Lab copy of the PDF.

**Their vocabulary.** Emotional contagion operationalised as "exposure to verbal affective expressions leads to similar verbal expressions". Positive and negative post: contains at least one positive or negative word in LIWC2007. "Cross-emotional" effect. "Withdrawal effect".

**Drives/operands.** Affect only, as two separate non-opposed channels (positive word share, negative word share). "Negative and positive word use scarcely correlated [r = -0.04, t(620,587) = -38.01]" [read]. No drive in the algebra's sense.

**Design [read].** N = 689,003 English-language Facebook users. Two parallel experiments, 1 week, 11-18 January 2012. In each, when a person loaded the News Feed, each post containing the relevant emotion had "between a 10% and 90% chance (based on their User ID) of being omitted". Controls omitted a matched proportion of posts at random regardless of content (22.4% of posts contained negative words, 46.8% positive; so a 10% cut of positive posts is matched with a 4.68% random cut). About 155,000 participants per condition who posted at least once; over 3 million posts and 122 million words (4 million positive = 3.6%, 1.8 million negative = 1.6%). No text was seen by the researchers.

**Estimator [read].** Weighted linear regression of the person's percentage of positive (or negative) words on a condition dummy (experimental vs control), "weighted by the likelihood of that person having an emotional post omitted from their News Feed on a given viewing". Posting volume checked by Poisson regression (omitting emotional content reduced words produced; 99.7% as many words when negative omitted, 96.7% when positive omitted).

**Results, transcribed [read].**

| Condition | Outcome | B (percentage points) | t | P as printed | d as printed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| positive reduced | positive words | -0.1% | t(310,044) = -5.63 | < 0.001 | 0.02 |
| positive reduced | negative words | +0.04% | t = 2.71 | 0.007 | 0.001 |
| negative reduced | negative words | -0.07% | t(310,541) = -5.51 | < 0.001 | 0.02 |
| negative reduced | positive words | +0.06% | t = 2.19 | "< 0.003" | 0.008 |

Also: the stronger effect on posting volume when positive words were omitted (z = -77.9, P < 0.001); no negativity bias in a post hoc test of effect sizes (z = -0.09, P = 0.93; and z = -0.36, P = 0.72, using Fisher's method).

**Cross-check [computed].** With d approximately 2t/sqrt(df): 0.0202, 0.0097, 0.0198, 0.0079 against printed 0.02, 0.001, 0.02, 0.008. The second does not match, the others do. And t = 2.19 gives a two-sided P of about 0.029, not "< 0.003" (printed P is also a likely typo). Caveat: the regression is weighted, so d = 2t/sqrt(df) is approximate; but the same approximation reproduces three of four, so the one mismatch is informative.

**Authors' own interpretation [read].** "the effect sizes from the manipulations are small (as small as d = 0.001)". Defence: the manipulation was minimal, mood is hard to move, and "at Facebook's scale... hundreds of thousands of emotion expressions in status updates per day". Prentice and Miller 1992 cited for small effects mattering.

**Standing, blunt.** Randomised and large, so the sign is credible. Magnitude is tiny in expression terms: a shift of 0.1 percentage points on a base of roughly 5 percent positive words (the Fig. 1 axis spans about 5.0 to 5.4) [read, from the figure's axis] is a relative change of about 2%. What it does not show: that anyone's mood changed. It measures word choice in status updates after a week of reduced exposure. Three further limits: (a) the intervention removes content, so it tests a deprivation (reduced exposure) rather than added exposure; (b) the unit exposure is the feed, not a person, so the effect cannot be turned into a per-friend or per-post gain without the exposure counts, which the paper does not give; (c) one week, no persistence estimate. The authors' claim that this rules out mimicry ("the cross-emotional encourage-ment effect cannot be explained by mimicry alone") is an interpretation, not a test. The Expression of Concern concerns consent and the Cornell IRB, not the statistics. No replication of the experiment is known to me; Facebook has not released the data ("Data processing systems, per-user aggregates, and anonymized results available upon request").

**Operand/operator translation.** A clean randomised dose-response anchor: operator C (exposure to others' expressed affect) with an output of change in own expressed valence. Calibration: roughly 0.1 percentage points of positive-word share (about 2% relative) and d about 0.02, after a week with up to 90% of the matching content removed. It carries no information on what happens to a state variable.

## 2. The LIWC critique (measurement)

**What is read.** Kramer et al. themselves: LIWC2007, a post is "positive or negative if they contained at least one positive or negative word", correlated "with self-reported and physiological measures of well-being" (their citation 9: Pennebaker, Chung, Ireland, Gonzales, Booth 2007) [read].

**What is not read.** Panger 2016, "Reassessing the Facebook experiment: critical thinking about the validity of Big Data research", *Information, Communication & Society* 19(8), 1108-1126 (the Crossref record gives the print year of its issue and the online year 2015), DOI 10.1080/1369118X.2015.1093525. Existence and venue confirmed; no abstract or text retrieved this session. File 18's description of Panger is from search coverage, not the paper.

**Secondary coverage [secondary].** Grohol (BuzzFeed coverage): LIWC was not built for short texts; blog analysis: LIWC2007 scores "not" as negative, so "not happy" scores as both. These are plausible but unverified against the LIWC dictionary here.

**Calibrating the criticism.** The measurement-error criticism has a direction. Random misclassification of positive and negative words attenuates the estimated effect toward zero, which would make true d larger than 0.02, not smaller. It would bias in the other direction only if the misclassification correlated with the treatment (for example, a feed containing less emotional content changes the share of hedged or negated language). Kramer's design makes that unlikely at first order but does not rule it out. The same issue affects the other text estimates here (Coviello: LIWC; Ferrara and Yang: SentiStrength; Brady: moral-emotional dictionary). The honest reading is that the LIWC critique is an unknown attenuation factor on the expression-level effect, not a reason to doubt the sign. A validation study of LIWC versions on emotion (JMIR Formative, e18246) found good sensitivity across LIWC2001/2007/2015 [secondary, from a search snippet, not read]. Unconfirmed: any estimate of reliability of the LIWC positive/negative categories on Facebook status updates specifically.

## 3. Coviello et al. (2014): the natural experiment

**Citation.** Coviello, L., Sohn, Y., Kramer, A. D. I., Marlow, C., Franceschetti, M., Christakis, N. A., Fowler, J. H. (2014). Detecting emotional contagion in massive social networks. *PLoS ONE* 9(3), e90315. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0090315 (Crossref confirmed title and year; the author list is from my memory and the fetch did not state it; treat as [standard account]). [summary-fetch of the PLOS page.]

**Design.** 1,180 days (January 2009 to March 2012), English-speaking Facebook users in the 100 most populous US cities. LIWC positive and negative rates per city-day. Instrument: daily rainfall. Identification: rain affects the focal city's mood and, through friendships, the mood of friends in cities without rain. Only days on which the focal city has no rain are used for the contagion model, to protect the exclusion restriction. The paper reports placebo tests with future weather (no backward-in-time contagion) and controls for weather-related posts [summary-fetch].

**Model, in words (formulas were images in the fetch).** Stage 1: friends' emotion predicted from friends' rainfall plus time and city fixed effects. Stage 2: city-day average emotion regressed on predicted friends' emotion (weighted by tie strength and degree) with fixed effects; the coefficient is gamma. Unit of gamma: additional posts of an emotion by friends per additional post of that emotion in the user's city [summary-fetch].

**Estimates (95% CI in brackets) [summary-fetch].** Rainy day lowers positive posts by 1.19% [0.78, 1.60] and raises negative by 1.16% [0.78, 1.55]. One additional positive post causes 1.75 [1.51, 1.99] more positive posts among friends; one additional negative post causes 1.29 [1.19, 1.38] more negative posts. Cross-effects: positive reduces friends' negative by 1.80 [1.27, 2.33]; negative reduces friends' positive by 1.26 [0.81, 1.70]. Positive vs negative contagion differ at p = 0.001; the cross-effects differ at p = 0.12. Ratio of indirect to direct effect about 1.5 to 1, against about 2 to 1 for giving in a public-goods experiment and about 4 to 1 for voting mobilisation, which the authors cite as benchmarks. The paper says it cannot estimate contagion within a city.

**Reconciling with Kramer.** The numbers are not commensurable and should not be placed in one column. Coviello's gamma is a multiplier in posts per post at the city-day level; Kramer's is a percentage-point shift in word share per person after a week of altered exposure. A gamma above 1 per post cannot be a person-to-person gain (if each post caused more than one further post the system would be supercritical); read it as "extra posts among all friends per extra post of the user's city", a sum over a large friend set. That is a branching-count interpretation I am inferring, not the authors' statement. Two sources of upward bias to flag: (a) rain changes what people write about (weather, topics), so shared topic exposure rather than mood transmission; the paper's weather-word control addresses part of it; (b) the instrument shifts posting behaviour and mood together, so the exclusion restriction (rain affects friends' posts only through the focal city's emotion) fails if rain also changes how much a person posts and thereby the volume of content seen. The authors' use of only no-rain days addresses the main route but not these.

**Standing.** The only identified natural-experiment estimate on a platform. Not replicated independently as far as I could find. No replication search was run beyond the Ferrara and Yang citation.

**Operand/operator translation.** Gives a positive-negative asymmetry (contagion stronger for positive, p = 0.001) and an inhibition cross-term. The structure matches a two-channel signed coupling matrix (positive and negative expression as separate variables with own and cross gains), which is more informative than a scalar gain. The magnitudes need the branching-count interpretation above before use.

## 4. Fowler and Christakis (2008): the observational claim

**Citation.** Fowler, J. H., Christakis, N. A. (2008). Dynamic spread of happiness in a large social network: longitudinal analysis over 20 years in the Framingham Heart Study. *BMJ* 337, a2338. DOI 10.1136/bmj.a2338 (Crossref confirmed). Open access (PMC2600606). Read: abstract, Methods and statistical analysis.

**Data [read].** 4,739 individuals followed 1983-2003; happiness from a four-item scale; ties from the Framingham offspring cohort (5,124 egos and their alters).

**Model [read].** Regression of ego happiness on ego age, sex, education, **ego happiness in the previous exam**, and alter happiness in the current exam and in the previous exam; fitted by generalised estimating equations with independent working correlation; reported as risk ratios; effect sizes from simulating the first difference in alter contemporaneous happiness from 0 to 1 with 1,000 draws. In symbols (my notation): H_ego(t) = f(age, sex, edu, H_ego(t-1), H_alter(t), H_alter(t-1)), contagion coefficient on H_alter(t). The authors name three explanations: induction, homophily and confounding. Their separation argument: lagged ego happiness "substantially controls for ego's genetic endowment and any intrinsic stable predilection to be happy"; lagged alter happiness "helps to control for homophily"; direction of friendship ties (nominated vs nominating) tests unobserved shared exposure, since "if unobserved factors drive the association... then directionality of friendship should not be relevant".

**Results [read: abstract].** A friend within a mile who becomes happy raises the probability of being happy by 25% (95% CI 1% to 57%); coresident spouse 8% (0.2% to 16%); sibling within a mile 14% (1% to 28%); next-door neighbour 34% (7% to 70%); no effect between coworkers; effect decays with time and distance; extends to three degrees of separation.

**Standing, blunt.** The headline contagion estimate has an interval whose lower bound is 1%, so the point estimate is imprecise. The identification rests on exactly the assumptions Shalizi and Thomas show are not testable from data: lagging the outcome and the alter's value controls for latent homophily only if the model for the social process is right. The direction-of-tie asymmetry test is the specific device Shalizi and Thomas attack in their section 2.2 (below). The pattern that coworkers show no effect while neighbours show a large one is as compatible with shared neighbourhood exposure as with transmission. Lyons (2011) argues the conclusions do not follow from the statistics and that the studies "even provide some evidence against the existence of such transmission" [read: abstract; arXiv 1007.2876; DOI 10.2202/2151-7509.1024 confirmed]. I have not checked Lyons's arguments in the body, nor Christakis and Fowler's rebuttals, so I cannot adjudicate that dispute beyond the structural point below.

**Operand/operator translation.** As evidence for a gain: unidentified. As a design: useful structure (lagged state, distance and tie-type moderators), since the algebra's operator should carry a decay in time and in network distance.

## 5. Shalizi and Thomas (2011): the homophily critique, stated precisely

**Citation.** Shalizi, C. R., Thomas, A. C. (2011). Homophily and contagion are generically confounded in observational social network studies. *Sociological Methods & Research* 40(2), 211-239. DOI 10.1177/0049124111404820 (Crossref confirmed; the SAGE page was not accessed; read the arXiv version 1004.4704).

**Claims [read].** "latent homophily and contagion are generically confounded with each other (section 2), and any direct contagion effects cannot be nonparametrically identified from observational data. To identify contagion effects, we need either strong parametric assumptions or strong substantive knowledge that lets us rule out latent homophily as a causal factor." Corollary (section 2.2): the proposal that asymmetries in regression coefficients which match asymmetries in network ties establish direct contagion "also fails". Simple imitation models can "produce substantial correlations between an individual's enduring traits and their choices". Section 4 constructive responses include bounding the asymmetry attainable without influence, so a large enough observed asymmetry would count as a severe test of influence (Mayo's notion of severity); the paper gives this only as a proposal and states no bound.

**What this means for D219.** (a) Any gain for the algebra's transmission operator taken from Framingham-style or Twitter-exposure-style data is an upper bound at best. (b) The estimates that survive are the three with randomisation or an instrument. (c) The paper does not say that contagion is absent; it says the data cannot separate it from sorting. It does not show Christakis and Fowler's numbers are wrong, only that the method cannot identify them.

## 6. Observational Twitter and platform engagement studies

**Ferrara and Yang (2015).** Measuring emotional contagion in social media. *PLoS ONE* 10(11), e0142390. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0142390 (Crossref). [summary-fetch.] About 3,800 random English-tweeting users (last week of September 2014) plus their followees; stimulus is followees' tweets in the preceding hour (at least 20 required); sentiment from SentiStrength (positive and negative scores 1 to 5; polarity = difference, range -4 to 4). A null model reshuffles stimuli (baseline 34.44% positive, 48.27% neutral, 17.29% negative). Result: a negative post follows over-exposure to 4.34% more negative content than baseline, a positive one 4.50% more positive (Mann-Whitney p < 10^-6); response valence vs stimulus valence in 20 bins is linear with R^2 = 0.975 [summary-fetch; I did not see slope or intercept]. Susceptibility: low-susceptibility users 1.6 times more likely to adopt positive than negative, high-susceptibility users 3.96 times. The authors say "it is not possible to separate contagion from homophily" and call contagion "plausible yet not exclusive". Standing: observational, so by section 5 it cannot identify a gain; its value is the shape (linear response, positivity asymmetry, individual susceptibility). Numbers are percent over-exposure relative to a null, not a transmission coefficient.

**Brady et al. (2017).** Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks. *PNAS* 114(28), 7313-7318. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1618923114. [read: abstract.] 563,312 messages on three issues; each additional moral-emotional word raised diffusion by 20%; stronger within ideological groups than between. This is a diffusion (retweet) effect of message content, not contagion of a receiver's state.

**Burton, Cruz and Hahn (2021).** Reconsidering evidence of moral contagion in online social networks. *Nature Human Behaviour* 5, 1629-1635. DOI 10.1038/s41562-021-01133-5. [read: abstract.] Out-of-sample prediction, model comparison and specification-curve analyses: "the moral contagion model performs no better than an implausible XYZ contagion model". They generalise: purely correlational large-sample evidence with standard significance testing is unsafe. Body unread; the corpora and the definition of the XYZ model are unconfirmed (file 27 had the same gap). Effect on D219: Brady's 20% is not a validated effect; Burton is a placebo-dictionary test of the dictionary approach itself, so it also bears on the LIWC-based literature in general, not only Brady.

**Rathje, Van Bavel and van der Linden (2021).** Out-group animosity drives engagement on social media. *PNAS* 118(26), e2024292118. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2024292118. [read: abstract.] 2,730,215 posts; posts about the political out-group shared about twice as often; each out-group term raised odds of a share by 67%; out-group language about 4.8 times as strong a predictor as negative affect language and 6.7 times as moral-emotional language; out-group language predicts "angry" reactions. Again engagement with content, not receiver state. Interesting for the algebra because it suggests the group-membership tag on a message matters more than its valence, an operand-level distinction (in-group vs out-group source) absent from Kramer and Coviello.

**Brady et al. (2023) and Goldenberg and Willer (2023).** Brady et al.: Overperception of moral outrage in online social networks inflates beliefs about intergroup hostility. *Nature Human Behaviour* 7(6), 917-927. DOI 10.1038/s41562-023-01582-0. [read: abstract.] Observers overperceive authors' moral outrage; preregistered experiments show this inflates beliefs about group hostility. Goldenberg and Willer, Amplification of emotion on social media, *Nature Human Behaviour* 7, 845-846, DOI 10.1038/s41562-023-01604-x (read per file 18). Operand implication: the receiver reads a biased estimate of the sender's state, so the operator acts on a perceived state, not the true state (consistent with 18).

## 7. Muchnik, Aral and Taylor (2013) and Lorenz et al. (2011): randomised influence on judgements, not emotion

**Muchnik, Aral, Taylor (2013).** Social influence bias: a randomized experiment. *Science* 341(6146), 647-651. DOI 10.1126/science.1240466. [read: abstract.] Randomised prior ratings on a social news aggregator: positive prior rating raised the likelihood of a positive rating by 32% and increased final ratings by 25% on average; negative prior ratings inspired correction (asymmetric herding). This is a randomised herding effect on an evaluation, with an asymmetry, and the closest identified analogue in this set of a conformity operator.

**Lorenz et al. (2011).** How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect. *PNAS* 108(22), 9020-9025. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1008636108. [read: abstract.] N = 144; five consecutive estimation periods; conditions with no information, average information or full information about others' answers. Three effects: social influence (diversity falls without gain in collective accuracy), range reduction (truth moves to the edge of the range of estimates), confidence (confidence rises after convergence without accuracy gain). An identified, small-N lab result on convergence. The numeric effect sizes were not read.

**Relevance.** Neither measures emotion. They calibrate the conformity operator (a different drive from emotional mimicry) and show that identified influence effects on judgement are sizable in a controlled setting while emotion-expression effects on a feed are tiny. That asymmetry itself is a finding: the weak link in the emotion literature may be exposure dosage (a feed shows a friend's post among thousands), not the existence of the mechanism.

## 8. Lab and mechanism evidence

**Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson (1993).** Emotional contagion. *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 2(3), 96-100. DOI 10.1111/1467-8721.ep10770953 (Crossref; one search result lists pages 96-99, the DOI record says 96-100). Book: *Emotional Contagion*, Cambridge University Press (1994; Crossref dates the edition 1993), DOI 10.1017/CBO9781139174138 per file 18. **Not read.** From the 2014 review (Hatfield, Bensman, Thornton and Rapson, *Interpersona* 8(2), 159-179, DOI 10.5964/ijpr.v8i2.162, Crossref confirmed) as quoted by a search snippet [secondary]: primitive emotional contagion is "the tendency to automatically mimic and synchronize expressions, vocalizations, postures, and movements" with those of another, and the process occurs in "three stages: Mimicry -> Feedback -> Contagion". Their framework has no equations. A criticism in the snippet, that it neglects conscious mechanisms (social comparison, impression management), is from a secondary source I did not identify.

**Stage 2 (feedback): facial feedback.** If contagion runs through mimicry and facial feedback, its lab foundation is the facial feedback hypothesis. Wagenmakers et al. (2016), Registered Replication Report: Strack, Martin and Stepper (1988). *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(6), 917-928, DOI 10.1177/1745691616674458 [read: abstract]: 17 direct replications, difference in funniness ratings 0.03 units (95% CI -0.11 to 0.16), against 0.82 in the original. Coles, Larsen and Lench (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(7), 610-651, DOI 10.1037/bul0000194: a meta-analysis of 286 effect sizes from 138 studies reporting a "significant but small" facial-feedback effect, with the d = 0.20 figure appearing in the earlier thesis version (136 studies) rather than the paper abstract as I could see it [secondary: PubMed excerpt via search; I did not read the paper, so the 2019 d is unconfirmed]. Coles et al. The state of the evidence is therefore: the original pen-in-mouth result did not replicate, the broader effect is small and heterogeneous (about 70% heterogeneity in the thesis version), and a large multi-lab test found some effect of unconfirmed size. This is the support for Hatfield's stage 2, and it is weak.

**Barsade (2002).** The ripple effect: emotional contagion and its influence on group behavior. *Administrative Science Quarterly* 47(4), 644-675. DOI 10.2307/3094912 (Crossref). Per file 27: a lab experiment with a trained confederate in four mood conditions; groups with positive contagion reported better cooperation. Not re-read this session. Single study, confederate-driven, so the sender is an actor; replication status unknown to me.

**Olsson and Phelps (2004).** Learned fear of "unseen" faces after Pavlovian, observational, and instructed fear. *Psychological Science* 15(12), 822-828. DOI 10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00762.x (Crossref confirmed). [secondary: a press summary and a later paper's description.] Compared fear acquired by direct conditioning, by observing another's reaction, and by instruction; later papers summarise similar skin-conductance responses across the three groups. ## 9. Reconciliation: what number should the algebra carry?

A single "strength of emotional contagion" does not exist, because the sources measure different things in different units. A table of what each estimates:

| Source | Design | Measures | Unit | Magnitude | Identification |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kramer 2014 | randomised, platform, 1 wk | own expressed word share after exposure cut | pp of word share; d | -0.10 / -0.07 pp; d 0.02 (same valence), 0.008 and (printed) 0.001 (cross) | strong |
| Coviello 2014 | rainfall IV, platform | posts among friends per post | ratio | 1.29 to 1.75 own-valence; ~1.5:1 indirect:direct | instrument (exclusion assumptions) |
| Fowler & Christakis 2008 | observational panel | probability of being happy | relative risk | 8% to 34% by tie type; CI lower bounds 0.2% to 7% | confounded (Shalizi-Thomas) |
| Ferrara & Yang 2015 | observational, Twitter | over-exposure before a post | % vs null | +4.34% / +4.50% | confounded |
| Brady 2017; Rathje 2021 | observational | diffusion of messages | % odds | +20% per moral-emotional word; +67% per out-group term | confounded; Burton 2021 placebo test |
| Muchnik 2013 | randomised, platform | rating herding | % | +32% likelihood, +25% final | strong |
| Lorenz 2011 | randomised lab | convergence of judgement | N=144 | not read | strong |
| Lab facial feedback | RRR and meta-analysis | self-reported emotion | mean difference; d | 0.03 units (RRR); small (meta) | strong, no replication of the original |

**Judgement (mine).** (1) The defensible statement for text-based expression is: contagion of expressed affect through a feed is real in sign and tiny in magnitude per person-week (d on the order of 0.01 to 0.02), and it is amplified only by the size of the audience. (2) The large observational estimates are upper bounds contaminated by sorting and by common shocks; Burton's null-model result shows that a correlational "contagion" effect can appear in a placebo dictionary. (3) No source here tests contagion of a drive (curiosity, fear, desire); the one nearest, Olsson and Phelps for fear, is secondary-only. (4) The right calibration object for the algebra is therefore not a gain but a prior: gain per unit of exposure is small, positive, and probably channel-dependent (positive transmits more than negative on Coviello's estimate, with the usual "bad is stronger" intuition unsupported in Kramer's post hoc symmetry test).

**Suggested form [mine, not from a source].** For the algebra's operator C acting on a receiver j's expressed state e_j from sender set S with exposure weights w_ij: de_j = gamma * sum_i w_ij (e_i - e_j) with gamma per unit exposure small enough that the system's spectral radius is below one for realistic feed sizes. Coviello's ratio above 1 per post is then a sum over large audiences, not a per-edge gain. Calibrate gamma only from randomised or instrumented designs and treat the others as upper-bound priors.

## What exists vs what does not

**Exists.** A randomised, platform-scale experiment with a clearly small expression-level effect (Kramer). A rainfall-instrumented natural experiment with a signed own/cross-effect structure (Coviello). A formal result that observational network data generically cannot separate contagion from homophily (Shalizi and Thomas). An empirical demonstration that a correlational contagion measure can match a placebo (Burton). Randomised herding effects on ratings and estimates (Muchnik, Lorenz). A failed direct replication and a small heterogeneous meta-analytic effect for the lab mechanism (facial feedback).

**Does not exist (in what I retrieved).** An independent replication of Kramer et al. or of Coviello et al. A per-exposure transmission coefficient for emotion in units that compare across studies. Any experimental or instrumented estimate of contagion of a drive (fear, curiosity, desire) or of a persisting mood, as opposed to expressed word share. A randomised test of Hatfield's three stages end to end. A validated measurement-error correction for LIWC on short posts applied to these estimates. A group-level (not dyadic) contagion estimate.
