## 2. Smith, Seger and Mackie 2007: what was actually done [read]

**Their vocabulary.** "Group-level emotions" (their term), "intergroup emotions theory" (IET), "group identification", "group average (emotion) profile", "convergence". They treat IET's premise as: when an individual identifies with a group, the group "becomes part of the self" and events impinging on the group are appraised "just like events that occur in an individual's personal life". Quoting their gloss of Smith 1993: "when group membership is salient, the group functions as a part of the self, and therefore ... situations appraised as self-relevant trigger emotions just as they always do" (p. 432, quoting Smith 1993, p. 303).

**Operationalisation, which matters for everything below.** They did not measure reactions to events. They asked each participant how much they *generally* feel each of a list of emotions (12 in Study 1, 13 in Study 2: angry, disgusted, irritated, afraid, uneasy, guilty, satisfied, hopeful, proud, happy, grateful, respectful; Study 2 split anger into anger at ingroup and at outgroup), once "as an individual" and once "as a member of" a named group (Indiana University, Democrats, Republicans in Study 1; Americans, Democrats, Republicans in Study 2). Ratings 1-7 [read]. Group identification was a 4-item scale. Samples: Study 1 N=110 introductory psychology students (analysed N about 94-101 for IU, 40 Democrats, 42 Republicans); Study 2 N=445 students (Americans 349-405, Democrats 160-229). So every claim below is about **chronic, self-reported, trait-like emotion profiles of US university students, for a few categories**, not about transient emotion states in interacting groups.

**The four criteria, as stated.** (1) distinct from the same person's individual-level emotions; (2) depend on level of group identification; (3) socially shared within a group; (4) contribute to motivating and regulating intragroup and intergroup attitudes and behaviour. They explicitly say the criteria need not each hold universally: "Our claim is not that each criterion is individually critical ... but simply that evidence supporting all four would lend strong impetus to that conclusion" (p. 432).

### 2.1 Criterion 1, distinctness [read]
- Average correlation between corresponding individual and group emotions: Study 1 .52 (IU), .25 (Democrats), .33 (Republicans), "accounting for 6%-25% of the variance in group emotions"; Study 2 .24-.33 (p. 439).
- Between-subjects 2 (level) x 12 (emotion) ANOVA in Study 1, interaction (the emotion *profile* differs by level): F(11,82)=2.35* (IU), F(11,26)=2.31* (Democrats), F(11,30)=2.64* (Republicans). Study 2 (within-subjects): Level x Emotions F(12,4368)=12.35***, F(12,2316)=20.78***, F(12,1824)=13.41*** for the three groups. Group profiles also differ from each other (Emotions x Group, e.g. F(12,4334)=22.25, p<.001).
- Footnote 1: they report the between-subjects analysis in Study 1 because a within-subjects comparison "might exaggerate differences" through conversational postulates (people inferring that the second question set wants different answers). Study 2 is within-subjects with individual items first, so the demand-characteristic worry is not removed there.
- **What "distinct" means here:** the mean *profile across emotions* differs between the individual and group framing, and the two correlate .24-.52. It does not mean group emotion is not reducible to individual emotion plus a category cue.

### 2.2 Criterion 2, identification [read]
Identification correlates with positive group emotions and negative with negative ones in some groups; the authors themselves say direction is ambiguous for negative emotions (identifiers may stay despite guilt; may reappraise to avoid it; may disidentify). They conclude Study 2 "was unable to discriminate" between negative emotion causing disidentification and identification causing reappraisal. So criterion 2 is a correlation of unspecified direction.

### 2.3 Criterion 3, convergence: the equation [read]
Multilevel model (footnote 3, after Singer 1998), with emotion i, participant j:

    Level 1:  grpem_ij = B0j + B1j * indem_ij + B2j * grpavg_i + r_ij
    Level 2:  B0j = gamma00 + mu0j
              B1j = gamma10 + gamma11 * ident_j + mu1j
              B2j = gamma20 + gamma21 * ident_j + mu2j

where grpem_ij is j's reported group emotion i, indem_ij is j's individual emotion i, grpavg_i is "the average level of group emotion i across participants who are members of that group", ident_j is j's identification. "Convergence" = gamma20 > 0 after controlling indem; "identification moderates convergence" = gamma21 > 0.

Results [read; column assignment for Study 1 inferred from the extracted order and the authors' narrative "the effects of the average group emotion profiles were in every case much larger than the effects of individual emotions"]:

| | indiv. emotion | group-average profile | ident x indiv. | ident x group-avg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Study 1 IU (N=101) | .45*** | .55*** | .04 | .07 |
| Study 1 Democrats (N=40) | .07 | .71*** | .12* | .14 (p<.10) |
| Study 1 Republicans (N=42) | .20*** | .67*** | .00 | .33*** |
| Study 2 Americans (N=349) | .23*** | .76*** | .03* | .22*** |
| Study 2 Democrats (N=220) | .18*** | .81*** | .01 | .23*** |
| Study 2 Republicans (N=160) | .14*** | .89*** | -.05*** | .11*** |

(Study 2 table transcribed directly; Study 1 as above.) Identification moderates convergence in all three groups in Study 2, in one of three in Study 1 (one more marginal). The interaction of identification with *individual* emotion goes in opposite directions across groups, which the authors call "extremely weak and conditional".

**Three problems with reading this as dynamics, none fatal but all load-bearing:**
1. **The "group average" is the mean of the same respondents' own reports (including the person's own).** The authors address it: excluding self changes "a negligible difference" at N about 100-400, and a split-sample check (random third for the mean, other two thirds for the regression) gave "virtually identical" results (footnote 6). Seger et al. 2009 add that the expected correlation between the all-N and (N-1) mean vectors is above .99 (their footnote 1). So circularity of the self-inclusion kind is dealt with.
2. **A deeper problem is not dealt with: the regression predicts the *shape* of one person's 12-13 emotion ratings from the shape of the sample mean.** Anything that makes people's profiles for a category similar produces a large gamma20 (a common stereotype of what "Americans feel", common salient events, a common response set). The authors list three candidate mechanisms and say the studies "were not designed to distinguish among" them: emotional contagion, conformity to group emotion norms, and shared appraisals of the same salient events (p. 443). So convergence is **shared profile**, not **influence**. There is no time axis, no interaction, and no test of one member's emotion changing another's.
3. The coefficients (.55-.89) are partly produced by the generic fact that people agree on which emotions are high or low for any target (the mean profile also describes the item difficulty of the scale). The model has no baseline of "convergence expected for a *non*-group target" in Study 1/2 (they do have Emotions x Group interactions showing different groups have different profiles, which is a partial control).

### 2.4 Criterion 4, regulation [read]
Regressions of action tendencies (support for ingroup, confront/avoid the outgroup) on group-level and individual-level emotion composites (anger, anxiety, guilt, positive), for IU (Study 1) and for Americans, Democrats and Republicans (Study 2). Headline tally the authors give: "24 of the 48 effects of group emotions on behavioral intentions were significant (50%), compared with 8 of the 48 tests of individual emotions (17%)", and individual-emotion effects "never replicated from one group to another" while group anger and positive group emotions did. Example coefficients from the Americans table: U.S. anger -> confront 0.49***, U.S. positive -> confront 0.58***, individual anger -> confront 0.32** (unstandardised).

Caveats [read from the paper's own text]: action-*tendency* items (willingness to tell a critic he is wrong, sign an anti-immigration petition), not behaviour; same-instrument, same-session self-report, so common-method variance; several outcome scales had low reliability (.41-.57 for some avoidance scales, which the authors acknowledge in footnote 8); a programming error lost part of the Study 2 outcome items (footnote 7). Direction of cause is not tested. The author-acknowledged oddity: group anger at the outgroup *increased* outgroup avoidance, explained post hoc as self-regulation.

### 2.5 Seger, Smith and Mackie 2009 [read]
Two experiments (Exp. 2 N=82, between-subjects) in which the group category was primed (music clips: Star-Spangled Banner for US, IU songs; photographs) and emotions were then measured without any group framing. Same multilevel model: primed emotion regressed on individual emotion and on the group-primed mean (Hypothesis 1), and on the participant's own earlier *explicit* group-emotion report (Hypothesis 2), each with identification moderators. Results: convergence significant; group-primed emotion predicted by explicit group emotion "at roughly the same level as individual emotions"; identification strengthened convergence in 3 of 4 cases, and "for those who identify more strongly with a group, their group-primed emotions show less overlap with their individual emotions" (negative identification x individual-emotion interaction). The group-ID effect on convergence was not significant for the IU prime in Exp. 1. This is the closest thing to a manipulation, but the manipulation is of **salience of category**, not of any group state, and the dependent variable is again a profile.

## 3. Challenge: what the "group emotion" measure measures [read]

Kuppens and Yzerbyt (2014) argue that the Smith et al. measure ("as a member of X, how much do you feel ...", no event specified) does **not** capture emotions about group concerns. Four studies (two with UCL/Cardiff-type student samples; Study 3 N=120 Belgian participants):
- General group emotions correlate **r = .70 and .77** (Studies 1 and 2) with emotions measured explicitly as *belonging* emotions ("As a UCL student, to what extent do you feel ... about being a UCL student").
- General group emotions are **not** related to appraisals of immediate group concerns, whereas group-based emotions (reactions to a specified group event) are (Studies 3 and 4).
- Negative general group emotions correlate *negatively* with identification (reproducing Smith et al.'s finding), whereas group-based negative emotions (anger at an outgroup threat) correlate positively with identification (as in Mackie et al. 2000). Simple effects in one comparison are order-dependent (B = -.47 when measured first; B = +.53 after group-based emotions), with the authors noting these were not individually significant.
- Their conclusion: general group emotions "are emotions about group belonging ... primarily or even exclusively a reaction to individual concerns, that is, the nature of the individual's belonging to the group." Footnote 2 says they dropped the mean-profile predictor from their model, citing Kuppens and Yzerbyt (in press).

**Consequence.** If Kuppens and Yzerbyt are right, the Smith et al. result is a result about *how people feel about being in the group* (a self-evaluative quantity), and its convergence is a convergence of those evaluations. It leaves open whether *event-driven group-based emotions* (collective anger at an attack, which Mackie et al. 2000 studied) converge in the same way. The two traditions have been merged under one name ("group-based emotion"; Seger 2009 and later Smith-lab papers use that label for the general measure, per Kuppens and Yzerbyt).

## 4. Mackie, Devos and Smith 2000 and Smith 1993 [abstract only]

Mackie et al. 2000 [abstract]: three studies; when group identity is salient, group-based appraisals elicit specific emotions and action tendencies toward outgroups. Anger and fear (Studies 1, 2) and anger and contempt (Study 3) were measured. Intergroup anger was distinct from intergroup fear; the tendency to act against the outgroup was distinct from moving away from it. Participants who perceived the ingroup as *strong* were more likely to feel anger toward the outgroup and to desire action against it, and **anger mediated** the effect of perceived ingroup strength on offensive action tendency. Smith 1993 [standard account, quoted via Smith et al. 2007]: the founding statement of IET (group becomes part of self, so self-relevant appraisals trigger emotion at the group level).

**Operand/operator form in IET (my reading).** Appraisal of an event against the *group's* concerns (identity salience as a switch) -> discrete emotion -> action tendency (anger -> approach/attack; fear -> avoid). This is a labelled map, not an equation: no magnitudes, no dynamics, no decay. The only quantitative object in the whole line is the regression above.

## 5. Does group emotion *regulate*? Goldenberg et al. 2016 and 2020; Pizarro et al. 2022

**Goldenberg et al. 2016 [see file 18].** Combines IET with Gross's process model of regulation: group-based emotion can be regulated at situation selection, situation modification, attention, appraisal and response stages. Criterion 4 in the Smith sense (emotion regulates behaviour) is distinct from this (people regulate their group emotion). Verified DOI above; not re-read here.

**Goldenberg et al. 2020, *Collective emotions* [abstract]:** defines collective emotions as emergent macro-level affective phenomena, reviews collective psychological processes, and maps emergent properties along three dimensions: **quality, magnitude, time course**. It poses open questions and does not (from the abstract) give equations. The three dimensions are directly usable as a state-vector axis: quality = which emotion; magnitude = level; time course = trajectory.

**Pizarro et al. 2022 [read].** A different construct from the Smith line: *collective effervescence* (CE), Durkheim's intense shared emotional activation and unison during gatherings, with Collins's multi-faceted synchrony account. Random-effects meta-analysis of studies of gatherings (k about 54; types: community celebrations 10, demonstrations 15, religious events 10, sports 4, music festivals 3, others 12; 28 of the CE measures are the Intensity of Positive Emotional Experience scale, 6 "intense positive affect", the rest scales of mutual entrainment). Pooled correlations with CE [read, Table 3]:

| Criterion | k | N | r (95% CI) | I2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Individual arousal | 14 | 48,316 | .443 (.322, .564) | 95.4 |
| Individual negative emotions | 14 | 2,028 | .047 (-.053, .147) | 78.2 |
| Individual positive emotions | 22 | 5,834 | .547 (.468, .625) | 94.3 |
| Self-transcendent emotions | 17 | 5,340 | .577 (.500, .653) | 92.9 |
| Ingroup identification | 14 | 3,253 | .456 (.351, .562) | 92.7 |
| Fusion of identity (verbal) | 5 | 1,031 | .694 (.660, .729) | 6.9 |

Demonstrations only: CE with group identity r=.52, collective efficacy .37, negative emotions .14, self-transcendent emotions .58, morality-related beliefs .43. Publication-bias tests (rank correlation p=.914; regression p=.702; fail-safe N 22,003) were non-significant for the first outcome.

**Blunt reading.** The meta-analytic evidence is for CE as a *felt, self-reported, positive, self-transcendent* state that co-occurs with identification and efficacy. Heterogeneity is extreme (I2 mostly 90%+). Nearly all are cross-sectional self-reports collected at or after the event, and the same participant supplies CE and the criterion, so correlations are inflated by method variance and by retrospective sense-making. "Collective emotions" in this paper means perceived *emotional climate* ratings, a separate construct again. It is also notable that the one *negative* collective outcome (climate) is at zero: CE is not evidence about group fear or anger. Per the brief's cross-check angle: this supports file 37's and file 18's moderate standing for "perceived emotional synchrony at gatherings", but gives **no direct test of the Durkheim feedback loop** (ritual -> effervescence -> solidarity -> more ritual); the longitudinal moderator is described but I did not extract its result, so I do not claim the loop is tested.

## 6. Definitional frame: what counts as "collective" [read: von Scheve 2017; Livingstone 2011 abstract]

von Scheve 2017 (summary of the 2014 volume's field) lists at least four distinct senses of "collective emotion", and Smith-style group-level emotion is only the first:
1. **Group-membership emotions**: felt because of membership/affiliation; social identity and categorisation integral; can be felt by a single person (collective guilt as example).
2. **Joint-intention / plural-subject emotions**: collective or "we-intentions", normative commitment to feel in a certain way (Gilbert 2002; Salmela 2012). Non-aggregate, mental-state-centred.
3. **Aggregate/synchrony emotions**: several individuals feeling within a time-frame, usually toward the same event, with physical co-presence, mimicry, contagion and behavioural synchrony (Hatfield, Carpenter & Rapson 2014).
4. **Structure/culture-produced similarity**: shared ecology, stratification, shared values, feeling rules and display norms, "emotional climates" (Bar-Tal, Halperin & de Rivera 2007).
His closing assessment: "a substantial gap between the multifaceted theoretical concepts and the available measures of collective emotions", and theory remains "confined to disciplinary boundaries". Terminology overlaps heavily ("group-based emotions, intergroup emotions, or group affect").

Livingstone et al. 2011 [abstract]: the match between a participant's emotion and what the ingroup appeared to feel affected self-categorisation, and willingness to act was higher when the shared emotion was anger. This is the only item here that runs **emotion -> group membership** (the reverse of the IET arrow), in minimal and natural groups. A 2015 follow-up ("Fury, us", *Cogn. Emotion* 30, 183-192, DOI above) tested whether shared anger creates a *new* category; its abstract-level framing is that the 2011 paper had been the only prior test. Not read.

## 7. Answer to the question

| Smith et al. criterion | Supported by | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Distinct from individual emotion | Profile differs by level (Level x Emotion interactions, 3 groups, 2 studies); individual-group r .24-.52 | **Supported for a narrow construct.** The construct is a chronic belonging-emotion profile (Kuppens and Yzerbyt 2014, r about .7-.77 with belonging emotions), not event-driven group emotion. |
| 2 Depends on identification | Correlations and moderation (Study 2 all 3 groups) | **Supported as an association;** direction unknown, especially for negative emotion. |
| 3 Shared / convergent | gamma20 .55-.89 after controlling individual emotion; replicated with priming (Seger 2009); mean computed from independent third (footnote 6) | **Shared profile is robust; convergence as a *process* is untested.** Contagion, conformity and common appraisal are not separated (authors' own statement). |
| 4 Regulates | Group emotion predicts action tendencies better than individual emotion (24/48 vs 8/48 effects) | **Weak-moderate.** Intentions, self-report, same session, low-reliability outcomes, no causal test. |

**Is it a distinct, convergent and regulating variable?** For a state variable in an algebra: it is **distinct and convergent as a descriptive statistic** (a stable group-specific profile with within-group agreement), **not shown to be a dynamical state with its own update law**. Nobody here has measured emotion as a quantity that moves over time and whose movement is explained by other members. The distinction between a group's *mean/profile* and an emergent variable that *acts back on members* is exactly what criteria 3 and 4 fail to separate in the data.

## 8. Operand/operator translation for the algebra

**Operands.**
- A group G carries a *profile vector* e(G) in R^k over k emotion categories (their 12-13 items), plus a membership-specific identification weight w_j in [0,1] for each member j (the 4-item scale, rescaled).
- A person j carries an individual vector a_j and a group-framed vector g_j(G). Nothing in the evidence says the person-level object is not simply a_j plus a category-cued shift.

**Operators suggested by what was actually estimated (not by the theory's prose).**
- *Framing/priming operator* F_G: a_j -> g_j. Estimated empirically as g_j ≈ B1 * a_j + B2 * ē(G) with B2 about .5-.9 and B1 about .1-.5 [read, Tables 3, 7]. That is a **convex-like blend toward the group mean** whose weight on the mean rises with identification (gamma21 about +.1 to +.3; but negative for Republicans in Study 2 on the individual term). So F_G is an interpolation toward a prototype whose pull grows with w_j. Equivalent in form to a DeGroot/Friedkin-Johnsen step with the sample mean as the anchor, but **fitted to cross-sectional profiles, not to time series**.
- *Appraisal-to-action map* A: emotion -> action tendency (anger -> confront/attack; fear/anxiety -> avoid), with group emotion having more cross-group-stable coefficients than individual emotion. This is the criterion-4 content.
- *Salience switch*: identity salience s in {0,1} (or [0,1]) selects whether group appraisals count. IET asserts this as a gate; Seger 2009 manipulates it (priming).
- *Regulation operator* R_stage (Goldenberg 2016): stage-indexed intervention on group emotion. No equations.

**Mismatches to flag, tied to prior files.**
- The unit problem: ē(G) is a mean over members of a *chronic self-report*, so "group state = mean of member states" is assumed, not tested; Pizarro's CE and the "climate" measure are different aggregations again (the group's *perceived* emotion). Cross-reference file 18's open question on separating actual from perceived group state (this feeds D242).
- Time: no source here gives a decay rate, a convergence speed or a half-life for group emotion. Do not parameterise one from these papers.
- Individual-level prediction: B1+B2 is a within-sample fit; there is no out-of-sample prediction of any person's behaviour from ē(G) (cf. the file 07 caution).

## 9. What exists vs what does not

**Exists.**
- A replicated finding (Smith et al. 2007 two studies; Seger et al. 2009 two experiments; Kuppens and Yzerbyt 2014 reproduces the negative identification relation) that people report a *group-specific, identification-dependent emotion profile* that differs from their individual profile and is similar across members.
- A published multilevel specification (footnote 3) usable as a measurement model: grpem = B0 + B1*indem + B2*grpavg with identification moderators.
- Evidence that group-framed emotion predicts action *intentions* better than individual emotion.
- A large heterogeneous meta-analysis of CE correlates (Pizarro 2022).
- A conceptual map of four senses of "collective emotion" (von Scheve 2017).

**Does not exist (in what I found).**
- Any dynamical law for group emotion: no update equation, decay, or stability result.
- A test that separates contagion, conformity and common appraisal as causes of convergence (the authors say so).
- A manipulation of the *group's* emotional state with downstream tests of individual behaviour; the only manipulations are category salience (priming) and, in Livingstone 2011, apparent ingroup emotion.
- Evidence that the Smith measure is about event-driven group concerns (Kuppens and Yzerbyt say it is not).
- Behavioural, rather than self-report intention, outcomes for criterion 4.
- A formal bridge from this psychology-of-emotion line to the dynamical-systems line (Barsade/Collins style contagion, Kuramoto-type models); no paper here makes it.
- Out-of-WEIRD-student samples: all samples read are US or Belgian/UK university students.
