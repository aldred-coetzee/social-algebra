# Prior work 79: identifiability and power rules for recovering drive equations from sparse, noisy EMA and event data

Direction: D493

Date: 2026-10-29. Kind: model. Question: given that the algebra's drive equations (restoring rates, couplings, gates) must be fitted to sparse, noisy, irregularly timed data, what can be recovered at all, from how much data, and at what sampling interval? Seeds: Brunton, Proctor & Kutz 2016; Boker; Ryan, Kuiper & Hamaker 2018. Context: file 27 (what is measurable), 59/60/70 (coupling matrices).

**Method and honesty note.** Read at source this session (PDF downloaded and text-extracted, so not via a summarising model): Brunton, Proctor & Kutz (arXiv v1 of the PNAS paper: Sections 3, 3.1, 3.2, 5 and the Lorenz noise example); Fasel, Kutz, Brunton & Brunton 2021 arXiv v1 of Ensemble-SINDy (abstract, algorithm, Lotka-Volterra and Lorenz sections); Ryan, Kuiper & Hamaker, the author pre-print of the 2018 chapter (Eqs. 1-4, the CT-versus-DT section, the matrix-logarithm caveat, the multilevel section); Lafit et al. 2021 (arXiv-style pre-print, power-by-simulation method and one worked power curve). **Not read:** Boker's papers (only the Crossref record: no abstract was returned), Schultzberg & Muthen 2018, Voelkle et al. 2012, Hamaker et al. 2015, Dormann & Griffin 2015. Those are marked [citation only]. WebSearch was unavailable (budget exhausted), so no replication hunt beyond the papers' own text. Section 5 contains **my own derivations and a small simulation**; those are tagged [derived] or [my simulation] and are not literature. DOIs were checked against Crossref (title and authors matched) unless noted.

**Summary.** (1) The two seed methods answer different questions. SINDy-style sparse regression asks *which terms* are in the law, and its evidence is that it works when data are dense, nearly clean and the library contains the truth; its own authors give no data-size or identifiability condition, only a sample-count inequality (m much larger than p) and a noise example. The continuous-time (CT) SEM / Ornstein-Uhlenbeck line asks *how large* the coefficients of a fixed linear law are, and it states honestly why lag-dependent coefficients are not comparable across sampling intervals. (2) For an algebra whose drive laws are mostly "relax towards a set point, coupled to others", the identifiability limit is set by the **ratio of sampling interval to relaxation time** and by **total observed time in relaxation times**, not by the number of prompts. [derived, Section 5]. (3) The records that the algebra needs (strong nonlinear gates, thresholds) are the ones for which no power rule exists in this literature at all.

## 1. Brunton, Proctor & Kutz (2016): SINDy

**Citation.** Brunton, S. L., Proctor, J. L., & Kutz, J. N. (2016). Discovering governing equations from data by sparse identification of nonlinear dynamical systems. *PNAS* 113(15), 3932-3937. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1517384113 (Crossref confirmed). Read: arXiv:1509.03580v1.

**Their vocabulary.** Data matrix X (rows are time samples of the state), derivative matrix Xdot, library Theta(X) of candidate nonlinear functions (columns), coefficient matrix Xi, sparsity knob lambda, Pareto front (accuracy against number of terms).

**Drives/operands.** None; physical state variables. The authors name neuroscience, epidemiology and finance as intended fields; no psychology.

**Operators / equations [read].**
- Model class: xdot = f(x), written row-wise xdot_k = f_k(x).
- Regression: **Xdot = Theta(X) Xi** (their Eq. 7), one sparse column xi_k per state equation. The only structural assumption is that "there are only a few important terms that govern the dynamics".
- Noise model (their Eq. 10): **Xdot = Theta(X) Xi + eta Z**, Z i.i.d. zero-mean Gaussian, eta the noise magnitude. Note this puts the noise on the *derivative* ("In the case that X is relatively clean but the derivatives Xdot are noisy").
- Algorithm, "sequential thresholded least squares": start from the least-squares Xi, zero every coefficient with |xi| < lambda, regress again onto the surviving columns, repeat until the support stops changing. LASSO is named as the alternative, "computationally expensive for very large data sets".
- Derivatives from noisy data: "total variation regularized derivative"; an alternative is optimal hard-threshold SVD filtering.
- Choice of lambda: held-out data plus the "elbow" of the accuracy-versus-complexity curve (Pareto front).
- Conditions stated: p candidates, m time samples, "m much greater than p" (Section 3.1). That is the whole data requirement. Compressed-sensing results are cited for the sparse-recovery guarantee in general, with the note that those need "incoherent" measurements; the paper does not check incoherence of Theta for any example.
- Noise robustness claim: "previous algorithms ... have been quite sensitive to noise. The algorithm in Code (1) is remarkably robust to noise". Evidence: the Lorenz system with Gaussian noise added to the derivative, eta = 0.01 and eta = 10 (figures of trajectories, plus a Fig. 6 of l2 error against time for eta in {0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0}, and a Table 4 of the coefficients identified at eta = 1.0; the text says "Although the l2 error increases for large noise values eta, the form of the equations ... are accurately captured". There is no table of recovery *rates* over replications). Note the text calls eta both the noise "magnitude" (Eq. 10) and the "variance" (Lorenz example).

**Theorems or derived predictions.** None proved. The Discussion lists open problems in the authors' words: dynamical symmetries and conserved quantities that "may alter the form of the identified dynamics"; a linear system fitted in a high-order polynomial library gives a "degenerate identification" (their 3-D linear example, Eq. 21, is recovered in polynomials to order 2 or 3, and the degeneracy appears at order 4 or 5); and "it will be important to identify which approximating function space to use based on the data available". That last sentence is the library-choice problem stated as unsolved.

**Empirical standing.** Demonstrated on simulated systems (linear and cubic oscillators, a 3-D linear system, Lorenz, a PDE-derived fluid-wake example, the logistic map and the Hopf normal form). **Not demonstrated in this paper on any behavioural or social data.** Ensemble-SINDy (Section 2) later shows the original is "sensitive to noise, especially in the low-data limit" (their abstract), which qualifies the 2016 robustness claim.

**Operand/operator translation.** SINDy's output is a sparse coefficient matrix over a user-chosen library; for the algebra the library would be a set of drive-law terms (restoring, coupling, gate, saturation). It identifies which operators are present; it presumes the state is observed directly, which for drives it is not (file 27: only affect proxies are observed).

## 2. Robust SINDy variants (what changes in the low-data, high-noise limit)

**Fasel, Kutz, Brunton & Brunton (2022).** Ensemble-SINDy. *Proc. R. Soc. A* 478, 20210904. DOI 10.1098/rspa.2021.0904 (Crossref confirmed). Read: arXiv:2111.10992v1.
- Method [read]: fit q = 100 SINDy models on bootstrap resamples of the data (bagging) or on random subsets of the library (library bagging, each sample uses l = 0.6 D of D terms); the fraction of models that include a term is its **inclusion probability**; terms below a threshold (tol = 0.6 for bagging, 0.4 for library bagging) are dropped. lambda = 0.2 in the Lorenz test.
- Noise protocol [read]: Gaussian white noise with variance proportional to the RMS of the trajectory, reported as a percentage (the forecast example uses 2.5%); each noise-by-data-length cell run on **1000 noise realisations**, scored by the rate of identifying the correct term structure and by coefficient error.
- Headline results [read]: ensembling "enabling model identification with less data and more noise than standard SINDy"; for PDEs it raises the tolerable noise of weak-SINDy by a factor of 2.3 (their text) and the abstract says "more than twice". A real-data test: the Hudson Bay lynx and hare pelt counts, 1900-1920, **21 points per species**, recovers Lotka-Volterra with coefficient and inclusion-probability uncertainty. Their own caveat: the data have "large uncertainty ... arising from weather variability, consistency in trapping".
- What it means here: it supplies the one published real-data case of a two-species coupled law recovered from about twenty noisy annual points, and the useful output for us is the **inclusion probability**, a calibrated "how sure that this coupling exists", not a point estimate. Success is still scored against a known truth in simulation; the pelt case has no ground truth other than the textbook Lotka-Volterra form.

**Not read, [citation only], real references.** Kaheman, Kutz & Brunton (2020), SINDy-PI (implicit/rational dynamics), *Proc. R. Soc. A* 476, 20200279, DOI 10.1098/rspa.2020.0279. Messenger & Bortz (2021), Weak SINDy, *Multiscale Model. Simul.* 19(3), 1474-1497, DOI 10.1137/20m1343166 (integral formulation avoids differentiating noisy data; Fasel et al. cite it as the noise-robustness baseline). Take both as pointers, not as findings.

## 3. Ryan, Kuiper & Hamaker (2018): the continuous-time view of intensive longitudinal data

**Citation.** Ryan, O., Kuiper, R. M., & Hamaker, E. L. (2018). A continuous-time approach to intensive longitudinal data: What, why, and how? In K. van Montfort, J. H. L. Oud & M. C. Voelkle (Eds.), *Continuous Time Modeling in the Behavioral and Related Sciences* (pp. 27-54). Springer. DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-77219-6_2 (Crossref confirmed). Read: the author pre-print (OSF ukdv3). Page numbers are the publisher's; I could not check them against the print.

**Their vocabulary.** Discrete-time (DT) VAR(1); continuous-time (CT) first-order stochastic differential equation; drift matrix A (auto-effects on the diagonal, cross-effects off it); diffusion matrix; "lag problem".

**Operators / equations [read].**
- DT model: y_tau = Phi y_{tau-1} + innovation (their Eq. 1).
- CT model, differential form (Eq. 2): **dy(t)/dt = A (y(t) − mu) + G dW(t)/dt**; innovation covariance G G' = Gamma "the diffusion matrix".
- Integral form, the CT-VAR(1) or Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model (Eq. 3): **y_c(t) = e^{A dt} y_c(t − dt) + w(dt)**, where w has covariance that is a function of dt, A and Gamma.
- Link (Eq. 4): **Phi(dt) = e^{A dt}**.
- Stated consequences: Phi depends on dt ("the lag problem"); with unequally spaced data a DT fit returns "a blend of different Phi(dt) matrices"; "the same lagged parameter may be negative for some time-intervals and positive for others, and zero-elements of A can result in corresponding non-zero elements of Phi"; the CT matrix A "is independent of time-interval (assuming a sufficient frequency of measurement to capture the relevant dynamics)". That parenthesis is the identifiability condition they actually state.
- Indirect estimation, and its limit [read]: given an estimate of Phi(dt) one can solve for A, "the indirect method", but "this approach cannot be applied in all circumstances, as it involves using the matrix logarithm ... As the matrix logarithm function in the general case does not give a unique solution, this method is only appropriate if both the estimated Phi(dt) and true underlying A matrices have real eigenvalues only". (They cite Hamerle, Nagl & Singer 1991.)
- Parameter count [read]: a full drift matrix for V variables has V^2 parameters ("nine ... for three variables", four for two), plus the V x V innovation covariance; complexity "grows rapidly".
- Practical remedies [read]: put the data on a time grid with missing values rather than fit unequal intervals as if equal; CT estimation handles unequal intervals natively.
- Multilevel [read]: traditional panels with 2 to 6 waves can only allow person-specific intercepts and must treat the lagged effects as common; ESM "with many repeated measurements per person" allows person-specific lagged coefficients, by analysing each person separately or by a dynamic multilevel model. "The degree to which this can be done depends on the number of time points we have per person." No numeric threshold given.

**Theorems.** None in the chapter; Eq. 4 is the standard linear-SDE solution. Related work cited but not read: Dormann & Griffin 2015 and Deboeck & Preacher 2016 use Eq. 4 to find the dt at which a cross-lagged effect peaks (Dormann & Griffin, *Psychological Methods* 20(4), 489-505, DOI 10.1037/met0000041; Deboeck & Preacher, *Structural Equation Modeling* 23(1), 61-75 (online 2015), DOI 10.1080/10705511.2014.973960; both Crossref-confirmed, [citation only]). Kuiper & Ryan 2018, *Structural Equation Modeling* 25(5), 809-823, DOI 10.1080/10705511.2018.1431046 [citation only]: the Phi-to-A transformation for comparing studies with different intervals.

**Empirical standing.** An expository chapter with a bivariate illustration; it is not a simulation study of recovery. Software: ctsem (Driver, Oud & Voelkle 2017, *J. Stat. Software* 77(5), DOI 10.18637/jss.v077.i05, [citation only]). The structural-equation CT route is also Voelkle, Oud, Davidov & Schmidt 2012, DOI 10.1037/a0027543 [citation only]; the hierarchical OU model of affect is Oravecz, Tuerlinckx & Vandekerckhove 2011, DOI 10.1037/a0024375 [citation only, and file 27 holds the affect-dynamics parameter data].

**Operand/operator translation.** The drift matrix A is the natural carrier of drive-to-drive coupling in the linear regime: diagonal = restoring rate of a drive to its set point, off-diagonal = coupling. Phi(dt) = e^{A dt} is a **semigroup**: composing two lags is a product, e^{A s} e^{A t} = e^{A(s+t)}. For the algebra this is a ready-made composition law for "let time pass", and the fitting of A is where the data bite.

## 4. Boker and latent differential equations, and power in ESM designs

- **Boker, S. M. & Nesselroade, J. R. (2002).** A method for modeling the intrinsic dynamics of intraindividual variability: recovering the parameters of simulated oscillators in multi-wave panel data. *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 37(1), 127-160. DOI 10.1207/s15327906mbr3701_06 (Crossref confirmed: title, authors, volume, pages). **Abstract and results not read.** What the title says: latent differential equation (LDE) modelling, where derivative estimates are formed from time-delay embedded data inside a SEM, tested by recovering parameters of simulated oscillators. I will not quote recovery accuracy or sample sizes from it. Boker, Neale & Rausch (2004), *Latent differential equation modeling with multivariate multi-occasion indicators*, in van Montfort, Oud & Satorra (Eds.), *Recent Developments on Structural Equation Models* (pp. 151-174), Kluwer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4020-1958-6_9, [citation only]. This is a different route from SINDy's: the second-order oscillator dx'' = eta x + zeta x' is the usual target, estimated through embedding rather than numerical differentiation. A direct read of the 2002 paper's simulation tables is the first follow-up this file needs.
- **Schultzberg & Muthen (2018).** Number of subjects and time points needed for multilevel time-series analysis: a simulation study of dynamic structural equation modeling. *Structural Equation Modeling* 25(4), DOI 10.1080/10705511.2017.1392862 (Crossref confirmed) [citation only; title says it contains exactly the N-by-T guidance this direction wants; unread].
- **Lafit et al. (2021).** Selection of the number of participants in intensive longitudinal studies: a user-friendly Shiny app and tutorial to perform power analysis in multilevel regression models that account for temporal dependencies. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science* 4(1), DOI 10.1177/2515245920978738 (Crossref confirmed). **Read** (pre-print). Method [read]: Monte Carlo power. Simulate many datasets from the hypothesised population model, fit, record the proportion that reject the null, repeat over a grid of participant numbers; "determine the number of participants necessary to reach a predetermined amount of power (e.g., 80%)". Worked case [read] (their Illustration 1): the effect of baseline depressive-symptom score (a continuous level-2 predictor) on momentary negative affect in people with MDD, unstandardised fixed effect beta01 = 1.50, **70 measurements per participant**, AR(1) within-person errors (rho = 0.46), 1000 Monte Carlo replicates: power 53.8% at 15 participants, above 80% when participants exceed 30. (The "six equidistant time points" in their paper describe the toy dataset of their Table 1, not this power run.) Limits for us: it is **power for fixed effects in multilevel regression with autocorrelated residuals**, not power to recover an A matrix or a gate; its worked examples take parameter values from a clinical dataset; and its authors note that runs can take hours. The method (power by simulation from a stated generative model) is what transfers.

## 5. A recovery model for the algebra (my derivations and simulation, not literature)

Everything in this section is **[derived]** or **[my simulation]**. It is built on Eqs. 2-4 of Section 3 and is meant as a specification to check, not a finding.

**5.1 Generative model.** For a person (or group) with d drives, latent drive deviations x(t) follow the linear regime of the algebra's law, dx = A x dt + G dW (Section 3, Eq. 2), observed at times t_1 < ... < t_n (irregular) as y_i = H x(t_i) + e_i with e_i ~ N(0, R). H is the measurement map from drives to the observed affect or event proxies (file 27: observed variables are proxies, so H is itself unknown and unidentifiable beyond scale and rotation unless fixed by convention). Nonlinear gates enter as an additional term f(x) in the drift, the SINDy-style part (Eq. 7 of Section 1).

**5.2 Scalar case: the only exact result.** Take d = 1, A = −theta, theta > 0, relaxation time tau = 1/theta, stationary variance sigma^2/(2 theta). With continuous observation over span T the Fisher information for theta is T E[x^2]/sigma^2 = T/(2 theta). So

  **var(theta_hat) ≈ 2 theta / T, relative standard error = sqrt(2/(theta T)) = sqrt(2 tau / T).**

For a 20% relative SE, T = 50 tau; for 10%, T = 200 tau. It is the span measured in relaxation times, not the count of prompts, that sets precision. Sampling at interval D gives AR(1) coefficient phi = e^{−theta D}; the delta method gives var(theta_hat) ≈ (e^{2 theta D} − 1)/(T D), so the inflation of the standard error over continuous observation is **sqrt((e^{2 theta D} − 1)/(2 theta D))**:

| theta D (interval / relaxation time) | 0.1 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SE inflation | 1.05 | 1.14 | 1.31 | 1.79 | 3.66 | 8.19 |

(computed). Reading: sampling at one relaxation time costs about 1.8 times the standard error, at three relaxation times about 8 times. A drive with a half-day relaxation time sampled once a day is at theta D ≈ 1.4 (since theta = ln 2 / half-life if "half-day" is the half-life) and sits in the poor region. [my simulation, 2,000 replications, theta = 1, no measurement error]: n = 50 at D = 1 gave a median theta_hat of 1.12 with a spread (IQR/1.349) of 0.42, against a theoretical 0.36; the theory is a large-sample figure and understates finite-sample spread by roughly a fifth here.

**5.3 Measurement error biases the restoring rate upward.** Observed y = x + e turns the AR(1) latent process into AR(1) plus noise, which has an ARMA(1,1) autocorrelation structure; an AR(1) fit attenuates phi. [my simulation, same settings, e with SD 0.5 against latent SD 1]: at D = 1, n = 50 the median theta_hat was 1.36 (true 1); at D = 0.5, n = 100, 1.56; at D = 0.25, n = 200, **2.0**. More prompts at a shorter interval made the bias worse, not better, because the noise floor dominates adjacent-sample correlation. Practical consequence: a restoring rate fitted to ESM self-report without modelling measurement error is an overestimate, and "inertia" results in file 27 inherit this. The remedy is a state-space fit with R as a parameter (the CT SEM / Kalman route, Section 3), which needs replicates of the measurement (multi-item scales) or enough span to separate R from the dynamics. Reliability of the momentary scales is the thing to feed in; file 27 records within-person reliabilities of about .3 to .7 for momentary motive scales, a range in which R is large.

**5.4 Aliasing for coupled and oscillatory drives.** For d ≥ 2 with complex eigenvalues of A (coupled drives that overshoot, as in the Gottman-Murray style pair), Phi(dt) = e^{A dt} no longer determines A uniquely: the principal matrix logarithm cannot distinguish an angular frequency omega from omega + 2 pi k / dt. [my simulation] A = [[−0.1, −2], [2, −0.1]] sampled at D = 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 returned omega = 2.0 correctly through the principal log (omega D = 1, 2, 3, all below pi), but at D = 2 (omega D = 4) it returned −1.14 and at D = 3 (omega D = 6) it returned −0.09. That is the Nyquist condition omega D < pi, **i.e. the interval must be shorter than half the period**. It matches Ryan et al.'s statement that the matrix-log route is safe only when the eigenvalues are real, and their qualifier "sufficient frequency of measurement". With irregular intervals the aliasing is partly broken (different D give different ambiguities) but not removed in a small sample.

**5.5 Parameter counts.** A full d-drive drift matrix has d^2 parameters, the innovation covariance d(d+1)/2 more, plus R (measurement) and H. A person-level fit with d = 4 drives has 16 + 10 = 26 dynamic parameters before measurement. With span 50 tau as the minimum for 20% precision on *one* rate (5.2), the coupling terms, whose signal is the product of coupling size and the others' variance, need more: no closed form is given here. A multilevel (partial pooling) fit is the only realistic route, which is the Ryan et al. conclusion for ESM, but no sample-size table in these sources covers it.

**5.6 Which problems are "identifiable at all" before any power question.** (a) The scale and the rotation of the drive coordinates are not identified from proxies (H unknown): the algebra's drive names need anchoring operations (an intervention that moves one drive; file 27's measurement gaps). (b) With a SINDy library, correlated candidate terms make support recovery ambiguous; the original paper warns of degeneracy in the linear-in-polynomial case; the ensemble inclusion probability reports it as lower than 1 rather than hiding it. (c) For a hidden (unobserved) drive, a latent coordinate is identifiable only up to a similarity transform; the observed variables' Phi(dt) fixes the eigenvalues but not the labels. These are structural; none of the cited papers states them as theorems for the social setting, so they are my reasoning from the linear-systems structure.

**5.7 A recipe the algebra can adopt** (a specification, to be checked against Schultzberg & Muthen and Boker before use):
1. Pre-register the generative model (5.1) with a stated tau per drive from the affect-dynamics priors in file 27 and D087.
2. Fix the design in units of tau: prompt interval D ≤ tau (SE inflation ≤ 1.8), span T ≥ 50 tau per person for a 20% relative SE on one restoring rate, and D < period/2 for any oscillatory pair.
3. Compute power by simulation (the Lafit et al. method, Section 4) over a grid of persons and prompts, with measurement error R set from the scale's within-person reliability; do not use closed-form power for A-matrix entries.
4. Fit by CT state-space (ctsem-type) or its Bayesian multilevel equivalent; use the E-SINDy inclusion probability as the report for "is this nonlinear term present".
5. Report the result as an interval or inclusion probability, never a bare coefficient, since 5.3 shows the point estimate can be biased by a factor of two with plausible measurement error.

## What exists vs what does not

**Exists.** (i) An exact-form CT model and its DT relation, Phi(dt) = e^{A dt}, with the stated limits on indirect inversion (Ryan et al., read). (ii) A sparse-regression recipe for finding which terms are in a nonlinear law, with a noise protocol and an ensemble version giving inclusion probabilities and a 21-point real-data case (Brunton et al.; Fasel et al., read). (iii) Power by Monte Carlo for fixed effects in multilevel ESM regression, with a worked power curve (Lafit et al., read). (iv) Titles of simulation studies on N and T for dynamic SEM and for LDE recovery (Schultzberg & Muthen; Boker & Nesselroade), unread.

**Does not.** (i) No source read gives a **power rule for recovering the entries of a drift matrix A** from ESM data in terms of persons, prompts, interval and relaxation time; the nearest results are unread. The scalar result in 5.2 is my derivation and the aliasing and bias numbers in 5.3-5.4 are one small simulation. (ii) No identifiability theorem for SINDy-type recovery on social or affective data; the sparse-recovery guarantees cited are for incoherent measurements and are not checked for any behavioural library. (iii) None of the sources read here recovers **gates and thresholds** (the step-like nonlinearities the algebra needs for fear and satiety laws) from sparse data. The wider literature is not empty: a multilevel threshold autoregressive model for state-dependent affect regulation fitted to intensive longitudinal data exists (De Haan-Rietdijk, Gottman, Bergeman & Hamaker 2016, *Psychometrika* 81(1), 217-241, DOI 10.1007/s11336-014-9417-x, Crossref-confirmed, [citation only]); no power rule for it was found in this file's sources. (iv) Nothing handling **event data** (point processes of acts) alongside ESM states; this direction's "event" half is not covered by any of the seeds, and is a separate literature (Hawkes-process identifiability; not searched). (v) No validated H: the mapping from drives to observables.
