**Short version.**

1. **None of the four named papers is open** (Caplow 1956, Caplow 1959, Vinacke & Arkoff 1957, Gamson 1961) and the 1968 book is lending-library only, so none is [read] at source. What I read instead: an author-hosted mathematical restatement of both theories that lists Caplow's assumptions, all eight types, his predictions type by type and Vinacke & Arkoff's counts (Shenoy 1978); an independent review whose table agrees with it on every type except Type 3, where the review's row is a relabelling slip (Mesterton-Gibbons et al. 2011; see section 11); Vinacke's own technical reports; and open modern papers, including a preregistered three-platform replication (Wissink et al. 2022, CC BY).
2. **The eight types.** Six are in Caplow 1956 and two were added in 1959 (Shenoy). Eight is also 4 orderings of three strengths, with the sign of A minus (B + C) split out where more than one sign is possible (Mesterton-Gibbons). So 084's "six" and the map's "eight" are both right, for different dates.
3. **"Weak unite against the strong" is a prediction for part of the table only.** The weak pair BC is Caplow's unique prediction for Type 2 and one of two for Type 5. In Types 7 and 8 the strongest member is in every predicted pair, in Types 4 and 6 nobody coalesces, and Caplow's 1956 text explicitly declines to choose between AC and BC in Type 5. The weak-pair choice there comes from Gamson (cheapest winning coalition), Chertkoff 1967 and Walker 1973.
4. **The effect replicates, at nearly the same size, across 60+ years and three media.** In the 4-3-2 triad the weakest pair is 67% of coalitions in Vinacke & Arkoff (59 of 88, 1950s, face-to-face, male students), 67% (35 of 52) in a lab replication run by mid-2018 (data deposited June 2018), 65% (52 of 80) on MTurk, and 60% of two-person coalitions (93 of 155) in a probability sample of Dutch adults playing online. Effect size w against equal thirds: 0.73 [ours, V&A], 0.78 and 0.72 (printed, lab and MTurk), 0.60 [ours, Dutch panel].
5. **The mechanism is not power.** The best-supported account is an equity expectation: strong bargainers claim more (first-offer self-allocation 60.2 against 42.1 of 100 for the strongest and weakest), weak bargainers expect that and approach each other. In these games every bargainer belongs to the same number of winning pairs, so the weights confer no extra winning chances and the effect is better described as a price effect than a power effect [ours].
6. **It flips or vanishes under conditions that matter for the algebra.** Families (conservative coalitions 9.75 per family against 1.56 revolutionary), a competing outside triad (decisions become consensus, weights ignored), weights earned by work (6 of 6 triads allied the two strongest, n = 6 per cell, 1974), a computer game against fictional opponents (players chose the stronger partner), n of 4 or more (strongest included), and primates (mixed by species).
7. **As a sign-generator.** Coalition structure maps onto a balanced signed triangle: AB|C gives (+AB, -AC, -BC), ABC gives all positive, no coalition gives no ties [ours]. Balance theory leaves the three two-negative triangles symmetric; Caplow and Gamson are rules for choosing which edge is the positive one from the strength vector. But a coalition here is a binding agreement to share a prize, not an affective tie ("Coalitions are not the same as affective cliques of mutual attraction", Grusky et al. 1995), so the mapping to the algebra's signs is ours and untested against affect.

## 1. The theory as the sources state it

### 1.0 Their vocabulary

- **Caplow / Gamson tradition.** triad; strength, power or "resources" (additive); coalition (two against one); isolate (the excluded member); precoalition condition; control, internal and external; "type" 1-8 of triad; revolutionary coalition (the two weaker unite against the strongest), conservative coalition (the strong keep the order, "strong against weak"), improper coalition (neither); minimum resource theory, cheapest winning coalition; proportional payoffs; power index (Caplow, Gamson, Shapley-Shubik, Banzhaf, Deegan-Packel); simple weighted majority game written [q; a, b, c] or q(a-b-c), e.g. "5(432)"; "triple alliance" or "grand coalition" (ABC); "no coalition" (I).
- **Vinacke's programme.** power pattern; "all-equal", "all-different", "all-powerful"; exploitative and accommodative strategy; weak and strong alliances, 50/50 deals, triple alliances; "Strength-is-Weakness" is the later label (Wissink et al.).
- **Ethology.** resource holding potential (RHP); all-up (= revolutionary), all-down (= conservative) and bridging coalitions (van Schaik et al. 2006; Chapais 1995, as given in the review); coalition and alliance (an alliance is a long-lasting coalition).
- **Equity versus equality.** The "equity norm" (payoff proportional to input; Adams 1965) against equality (equal shares); Komorita & Chertkoff's strong-apply-equity, weak-apply-equality.

### 1.1 Caplow's assumptions [read: via Shenoy 1978, p. 182, who attributes them to Caplow 1956; of his own mathematical restatement he says that it was "never formally stated" but is implicit in Caplow]

- A.1 Members of a triad may differ in strength. A stronger member can control a weaker one and will seek to.
- A.2 Each member seeks control over the others. Control over two others is preferred to control over one, and control over one to control over none.
- A.3 Strength is additive: a coalition's strength is the sum of its two members' strengths.
- A.4 Coalitions form inside an existing triad that has a pre-coalition condition. If a stronger member tries to coerce a weaker one into a coalition that does not advantage it, an advantageous opposing coalition forms.

Mesterton-Gibbons et al. 2011 section 2 add two readings of the same assumptions [read]: individuals are indifferent between "internal" control (the stronger partner inside a coalition) and "external" control (belonging to a coalition stronger than the target), and the starting configuration is three unallied individuals. Shenoy notes Caplow "implicitly assumes" that bargaining starts from no coalition. Caplow 1968, as quoted by Grusky et al., adds that in linked triads a partner in one triad is not an opponent in another [read: via Grusky et al.]; they found that untenable in families.

- **Lettering warning.** In Shenoy's Table 1 the weak member of Type 3 is A. Other sources relabel so that A is always the strongest (Grusky et al. write Type 3 as "A = B > C"). Check which letter is weak before comparing predictions across sources.
- **Why eight** [read: Mesterton-Gibbons et al.]: four orderings of three strengths (X = Y = Z; X = Y > Z; X > Y = Z; X > Y > Z), and in the last two X may be less than, equal to or greater than Y + Z: 1 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 8.
- **Dates.** Types 1-6 are Caplow 1956. Types 7 and 8 "were overlooked in the original presentation" and added in Caplow 1959 [read: via Shenoy, p. 182].
- **Context.** Caplow formulated the 1956 theory for what he later called "continuous" triads (an enduring triad, control of joint activity and of rewards inside the situation). In 1959 he revised it for "episodic" and "terminal" triads and drew different conclusions for Cases 2-4, 6 and 7 of the review's numbering (Caplow Types 3, 2, 8, 5 and 7) [read: Mesterton-Gibbons et al., Table 1 caption]. The reviewers' comment: this "merely emphasizes that coalition formation is highly context-dependent". The lab games below, with a binding coalition per game and many games, sit between "episodic" and "continuous" [ours].
- **Caplow's reasoning for the type that matters most** [read: via Shenoy, p. 187, quoting Caplow 1956, Type 5]: A seeks to join both B and C, and C seeks both A and B, "but B has no incentive to enter a coalition with A and has a very strong incentive to enter a coalition with C". And: "Whether the differential strength of A and B will make them differentially attractive to C lies outside the scope of our present assumptions." So the 1956 prediction for 4-3-2 is AC or BC, undecided between them, and never AB.
- Caplow's own summary, as the review paraphrases it: "surprisingly often weakness is strength".

### 1.3 What "weak unite against the strong" is, and is not

BC (the two weaker members against the strongest) is in Caplow's predicted set for Types 1 (one of three), 2 (the only one) and 5 (one of two). It is not predicted in Type 3 (there B and C are the strong pair and the weak A is courted by both), nor in Types 7 and 8 (the strongest A is inside every predicted pair), and in Types 4 and 6 (A beats B and C combined, so no true coalition) nothing is predicted. So the statement is local to the region where nobody is a dictator and the strongest is beatable by the other two (A < B + C). Even there the 1956 text does not pick BC over AC in Type 5; the weak-pair choice is added by later work (section 1.4).

### 1.4 Formal restatements [read at source for Shenoy's equations; read: via Shenoy or the review for the attributions]

**Shenoy 1978, restating Caplow** (his own words: "we will restate Caplow's theory in a mathematical setting"; I restate in mine):
- (E1) A weighted majority game is [q; a_1, ..., a_n]; a coalition R wins iff the sum of its weights is at least q. It is proper if q is greater than half the total weight, and the Caplow restatement assumes a proper game. Caplow's theory is stated for triads; Shenoy notes the restatement holds for n-person proper weighted majority games.
- (E2) Control: in a coalition structure P (a partition of the players), i controls j iff either i and j sit in the same winning coalition of P and a_i > a_j, or i sits in a winning coalition of P and j does not.
- (E3) Caplow power index: kappa(P)(i) = p(P)(i) / sum over j of p(P)(j), where p(P)(i) is the number of players i controls; kappa = 0 where the sum is 0.
- (E4) Domination: P1 dominates P2 iff some nonempty coalition R in P1 has kappa(P1)(i) > kappa(P2)(i) for every i in R. The prediction is the core (undominated structures) or the dynamic solution (the union of the closed communicating classes of the domination graph; Shenoy 1977b, 1979, not read).
- Result [read]: Shenoy's Table 1 compares Caplow with the core K_0(kappa) (K_0 is his symbol for the core, K_1 for the dynamic solution, p. 180). Starting from all-singletons, the core reproduces Caplow's predictions for all eight types; read without the starting-point assumption it predicts extra structures in Types 4, 6, 7 and 8. (Corrected in validation 2026-10-30: this line said "the dynamic solution"; Table 3's final structures are labelled K_1. A brute-force computation of E2-E4 for all eight types reproduces Table 1's K_0 column exactly and finds the core and the dynamic solution identical in every type, so either reading gives Caplow's predictions from all-singletons [ours, validation 2026-10-30].) Shenoy also finds a small inconsistency in Caplow's own analyses of Types 6 and 3 (in Type 6 Caplow implies that B controls C in the no-coalition structure; in Type 3, that B is superior to A there) and resolves it by saying "Caplow has erred".
- With equiprobable transitions (Shenoy Table 3 [fig]): Type 1: AB, AC, BC 1/3 each; Type 2: BC with probability 1; Type 3: AB 1/2, AC 1/2; Types 4 and 6: no coalition; Type 5: AC 1/3, BC 2/3 (AB passes through as an intermediate state only); Types 7 and 8: AB 1/2, AC 1/2. Chertkoff's 1967 extra assumption gives the same Type 5 split (BC twice as often as AC, AB never) and, for Type 3, AB and AC equally likely and BC never.

**Gamson 1961, restated by Shenoy section 7** [read: via Shenoy]:
- Main hypothesis: each participant expects others to demand "a share of the payoff proportional to the amount of resources which they contribute to a coalition".
- Predictions: (i) a player favours a cheapest winning coalition (least total weight); (ii) a pair forms iff both prefer it; (iii) coalitions form step by step, two merging at a time; (iv) a formed pair is replaced by one player whose weight is the sum.
- (E5) Gamson power index: gamma(P)(i) = a_i / (sum of weights in i's coalition) if i is in a winning coalition of P, else 0 (defined for proper weighted majority games without a dictator or veto players). Theorem 7.1 (Shenoy): for such a game the core K_0(gamma) is exactly the set of structures that contain a cheapest winning coalition; restricting it to structures reachable from all-singletons gives Gamson's predictions. (Corrected in validation 2026-10-30: this line said "the dynamic solution"; the theorem as printed is about the core. The proof shows every other structure is dominated by every structure in that set, so the dynamic solution equals it too; a brute-force check on Types 1, 2, 3 and 5 agrees [ours].)
- Shenoy's own caution: his model reproduces both theories "under the same assumptions", which "should not be misinterpreted as an endorsement"; both are descriptive and lean on an implicit power index that has shortcomings from a normative point of view.

**Later refinements** [read: via the review and Messe et al.]:
- Chertkoff (1967): a revised Caplow theory adding reciprocation (a coalition needs both sides to want it), argued superior to Gamson's.
- Walker (1973): refines it the same way. The review's calculation for the 4-3-2 triad: BC 7/12, AC 1/3, AB 1/12 (none 0); Messe et al. quote Walker's prediction for 12 triads as 7 : 4 : 1 for 2-3 : 2-4 : 3-4, which is the same thing.
- Komorita & Chertkoff (1973) bargaining theory [read: via Wissink et al., note 2]: strong bargainers apply equity (claim in proportion to resources), weak bargainers apply equality (claim half), and the predicted coalition is again the cheapest winning one.
- Equity arithmetic for 4-3-2 over a prize of 100 [read: Wissink et al.]: A expects about 66 with C but 57 with B; B and C expect 60 and 40 with each other, against about 43 and 33 with A. So each bargainer's best partner is the weakest other, and BC is the only pair where both sides' best partner is the other.

### 1.5 Limits the formalisers themselves state

- It is purely ordinal: only the ordering of strengths and the sign of A minus (B + C) enter, so the magnitude of strength differences is ignored [read: review, section 2].
- All of Caplow, Gamson and Walker are "largely verbal and ignore properties of the RHP distribution, in particular its variance" [read: review, section 9]. The review's back-of-the-envelope model (section 3) finds the weak pair stable under equal sharing when the strongest is less than twice the weakest (a sufficient condition: stability needs b < (Y + Z)/(X + Y - Z), which holds for every b < 1 once X <= 2Z; the PDF's text layer drops the minus signs; derivation re-checked in validation 2026-10-30 [ours]; the 4-3-2 triad sits exactly on X = 2Z), and analytical contest models in its section 5 flip the predicted coalition between "all-up" (the weak pair) and "all-down" or "bridging" depending on the convexity of the contest success function (its Table 3), and, for one contest function with r = 2 and proportional allocation within the winning coalition, on whether X squared exceeds Y squared plus Z squared [read: review, the cited models of Tan & Wang 2010, *IJGT* 39: 273-299, doi:10.1007/s00182-009-0203-4, not read]. So the sign of the prediction is not a property of the ordinal type alone.
- Willis (1962, *Sociometry* 25(4): 358-376, doi:10.2307/2785775) extended it to tetrads; experiments "offered only limited support" and it was not developed further [read: via the review]. Grusky et al. say Willis "identified seventeen different types of coalitions in the tetrad" and predicted the most frequent two- and three-way coalitions "within each type", which reads as seventeen tetrad power types [read: via Grusky et al.].

## 2. The classic test: Vinacke & Arkoff (1957)

**Design** [read: via Shenoy's footnote 4, which quotes Vinacke & Arkoff "almost verbatim", and Vinacke & Gullickson TR 3, whose "college" columns are the same two samples]. A modified pachisi board, outer lanes only; the aim is to reach "home" first; the winner takes 100 points (points, not money; Wissink et al. call these studies hypothetical-payoff). One die, cast by the experimenter; each player advances his weight times the die. At any time a player may, in return for a promise of a share of the prize, ally with another; allies pool strengths at once and move as one with the summed weight, and an alliance is permanent for that game. Terms are bargained face to face around the board, with the experimenter acting only as referee and recording the transactions (TR 3); that the bargaining was spoken aloud is an inference. Weights were drawn by lot: TR 3's Appendix A gives the college students' instructions, "Before each game each player will draw a counter out of a hopper. The number on this counter will determine the player's strength for that game" [read: TR 3, validation 2026-10-30]. If those college triads are V&A's (see Unconfirmed), the 1957 weights were drawn the same way. The children and adolescents in TR 3, and the TR 4 triads, also drew counters. Thirty male triads (Vinacke & Arkoff) and thirty female triads (Vinacke 1959) each played 18 games, three of each of six weight patterns, interspersed [read: author's summary, final report; TR 3]. The site and the data years are not stated in the sources I read (the programme was at the University of Hawaii from 1962, per the final report); the article is dated August 1957, so the data are from the mid-1950s [inference].

**Counts, male triads, 90 games per type** [read: via Shenoy Table 2, checked on the page image]:

| Type (weights) | none | AB | AC | BC | ABC | Caplow's predicted set | in that set |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (1,1,1) | 8 | 33 | 17 | 30 | 2 | AB, AC, BC | 80/90 = 89% |
| 2 (3,2,2) | 1 | 13 | 12 | 64 | 0 | BC | 64/90 = 71% |
| 3 (1,2,2; A weak) | 11 | 24 | 40 | 15 | 0 | AB, AC | 64/90 = 71% |
| 4 (3,1,1) | 62 | 11 | 10 | 7 | 0 | none | 62/90 = 69% |
| 5 (4,3,2) | 2 | 9 | 20 | 59 | 0 | AC, BC | 79/90 = 88% (BC alone, Gamson: 59/90 = 66%) |
| 6 (4,2,1) | 60 | 9 | 13 | 8 | 0 | none | 60/90 = 67% |

The right-hand column is my arithmetic [ours]. It is a descriptive hit rate: the predicted sets differ in width (Type 1's set is three of five outcomes), the three games per type within a triad are not independent, and the triad-level data are not available, so I give no p-values. Against a flat baseline over four outcomes (none, AB, AC, BC) the hit rate is higher in every row (Type 2: 71% against 25%; Type 4: 69% against 25%), but the baseline is arbitrary.

**Cross-checks.** (a) The Type 5 row, (2, 59, 20, 9, 0) of 90, equals the vector (1/45, 59/90, 2/9, 1/10, 0) that Mesterton-Gibbons et al. print for the same case. (b) Wissink et al. quote Vinacke & Arkoff's inclusion rates in the 3-2-2 triad as A 28.9%, B 86.5%, C 85.4%. From the Type 2 row: B in 77 of 89 coalitions = 86.5%, C in 76 of 89 = 85.4%, A in 25 of 89 = 28.1%. The first two agree to the digit; the printed 28.9 for A should be 28.1 (three inclusion rates must sum to 200%) [ours]. These two agreements are the main reason I treat Shenoy's table as faithful to the 1957 data.

**What the numbers say, and what they do not.**
- The weak pair is 59 of 88 coalitions (67%) in the 4-3-2 triad and 64 of 89 (72%) in the 3-2-2 triad; effect size against equal thirds, w = 0.73 and 0.82 [ours, pooling games].
- Caplow's own prediction for Type 5 (AC or BC, AB never) holds for 79 of 88 coalitions (90%); the 10% AB (strong pair) is outside it. Gamson's and Chertkoff's weak-pair preference is the better description (Gamson 1961 reads this very outcome as support for his theory, per the review). Walker's refinement (BC 58%, AC 33%, AB 8%) is close to the observed 66%, 22%, 10% of games [ours: observed shares of 90 games].
- Type 1 is symmetric yet unequal: AB 33, BC 30, AC 17 of 80 coalitions. No source I read explains the asymmetry (seat or role effects are possible).
- Types 4 and 6 (A can win unaided): "no coalition" about two thirds of the time, a coalition anyway in one third.
- **A structural caveat.** In pachisi the strongest player beats each rival alone because the die is shared and his multiplier is higher, so BC > A is a real reason to ally; Kelley & Arrowood (1960) argued part of the effect is confusion about bargaining power, and Wilke and Mulder's "conspiracy" account says the weak equalise an unfair advantage [read: via Wissink et al.]. Explicit instructions that weight confers no bargaining power did not remove the effect (Vinacke et al. 1964) [read: via Wissink et al.]. Wissink et al.'s reference list identifies this as Vinacke, Crowell, Dien & Young, *Behav. Sci.* 11: 180-189 (1966; TR 6 of 1964): the same study as "Information does not move it" in section 3, described there in Vinacke's own words. Kelley & Arrowood also found substantial change in a 4-3-2 game after 10 to 70 trials, so repeated-game results are partly learned [read: via Wissink et al.].
- Scale: group decision (which pair binds). Intervention: the experimenter assigns weights, openly; participants know the rule. Era: 1950s. Medium: face-to-face. Population: male university students.

## 3. What changed the result inside the original programme (Hawaii and Buffalo, 1957-1968)

Everything in this section is [read: author's summary] unless it says TR 3 or TR 4, which I read in full. All are face-to-face, in the board game or its variants, with students unless stated, and group-scale decisions.

- **Sex changes whether and how, not which pair** (TR 3, college triads, 30 male and 30 female, each group three games per pattern; Table 1 read from page images, Table 4 from the text layer).
  - All-different 4-3-2: two-person alliance in every game: 93% of male groups, 63% of female; at least one no-coalition game: 7% and 33%; any triple alliance: 0% and 7%.
  - All-equal: two-person alliances in at least two of three games: 93% and 54% (chi-square 8.14, p < .01); any triple alliance: 7% and 50% (chi-square 13.88, p < .001).
  - All-powerful (3-1-1, an alliance cannot help): no coalition in two or more of the three games: 76% of male groups and 47% of female; two-person alliances in two or more games anyway: 23% and 50%.
  - When an alliance forms in the 4-3-2 pattern, the weak pair is above chance (above one third of the group's games) in 73% of male and 67% of female groups, against 7% and 7% for an alliance with the strong player (chi-square 27.78 and 23.24, p < .001); the report finds no significant sex difference there. Females also reach 50/50 deals more often; males bargain more.
  - The programme calls the two styles "exploitative" (males) and "accommodative" (females) and measures them with a six-sign index and, later, a questionnaire "Test of Strategy" (TR 7; questionnaire superior; 1968 report).
- **Age** (TR 3; 10 triads per sex per age): at 7-8 years males are as accommodative as females or more (many triple alliances and 50/50 deals) and become steadily more exploitative with age, 14-16-year-olds lying between children and college students; females are accommodative at all ages. The report reads the children as largely ignoring the weights: in the all-different pattern the weak-to-weak first offer is significant at 14-16 and in college but not at 7-8 (TR 3 Table 5). In the 4-3-2 pattern the weak pair is above chance in 80% of 14-16-year-old male groups (n = 10) and 100% of female (n = 9), and in 70% and 57% of 7-8-year-olds (n = 10 and 7). At 7-8 the weak-against-strong contrast is not significant for either sex (males P = .10, females n.s.; TR 3 Table 4, page image; corrected in validation 2026-10-30, the line said "not significant for females" only).
- **A competing outside triad removes the effect** (TR 4, 1963; 10 pairs of triads per sex, 60 men and 60 women; weights are votes; the prize is also split half by weight). The great majority of decisions, on both the choice of entry and the bonus split, were triple alliances ("consensus"); "Only triple alliances are typical of the decisions in this inter-group competition", and "the relative strength of players in inter-group competition has very little, if any, influence upon decisions". Comparison with the board game: only 2 of 30 male triads there ever formed a triple alliance, and 4 of 30 female triads in more than 25% of games; differences from the inter-triad games are significant at p < .01 or better. Winning triads differed from losing ones in skill and in decision efficiency. Vinacke reads it as intra-group cooperation under inter-group competition (Deutsch).
- **Information does not move it** (Vinacke, Crowell, Dien & Young 1966, *Behav. Sci.* 11(3): 180-189, doi:10.1002/bs.3830110305; Wissink et al.'s "Vinacke et al., 1964"; one, two or all three players told about the "strictly rational" and "perception of weakness" strategies; six power patterns): no shift toward the strictly rational outcome, and informed players did not differ from uninformed ones. A questionnaire item on motivation to win correlated with fewer weak coalitions in both sexes, which Vinacke reads as information chiefly raising motivation.
- **Incentives** (Vinacke 1962/1964, four incentive conditions, 30 groups per sex per condition, 120 per sex): "a variety of effects", sex differences similar and significant under all. Cumulative score: players behind ally against the leader regardless of strength, without wholly overcoming the preference for weak pairs (Vinacke 1959 abstract).
- **Luck**: the stochastic condition (each player throws the die for his own move, against one throw that moves all three) and the shorter of two boards both reduced the incidence of coalitions, which Vinacke reads as a tendency to gamble on outcomes (Vinacke, Lichtman & Cherulnik 1967, *J. Gen. Psychol.* 77(2): 165-176 per Crossref, 163-176 in the final report; doi:10.1080/00221309.1967.9710399).
- **Culture and friendship**: Micronesian male triads included some cultures that played like American females and one strongly exploitative (Saunders 1960, MA thesis); friend and non-friend triads of Japanese-American students differed little (Eileen Iwaka?ai 1960, MA thesis, University of Hawaii; the surname is smudged on the final report's page image and "Iwakai" is a reading, not confirmed), with the authors suspecting a cultural norm.
- **Disposition**: high achievement goes with initiating offers and maximising resources, high nurturance with receiving offers (Chaney & Vinacke 1960; confirmed for males, not tied to strategy for females by Amidjaja & Vinacke 1965).
- **Mixed sex and family triads**: a majority effect in mixed-sex triads (Bond & Vinacke 1961); father-mother-son triads (problem, normal, "synthetic" families) were highly accommodative on the board game, and in a family decision task the artificial families disagreed most and compromised most (Bodin 1965).
- **Size**: see section 5.

## 4. Later tests, other eras and media

### 4.1 Messe, Vallacher & Phillips (1974): earned weights make the strong ally [read]

54 male Michigan State undergraduates recruited for "motivational research" for money, 18 triads, 6 per condition. A pretask (proofreading and completing pictures) lasted 2, 1.5 or 1 hour. A 4-3-2 cribbage-board game: winner $7, the two losers split $2; two players may trade pegs for one peg of the summed weight, and must first agree how to divide the $7 (so the excluded player gets $2). Conditions: resources earned by the pretask time; random resources (no pretask); "inputs-prepaid" (weights by time worked, but the work was paid first).

| Condition | 2-3 (weak pair) | 2-4 | 3-4 (strong pair) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earned inputs | 0 | 0 | **6** |
| Inputs, prepaid | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| Random | 4 | 1 | 1 |

Pooling the two control conditions gives 7 : 3 : 2, against Walker's predicted 7 : 4 : 1 [read: Table 2, image]. Earned inputs differ from the other two: F = 17.62 (Scheffe), p < .01; all six earned-input coalitions split the $7 "equitably"; 14 of 18 earned-input subjects mentioned equity or fairness when asked why, against 1 of 36 control subjects (chi-square 29.25, p < .0001). The authors conclude that no theory (Caplow, Komorita-Chertkoff, Walker) predicts the conservative coalitions, that a "competitive orientation" arises when rewards are trivial or oversufficient, and that Gamson's own hypothesis (revolutionary coalitions more likely when resources are seen as deserving reward) is contradicted. Era 1974 (the paper is dated August 1974; data years not stated). Medium: face-to-face. Scale: group decision. Intervention: the rule for assigning weights and the timing of pay, openly explained. Caveats: six triads per cell, male only, and this is the conference paper; a refereed version followed, Messé, Vallacher & Phillips 1975, *JPSP* 31(6): 1141-1146, doi:10.1037/h0076958 (found in validation 2026-10-30 via Crossref; closed, not read, so its sample and numbers may differ from the ERIC paper's). Its modern echo is in 4.5, much weaker.

### 4.2 Grusky, Bonacich & Webster (1995): real four-person families [read]

48 two-parent, two-child families drawn from a suburban Los Angeles junior high school (younger child 8-12, older 12-16; fathers mostly professionals or businessmen; the community's median income was $64,000; the funding grants date from 1978, data years not stated), 3-4 hour interviews with Fisher-Price dolls. A coalition is joint support in a recalled argument ("Gamson 1961b" criterion, quoted by them; their page cite, 1961b:84, does not fit 1961b's pages 565-573, see Corrections), explicitly "not ... affective cliques of mutual attraction". Power order from "who has most control over property and money": F > M > O > Y in 70%, F = M > O > Y in 16%, M > F > O > Y in 14%.
- 673 coalitions were named; a coalition response occurred in 29.2% of reported argument incidents; in 28.2% the respondent did not know of the argument.
- Distribution: Father + Mother 278 (41.3%); parent + older child 190 (28.2%); parent + younger child 129 (19.2%); the two children 76 (11.3%).
- Wins against a lone opponent: father + older child 70.8%, father + younger 68.2%, mother + older 67.2%, mother + younger 58.3% (chi-square 7.58, df 3, p = .056); against another coalition: 54.2%, 41.1%, 39.1%, 31.8% (chi-square 20.65, p = .0001). Coalitions including the father win most; those with the younger child least.
- Per family: conservative coalitions (those that keep the power order) 9.75 (SD 3.88), revolutionary 1.56 (SD 2.75), improper 2.70 (SD 1.94); conservative against revolutionary and against improper, both p < .001. Older-son families formed more conservative (11.17 against 8.33 for older-daughter families; F = 7.23, p = .01) and fewer revolutionary coalitions (0.67 against 2.46; F = 5.55, p = .023).
The authors read it as status maintenance (Bonacich, Grusky & Peyrot 1985, not read): coalitions form to preserve the power structure. This is the opposite of the lab Type 5 result and of Caplow's 1956 prediction for a triad (AB, the strong pair, never), though a four-person family is an extension (the authors adapt Caplow to tetrads) and not a triad. Grusky et al. themselves read Caplow (1968) the other way for families: for the Type 5 order "The most likely coalition is an AB coalition because this facilitates A's maintainance of control and prevents a BC coalition", and they present their data as support for Caplow's model. The conflict is with the 1956 game-style prediction, not with their reading of the 1968 book, which this file has not read (added in validation 2026-10-30) [read: Grusky et al.]. Scale: group (family). Medium: interview about face-to-face life, recalled. Era: probably late 1970s [inference from the 1978 grant]. Observational; no intervention. The authors also drop Caplow's linked-triad assumption: a partner in one triad is an opponent in another.

### 4.3 Benenson, Markovits, Emery Thompson & Wrangham (2009): a computer game and chimpanzees [read]

124 subjects (61 male, 63 female) each played 28 rounds alone against two fictional same-sex opponents labelled "friend" or "non-friend" (the label arbitrary). Strength is the probability of winning 100 points alone; the three strengths sum to 100%. Choices: compete alone; ally with one opponent (strengths add; the points are split in proportion); or take a guaranteed split by strength. Every option has the same expected payoff, so Caplow's additivity (A.3) and Gamson's proportionality are built in, and there is no bargaining. Half played under competitive instructions (money only for the top scorer), half non-competitive.
- 85.5% (106 of 124) stopped competing alone at some probability and never competed alone below it, typically at 50% or below.
- When allying, subjects chose the stronger opponent more (33.2% against 16.8% of choices) and the "friend" more (33.6% against 16.5%), additively (strong friend 42.5%, weak non-friend 9.0%). Allying with one opponent (54.6% of rounds) beat taking the guaranteed split (10.5%). No sex difference.
- Experiment 2 (26 subjects shown the screens but not playing) found that nobody recommended the rule actual players followed: the rule is neither obvious nor conscious.
The authors compare this with wild chimpanzees (Watts 1998, *Behav. Ecol. Sociobiol.* 44: 43-55: top-ranking males form dyads when their individual mating success falls below 50% and triads below 33%). Separately, citing Duffy, Wrangham & Silk 2007 (*Curr. Biol.* 17: R586-R587) and Perry 1996 (*Am. J. Primatol.* 40: 167-182), they say high-ranked individuals tend to be the most attractive coalition partners and low-ranking animals that intervene tend to support the higher-ranking contestant. (Corrected in validation 2026-10-30: this sentence credited those two claims to Watts 1998.) They note the heuristic "does not apply across primate species" (savanna baboons: low- and middle-ranked males ally against a high-ranking male, Bercovitch 1988) [primate claims read: via this paper only; the primary field studies not read]. Era: published 2009, data years and subject recruitment not stated in the text read. Medium: computer, single human against fictional opponents. Scale: membership (choose a partner). Intervention: structure and instructions; whether subjects were told the opponents were fictional is not stated. It is the clearest case where players who had fallen below the competing-alone threshold chose the stronger partner (and the "friend"), not the weaker one, a co-opting rule, not a leveling one. The design has no bargaining and no second human, so it does not test the reciprocal-pair logic that produces the weak pair in the bargaining games.

### 4.4 Wissink, van Beest, Pronk & van de Ven (2022): a preregistered replication in three populations [read; CC BY 4.0]

Studies 1 and 2 use the Online Coalition Game (oTree); Study 3 is a hypothetical scenario on Prolific in which "no actual bargaining took place" (corrected in validation 2026-10-30: this line said all three used the game). All three are simple weighted majority games in which any winning pair gets the same fixed prize, so a strong bargainer cannot win alone and the pachisi "confusion" and "conspiracy" accounts do not apply. Komorita-Meek one-step protocol: everyone makes one offer, everyone picks one offer, a matching pair forms the coalition, otherwise another round. Preregistered (AsPredicted). Data packages are open at DataverseNL (doi:10.34894/JXRELG, CC0; the DataCite record, titled "Experimental data for: Replicating the Strength-is-Weakness Effect Using the Online Coalition Game", gives submitted 21 June 2018 and available 6 July 2018, so the lab and MTurk data were probably collected by mid-2018, an inference).

- **Study 1, lab** (undergraduate psychology students in the authors' lab, Tilburg University per the repository record; 180 recruited, mean age 19.3, 142 female, 37 male, 1 other; 156 matched into 52 triads). Land sale, A 4 acres, B 3, C 2, buyer needs 5 acres, price EUR 100,000, bonus EUR 0.10 per EUR 1,000. Coalitions: BC 35 (67%), AC 15 (29%), AB 2 (4%); chi-square(2, N = 52) = 31.89, p < .001, w = 0.78.
- **Study 2, MTurk** (240 US participants in 80 triads, mean age 36.9, 119 female, 121 male; USD 0.05 per USD 1,000): BC 52 (65%), AC 22 (27.5%), AB 6 (7.5%); chi-square(2, N = 80) = 40.90, w = 0.72. Lab and MTurk do not differ (chi-square(2, N = 132) = 0.74, p = .69).
- **Study 3, Prolific** (320 participants, 160 strong and 160 weak positions; a hypothetical 4(3-2-2) municipal-party scenario, GBP 100 million; first offers and expected offers only).
- **Inclusion of the strongest bargainer, recomputed from the printed counts** [ours]: A 17 of 52 = 33%, B 71%, C 96% in Study 1; A 35%, B 72.5%, C 92.5% in Study 2. The paper prints A 19% and B 69% for Study 1 (which cannot sum to 200% with C's 96%) and "15%" for A in the Study 2 discussion (the Results say 35%). I take the counts as right; the DataverseNL package could settle it.
- **First offers carry the mechanism.** Self-allocation in first offers, Study 1: A 60.21, B 57.10, C 42.11 (F(2, 177) = 165.02, eta-squared .65); Study 2: A 61.95, B 55.21, C 41.85; Study 3: strong 61.87, weak 51.44 (d = 1.00). Each bargainer sends the first offer mostly to the weakest other (Study 1: A to C 48 against to B 10; B to C 56 against to A 5; C to B 55 against to A 6). Weak bargainers expect more from weak than from strong partners (Study 3 expected first offers: strong claim 62.29, weak 52.65; expected final offers 58.10 against 50.28). The strong are included mainly when their first offer beats B's (Study 1: included in 9 of 15 triads when A's offer was more attractive, 7 of 26 when B's was, 1 of 11 when equal; Study 2: 11 of 17, 10 of 46, 7 of 17).
- **Inside formed coalitions the strong take more, but less than proportional.** Study 1 mean share of the more-resourced member: AC 54.13 (proportional 66.7, equal 50), BC 57.06 (60.0, 50), AB 55.0 (n = 2; 57.1). Study 2: AC 54.77, BC 55.10, AB 52.5 (n = 6). Between equality and equity, closer to equality in AC.
- Authors' reading: the equity norm is both applied (strong claim more) and expected (weak avoid the strong). They also report the effect in two resource configurations (5(432) and 4(322)) and three populations.
Era: collection by mid-2018 for Studies 1-2 [inference from the DataCite submission date; Study 3's date not established], reported 2021-22. Medium: lab computers, then online synchronous with strangers (MTurk, Prolific). Scale: group decision (which pair) with membership-level offers. Intervention: structure only (assigned weights, prize rule), disclosed. They suggest, untested, informing bargainers of the consequences of using the equity norm.

### 4.5 Earned against random weights, online (Wissink et al. 2023) [read: abstract and a companion paper's summary; body not read]

Two partial sources. ### 4.6 Cantiani, van Beest, Cruijssen, Kant & Erle (2024): a probability sample of Dutch adults [read, results]

The "Transport Game": a 5(4-3-2) game (trucks carrying 4, 3 and 2 tons; a shared truck needs at least 5 tons; a EUR 9,000 premium) on the Online Coalition Game, **with the grand coalition allowed**. LISS panel (a probability sample of Dutch households, internet provided where needed); 573 players recruited, 189 triads analysed (567 players, mean age 50.8, range 16-87; 311 female, 255 male, 1 other); about 20 minutes; EUR 5 plus "∼0.55 cents per 1000 euros" as printed, though the paper's own payout range of EUR 5 to 10 on a maximum of EUR 9,000 implies about EUR 0.55 per EUR 1,000 [ours]. Rounds repeated until a coalition formed or 20 rounds passed. Fieldwork dates are not stated in what I read (received September 2023).
- Formed coalitions: AB 19 (10.0%), AC 43 (22.8%), **BC 93 (49.2%)**, ABC 34 (18.0%). Among the 155 two-person coalitions BC is 60%, AC 28%, AB 12% (w = 0.60 [ours]).
- Inclusion of the strongest bargainer in formed coalitions: A 51% (96), B 77% (146), C 90% (170); in two-person coalitions only, A in 40% [ours].

### 4.7 Mash et al. (2020): five-player weighted voting online, and a non-human member [read]

A five-player online weighted voting game: weights 1-9 summing to 13-17, quota 10, prize 100, weights public, a random proposer each round, at most three rounds. 111 second-year software-engineering undergraduates (Ben-Gurion University), 180 games and 343 proposals; payment USD 8 plus winnings. Logistic regression on power-index features predicts whether a responder accepts a proposal with a cross-validated AUC of 0.715-0.721 depending on the index (extended Deegan-Packel 0.721, Shapley 0.719, Banzhaf 0.717, Deegan-Packel 0.717, least core 0.715; "always accept" 0.5). Proposers often asked for shares not matched to responders' power and proposed coalitions larger than needed. In 594 further games (107 more subjects) with four humans and one undisclosed software player, agents built on the extended Deegan-Packel and Shapley predictions out-earned human proposers (Mann-Whitney p near 0 and .033; the Banzhaf agent did not differ) by asking for more. In a separate 20-subject comparison a subgame-perfect-equilibrium agent did worse than humans (21% of its proposals formed a coalition against 67%; it asked for 56% of the prize against 32%). Humans were told they might face computer agents. Scale: membership (accept or reject a proposal) and group decision. Medium: online. Era: collected before March 2019; the first version appeared in 2017 [inference]. Data are said to be public (a shortened link; not checked). Power indices predict acceptance only modestly, and the non-human member exploited the structure.

### 4.8 Two items I could use only lightly

- Funaki, Sol & Willinger (2025/26), a lab three-person symmetric coalition game with an empty core and random re-matching: over 86% of realised coalitions are two-person, equal splits dominate and rise over time [abstract only, via IDEAS; closed]. It is a Type 1-style game, a modern lab datum for "any pair, equal split".
- Mouginot, Garfield & Glowacki (2026): focus groups in three subsistence communities in southwest Ethiopia (Kwegu, Kara, Nyangatom), then structured interviews in one Kwegu community (60 men, 82 women; data June 2024 to August 2025). ### 4.9 Two models that bear on the form of the sign rule [read]

- **Gavrilets, Duenez-Guzman & Vose (2008).** Group of N with fighting abilities s_i and signed affinities x_ij. Each individual enters a conflict at rate alpha; each other member notices it with probability omega and helps side i with probability h = [1 + ((1 - beta)/beta) exp(-eta x)]^(-1), so help is beta at zero affinity. A coalition of n with mean ability s has strength s n squared (Lanchester square law) and beats a rival with probability S_I / (S_I + S_J). After each conflict affinities change: winners among themselves by delta_ww, losers by delta_ll, winner to loser by delta_wl, loser to winner by delta_lw; all affinities decay toward 0 at rate mu. Defaults: alpha 1, beta 0.05, delta_ww 1, delta_ll 0.5, delta_wl = delta_lw = -0.5, eta 0.5, omega 0.5, mu 0.05. Result: alliances (pairs whose mutual help exceeds baseline by 50%) appear in a phase-transition-like jump as N, omega, beta or eta rise or mu falls; within an alliance social success is only weakly related to fighting ability. A simulation, no data. It is a sign-generator from conflict participation (signs from who helped whom), not from relative power, and the abstract's own premise is that "ape societies remain essentially hierarchical, and coalitions rarely weaken social inequality".
- **The review's cardinal point** (section 1.5): in contest models the predicted coalition type flips with the contest success function and with the variance of strengths, so an ordinal type table cannot be the whole law.

## 5. What changes with group size (n)

- n = 3 is Caplow's domain. Shenoy's restatement and Gamson's theory extend to n-person proper weighted majority games; the review says "analytical models in CAFT are still overwhelmingly triadic" and that two-against-one is the coalition structure most commonly observed in nature (Bercovitch 1988; Bissonnette et al. 2009b) [read: via the review; not checked].
- **Tetrads and larger, board and politics games** [read: author's summary, final report]. Shears (1965, tetrads, five power patterns): a marked tendency for strong winning coalitions rather than weak triple coalitions, "an important difference between triads and larger groups"; weaker members, when included, got more than a proportional share. Vinacke's politics game (1968; groups of 3 to 9, 49 male and 56 female groups, five power patterns): coalitions and deals vary with size first and power pattern second; beyond three there is a shift toward coalitions that include the strongest member, especially in male groups; the weakest members in coalitions get more than their proportional share and the strongest less; male groups become more exploitative as size grows.
- **Families (n = 4)**: parents unite, conservative coalitions dominate (section 4.2). Willis's tetrad extension got "limited support".
- **n = 5 online**: power-index features predict acceptance with AUC about 0.72 (section 4.7).
- **Models**: alliance emergence is threshold-like in N (Gavrilets et al. 2008).
- Net: the weak-pair result is an n = 3, equal-prize, all-pairs-win result. At n of 4 or more the data I read move toward coalitions that include the strongest, and no source gives a size at which the switch happens. The "size principle" (minimal winning coalitions at larger n) is a separate literature I did not open.

## 6. What changes with composition and definition

- **Sex**: changes whether a coalition forms and the split (female triads: more triple alliances, more 50/50 deals, fewer coalitions), not the weak-pair preference when a pair forms (TR 3 Table 4, no significant difference; 1950s-60s). Not tested in the modern replications, whose samples were 79% female (lab), 50% (MTurk) and 55% (panel); the weak-pair share was about 60-67% throughout.
- **Age**: children ignore weights and avoid coalitions; males become steadily more exploitative from 7-8 through 14-16 to college (TR 3, 1962-63).
- **Culture**: Micronesian male triads ranged from accommodative to exploitative; friend triads of Japanese-American students differed little from strangers [read: author's summary]. Modern non-WEIRD data are interviews, not triads (Mouginot et al.).
- **Legitimacy of weights**: earned weights flipped the 1974 lab result (6 of 6 triads allied the two strongest); in the online era earning weights, or linking them to payoffs, weakened the effect (three studies, 915 triads, per the paper's abstract, read in validation 2026-10-30; in the project the Behavior Research Methods paper summarises, the strongest was included 67.3% of the time against 57.6%, not significant).
- **External rival**: a competing triad turns coalitions into consensus (TR 4).
- **Persistence and role**: in families the strong unite and the parent-child pairs win; the coalition measure is joint support in recalled arguments, not a game agreement.
- **Dispositions**: motivation to win and achievement go with fewer weak coalitions and more initiating of offers (1960s, small effects as summarised); perspective-taking raises inclusion modestly (OR 1.47, R-squared .03; 2020s).
- **Definitions that change base rates**: the lab coalition is a binding agreement to split a prize (nearly every game); the family coalition is joint support in an argument (29% of incidents); Benenson's is "two or more individuals acting cooperatively to compete against a third party" (after Harcourt & de Waal 1992); the review's ethology definition is joint aggression against a common target. "Strength" is an assigned weight (lab), perceived control over money and property (family), a probability of winning (Benenson), or influence, respect and wealth (Mouginot). The same word covers four different operands.

## 8. Operand/operator translation for the algebra

- **Operands.** A person with a scalar strength a_i (assigned weight, perceived control, probability of winning, fighting ability: four different referents, section 6); a triad (or n-group) with a quota q and a fixed divisible prize; a coalition structure (I, AB, AC, BC, ABC). Persons' claims c_i, proportional to strength (Gamson) or compromised between equality and equity (observed shares above), are a derived operand that the replications say does the work.
- **Operators.** (a) Coalition formation: (strength vector, quota) to a distribution over structures. Caplow gives a support by type; Gamson the cheapest winning pair; Walker probabilities; the equity-bargaining account first offers biased toward the weakest partner. (b) Allocation: payoffs within the coalition (observed between 50% and the proportional share). Equations are in section 1.4 (E1 to E5) and in 4.9.
- **Where it plugs into v0.1, as ours** [ours]. v0.1 takes sgn(a_ij) as an input with no derivation (section 2.2; section 8, item 1) and reads the block partition Pi_T off the two-colouring of sgn A(T) where the graph is balanced (composition law 5.1). A coalition structure on three persons is a block partition: AB|C is the blocks {A, B} and {C}. Caplow, Gamson and the equity account therefore give Pi_T directly from the strengths, without signs. A sign pattern consistent with it (positive inside blocks, negative across) would then be a derived input for (4.2b): AB|C to (+AB, -AC, -BC), an even number of negative edges and so balanced; ABC to (+,+,+); no coalition (I) to no ties, not to (-,-,-). Structural balance treats the three two-negative triangles as equally balanced and cannot say which occurs; the strength rule picks the positive edge. In (5.1)'s vocabulary the isolate is the singleton-block member with two negative ties, v0.1's "leaver candidate"; none of the sources follows the isolate after the game, so whether exclusion predicts leaving is untested.
- **Why the mapping is weak.** (1) v0.1's sgn(a_ij) is the sign of *influence*; a coalition here is a *joint-action* agreement to split a prize for the length of one game, and the negative edges are exclusions, not dislike. Grusky et al. insist a coalition is not an affective tie; an arbitrary "friend" label added strongly to partner choice in Benenson's game (33.6% against 16.5%), while triads of friends in the Hawaii programme differed little from strangers (Iwakai's 1960 thesis, summary only, Japanese-American students): the link to affect is not established either way. (2) The generator is conditional: it needs public strengths, an equal prize for any winning pair, no external rival, no persisting structure to defend, n = 3, and weights not seen as earned. (3) The ordinal types lose the magnitude information the review says matters.
- **What it changes for v0.1.** Nothing here contradicts (4.2b) or (5.1). For n = 3 with public strengths it offers an independent generator for Pi_T and, through the mapping above, for the sign pattern, where v0.1 now has an input; v0.1's section 8, item 1 (sgn as an input) stays open because the identification of "joined against" with "influences positively" is ours. For any group that persists or competes with another, the sources say the strong-and-weak pairing is reversed or erased, so a generator would need a validity switch.
- **Candidate editable soft settings for v0.2, with the validity domain attached** [ours]: weak-pair share of two-person coalitions in a 4-3-2 triad (0.60 to 0.67, observed 1950s to 2020s; both ends are set by directly read studies, 0.60 Cantiani et al. and 0.67 Wissink et al. lab, so the 1950s value, which is only [read: via Shenoy], is not needed to fix the range); inclusion of the strongest in two-person coalitions (0.33 to 0.40); claim exponent between equality and equity (observed shares of the strongest 52-59%, proportional would be 57-67%); domain: n = 3, public weights, unearned, no external rival, one-shot or short-run; switches that turn the rule off or reverse it: rival group present, weights earned, persisting family or organisation, n of 4 or more. Form-level statements are era-invariant given their hypotheses (Shenoy's theorem that proportional claims select the cheapest winning pair); the magnitudes are era-, medium- and population-bound and are to be re-estimated.

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

Partly, and the part that reduces is not the part that does the predicting.

**What reduces to drives of persons or groups.**
- *Control and payoff.* Caplow's A.2 (each member wants more control over others) and Gamson's payoff maximisation are generic monotone preferences, the closest thing to a power or reward drive. They are not curiosity, fear or desire, and they are the same for all three players; they cannot by themselves select a pair.
- *Competitive against accommodative orientation.* The Hawaii programme's exploitative and accommodative strategies, achievement and nurturance, and motivation to win are person-level parameters that change whether a coalition forms (triple alliances, no coalition, 50/50 deals) and, per the questionnaire work, somewhat which pair. Sex, age and culture shift them. They behave as drive parameters with a developmental trajectory (males, from 7-8 years to college), measurable with a questionnaire.
- *Group-level motives that override the power rule.* An outside rival makes consensus the norm (TR 4); status maintenance and family solidarity make the strong unite (Grusky et al.). These are group drives in the algebra's sense, and they are the largest effects in the tradition: they erase or reverse the weak-pair result.
- *Risk tolerance.* 85% of Benenson's subjects stopped competing alone at a threshold probability of winning near 50%, a rule neither obvious nor conscious to other subjects; it reads like a drive-like heuristic.

**What does not reduce.**
- *The structure.* Weights, the quota and the winning set are properties of the game, not of any person's drives. In the simple-majority replications the weights confer no extra winning chances ("resources are power-irrelevant"), so what the weight does is create a claim.
- *The equity norm and expectations about others' claims.* The best-supported mechanism is a distributive-justice norm applied by the strong and anticipated by the weak (Wissink et al.). That is a social norm plus a second-order belief, not a first-order drive; perspective-taking helps only a little (R-squared .03).
- *Legitimacy.* Whether weights are seen as earned reverses the result in 1974 and nudges it in 2020; that is a norm about entitlement, again not a drive.
- *The selection among feasible pairs.* Given payoff maximisation and an equity expectation, the weak pair follows by arithmetic from the weights; the drives supply the motive and the structure supplies the choice.
- *The sign.* A coalition is a contract, not an affect; nothing in the tradition derives liking or dislike from a drive.

**Verdict on the hypothesis under test.** "Drives as primitives" gets weak support here: the sign pattern in a triad is a function of structure, a fairness norm and expectations, with drive-like parameters (competition against accommodation, group-level motives) setting whether and how strongly the rule applies. If the algebra wants this tradition, its primitives must include a strength or claim operand and a norm operand beside the drives.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**The outcome it predicts.** Which pair, if any, forms a binding agreement in a three-person bargaining game with public strengths, a fixed prize and equal pay for any winning pair, and (Gamson, Komorita-Chertkoff) roughly how the prize is split.

**How well, in sample.** Type by type, Caplow's predicted sets contain 67-89% of games in the 1950s data (Type 2: 71%; Type 5: 88% for AC or BC, 66% for BC alone; Types 4 and 6, "no coalition", 69% and 67%; Type 1, any pair, 89%), against a flat four-way baseline of 25-75% depending on the set width. The Type 5 weak-pair share is 67% of coalitions, w = 0.73 [ours]. Predictions are sets, not frequencies; the later refinements (Chertkoff, Walker, Gamson) sharpen Type 5 to BC 58-100%.

**Out of sample.** The weak-pair share in 4-3-2 reappeared after 60+ years: 67% (35 of 52, lab), 65% (52 of 80, MTurk), 60% of two-person coalitions (93 of 155, probability sample), w = 0.78, 0.72, 0.60, in a different game form (a simple majority game, where the strong cannot win alone), with small real stakes, in the lab and online, with strangers of both sexes. That is strong evidence for the central frequency and for the equity mechanism. A first-offer rule (the strong claim 60 against 42 of 100) and a payoff pattern (the better-resourced member takes 52-59%, below proportional) also repeat.

**Failures and limits.**
- The strong pair AB, which Caplow's 1956 text never predicts for Type 5, is 10% of V&A's coalitions, 4% and 7.5% in the lab and MTurk replications and 12% of two-person coalitions in the Dutch panel; it is the dominant coalition in families (parents 41% of coalitions; 9.75 conservative against 1.56 revolutionary per family) and under earned weights (6 of 6 triads in 1974).
- An outside rival erases it (consensus; weights ignored). Children and female triads avoid coalitions or form triple alliances. At n of 4 or more the data move toward including the strongest.
- Type 1 is symmetric but unequal (33, 17, 30), unexplained.
- "No coalition" in Types 4 and 6 holds about two thirds of the time.
- The theory is ordinal and verbal; contest models say the predicted type flips with the contest function and the variance of strengths.
- Individual-level prediction is weak: power-index features give AUC 0.72 for acceptance in a five-player game; perspective-taking explains R-squared .03 of inclusion. The strongest evidence is aggregate and type-level, which matches the survey's standing warning that individual-level prediction has a poor record and aggregate claims have done better (`notes/2026-10-29-drives-as-the-core.md`).
- The weights are imposed; no source tests whether real groups have measurable strengths of this kind, apart from the family study's self-reported control over money.

**What it steers.** Only the structure, by the experimenter or the institution designer: assigning weights, the quota and the pay rule, openly. The consent-based interventions tried did not work: telling players the rational strategy did not shift outcomes (1960s); informing about equity is proposed and untested; making weights earned works strongly in 1974 (n = 6 per cell) and more weakly in the online era (not significant in the project the Behavior Research Methods paper summarises; across the three-study paper the abstract reports the strongest included more often when resources were earned or linked to payoff, but the body is unread). The one lever that works in the data is changing the group's situation (an outside rival), not the person's drive; Vinacke reads it as intra-group cooperation under inter-group competition. Nothing here steers affect.
