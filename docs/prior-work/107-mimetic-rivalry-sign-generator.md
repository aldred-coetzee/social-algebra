## 1. Result in brief

1. **The premise fails: Sack 2021 cannot be read.** The fragments that could be seen describe a desire-copying fixed point ("each node's object of desire matches its mediator's") with a convergence theorem. No negative tie is visible in them. Its reference list cites Heider (1946) and Cartwright & Harary (1956), the two sources v0.1's balance theorem rests on, so signs may enter; the fragments cannot say how. *Validation:* Sack's own earlier NetLogo model (2012, open, read; section 2) is a structural-balance model of friend and enemy ties in which the share of enmity ties is a user input; there, as in v0.1, the sign is an input repaired by balance, not generated by desire. File 18's label "an appendix gives formal definitions and theorems [from abstract]" is not confirmed by what I could obtain.
2. **No source I could read derives a tie sign from shared desire by an equation that has also been tested.** What exists: a verbal law (Hobbes 1651) conditional on the object being unshareable; one field experiment on groups (Sherif 1954, 22 boys, covert manipulation); one observational signature in a 17-man group (Newcomb, via 104, confounded with popularity); and two untested payoff games in which hostility or signs follow from payoff rules (d'Artigues & Vignolo 2003: deliberate harm to the leader; Hiller 2017: signs from strength differences).
3. **The copying step is real and small.** Mimetic desire, defined as a rise in a rating of an object once it is seen as the goal of another's action, has d = 0.46 within-subject in each of two groups (n = 20 and 19), mean 0.08 z-units, although the 60 stimuli were chosen for their large original effect. *Corrected in validation:* the "0.18 in the original" that the replication cites is the largest of Lebreton et al.'s **five** samples (the fMRI group, n = 19). The five samples (n = 116) gave 0.12 to 0.18 z, each p < .05 [read]. So the replication's 0.08 is 45% of that one sample and about 59% of the n-weighted five-sample mean of 0.138. Its d = 0.46 sits inside the original range of about 0.38 to 0.98, computed from the reported paired t values [derived]. No rivalry was measured in either paper.
4. **The two halves of Girard's chain (copy a desire; copying makes a rival) have not been joined in any test I could find.** Hobbes and Sherif make the object's real unshareability the cause (and Asplund, via Grande, faults Girard for forgetting "conflicts of real worth"); Girard, in the sentences Grande quotes, makes imitation the cause and gives the object less and less weight. The evidence read supports unshareability as sufficient and says nothing for imitation without it.
5. **"Both want X" has opposite predicted signs in different sources** (section 8). A single signed weight a_ij cannot carry both: mimetic rivals have a positive influence weight on each other's desires and a negative affect weight. v0.1 has no Object sort, so excludability cannot even be written down. [derived]
6. **The all-against-one configuration does not identify Girard's mechanism.** Hiller's strong Nash equilibria are all n-1 agents against one, with no imitation of desire. (*Corrected in validation:* the first pass said "coalition-proof", which is a weaker concept. Hiller's result is for strong Nash equilibrium: robust to a deviation by any coalition, for homogeneous agents, and possibly non-existent.) Paes's model expels a marked agent, marked preferentially for having few ties, from accumulated tension with no desire and no rivalry.
7. **Size.** The harm in d'Artigues & Vignolo needs a_j - lambda_j < mu/(n-1), harder as n grows; Paes's expulsion comes sooner with 30 agents than with 60 (median step 190 against 546.5); Hiller's multi-clique equilibria need geometrically growing clique sizes. Girard's minimal case is the triangle.
8. **Era.** Every sign-law result I could read on people is face-to-face and from 1954 or 1956; the copying result is a lab video task (data collected by 2011 and by mid-2016, published 2012 and 2016); the models are theory. Nothing *read here* tests the sign law online. The validation pass could not re-run a web search (the shared search budget was spent). Unread candidates that touch adjacent links online are: Ku, Malhotra & Murnighan (2005), on competitive arousal in live and Internet auctions, which bears on escalation and not on the sign; the online pile-on cases on Paes's project site, which bear on expulsion; and the signed online datasets in 117.

- **Sack's own earlier model (added in validation) [read].** Crossref gives the 2014 entry's full title as "Character networks for narrative generation: structural balance theory and the emergence of proto-narratives" (doi:10.1201/b16877-7, pp. 81-104). In Hobbes's and Sherif's terms that is exactly the configuration that breeds enmity if the object is unshareable, and in similarity-attraction terms it is a configuration that breeds liking. Which of the two the model assigns it, or whether it assigns neither, is the whole question for D599 and the fragments do not settle it.

## 3. Girard (the source theory) without the books

**Bibliography.** See the table in section 0. The sentences below are Grande's quotations of Girard's English editions, with Grande's page numbers (*Things Hidden*, Athlone 1987; *Violence and the Sacred*, JHU 5th ed. 1986). They are [quoted in Grande].

- **The rivalry law as Girard states it [quoted in Grande; Things Hidden 413].** The subject "automatically transforms the model's desire into a desire that opposes and frustrates his own", and "because he does not understand the automatic character of the rivalry" the imitator "converts the fact of being opposed, frustrated and rejected into the major stimulant of his desire." So: rivalry follows from imitation *automatically*, and the rivalry then raises the desire. No scarcity condition appears in that sentence.
- **Competition versus rivalry [Grande's footnote 26, citing Things Hidden 11].** Competitors run or walk together; rivals dwell on opposite banks of the same river; small changes in the mimetic game turn competitors into rivals. That is a *distance-and-opposition* criterion for the sign, not a scarcity criterion.
- **Distance as the sign parameter [standard account; Grande states only half of it].** The usual summary (seen in encyclopedia-level secondary pieces, not read by me) is that external mediation (the model far off in time, rank or space) gives imitation without rivalry and internal mediation (the model near) gives rivalry. What Grande says is that the first book moves from "external and transparent imitation, to an internal and hidden imitation" that is "more fundamental and possessive and much more intense", and that rivalry is "loose and external, governed by prohibitions" in the early stage (his footnote 34). The mediator is later renamed "model" (his footnote 17).
- **Escalation and the obstacle [Things Hidden 414-415, quoted in Grande].** The dynamism of mimetic desire "has always been oriented towards death and madness"; mimetic desire "turns increasingly towards the obstacle." The object fades and "there are no objects left to desire, there is only rivalry" (Grande's gloss).
- **Modernity as a time-varying parameter [Things Hidden 93, quoted in Grande].** Modern society "can permit and encourage growth of mimetic rivalries that are normally forbidden to man." This is Girard's own datedness claim: the share of internal mediation rises as prohibitions and social distance collapse. It is untested in anything I read.
- **Definition of desire [Things Hidden 287; Violence and the Sacred 147, quoted in Grande].** "Desire can be defined as a process of mimesis involving undifferentiation; it is akin to the process of deepening conflict that issues in the mechanism of reunification through the victim." And: "Once his basic needs are satisfied, man is subject to intense desires." Grande's own summary is that in mimetic theory desire "is seen as something distinct from instincts", and he reports that in the late 1990s Girard was dissatisfied with the word "desire" and floated "drive", "élan vital" and "project" (his footnote 13).
- **Critics inside the tradition.** Asplund (1989, *Rivaler och syndabockar*, via Grande) says Girard forgets conflicts over real worth, i.e. objective scarcity. Grande agrees in part: the tendency from the 1961 book to Things Hidden "has been to endow the object with less and less value", and he proposes that desire is also object-related when conflict is not intense. Grande's remark that a triangle is the number at which someone is certain to be left out, and that in a group of four the chance falls, is Grande's, not Girard's.
- **Operators implicit in the claims [sa]:** `copy(subject <- model, object)`; `oppose` (automatic once subject and model are near); `escalate` (rivalry feeds desire); `contagion` (rivalries spread to third parties, with undifferentiation); `expel(victim)` (all against one, unanimity). No equation is given by Girard in anything quoted.
- **Empirical standing.** None of these claims is tested by Girard. 17 found no experimental test; I found two partial operationalisations (section 7.5) and none of the distance claim.

## 4. Hobbes (1651): the sign law in its original, conditional form [read]

All from *Leviathan*, Gutenberg #3207. No equation; the law is verbal.

- **Ch. XIII.** "if any two men desire the same thing, which neverthelesse they cannot both enjoy, they become enemies; and in the way to their End ... endeavour to destroy, or subdue one an other." The preceding step is "equality of hope in the attaining of our Ends", from equality of ability. The consequence is "Diffidence", then anticipation, then war "of every man, against every man" while "men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe." Three "principall causes of quarrel": Competition (for Gain), Diffidence (for Safety), Glory (for Reputation, set off by "any signe of undervalue").
- **Ch. XI.** "Competition of Riches, Honour, command, or other power, enclineth to Contention, Enmity, and War: because the way of one Competitor, to the attaining of his desire, is to kill, subdue, supplant, or repell the other." Also: "competition of praise, enclineth to a reverence of Antiquity. For men contend with the living, not with the dead". *Corrected in validation:* the sentence goes on, "to these ascribing more than due, that they may obscure the glory of the other". So the dead are over-praised as a move against the living competitor. Distance (here, death) removes the contest with the distant figure, which parallels Girard's external mediation, but the reverence is a weapon in the rivalry with the near one, not a sign flipped by distance [derived]. The first pass read it as the flip itself.
- **Ch. VI.** One grief, two outputs: "Griefe, for the success of a Competitor ... if it be joyned with Endeavour to enforce our own abilities to equal or exceed him, is called EMULATION: but joyned with Endeavour to supplant or hinder a Competitor, ENVIE." The sign-deciding variable is the direction of endeavour, not the desire.
- **Conditions the law needs [derived reading of the above]:** (a) the same object; (b) the object is not jointly enjoyable; (c) parity, so neither is sure to win; (d) no third power that makes both safe. Change (b) or (d) and the sign changes.
- **Empirical standing.** A philosophical premise, not a measurement. Its strongest later test at group scale is Sherif (section 5). The emulation/envy branch is, by its title, the subject of the benign/malicious-envy work (van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters 2009, "Leveling up and down: the experiences of benign and malicious envy", *Emotion* 9(3): 419-429, doi:10.1037/a0015669). It is closed and not read, so whether it tests the branch is unconfirmed.

## 5. Sherif et al. (1954/1961): the Hobbes sign law at group scale [read]

- **Design and sample.** Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma. Groups arrived 19 and 20 June 1954; a three-week camp. 22 boys, about 11 years old (median 11 y 1 m), from stable Protestant, middle-class, two-parent families, none known to the others, selected so that differences in socio-economic, ethnic, religious or family background and unusual individual frustration could not explain the outcome (ch. 8 pp. 205-206, ch. 3). Split into two matched groups of 11 (Rattlers, Eagles). Stage 1: group formation, no contact. Stage 2: a tournament with a trophy, medals and four-bladed knives for the winners, plus staged frustrations. Stage 3: contact without interdependence (shared meals, a film, fireworks), then superordinate goals.
- **Their law (ch. 2 p. 46, ch. 8 p. 208).** Hostile intergroup attitudes arise "when the groups competed for goals which could be attained by only one group"; the "limiting condition" is "the nature of functional relations between" the groups. Positive interdependence reverses it: superordinate goals are those "whose attainment is beyond the resources and efforts of any one group alone" (ch. 8 p. 202).
- **Stage 2 results (end of Stage 2).** Friendship choices, on an item where out-group choices were "forced somewhat" (ch. 6 pp. 118-119, Table 1), went to the in-group 93.6% (73 of 78) for Rattlers and 92.5% (62 of 67) for Eagles. Stereotype ratings of the out-group (ch. 6 pp. 137-138, Table 3; *page corrected in validation*): Rattlers rated Eagles 53.0% unfavourable. Eagles rated Rattlers 76.9% unfavourable and 15.4% favourable. *Source inconsistency found in validation:* the text gives the Rattlers' favourable share as 24.9%, but Table 3's own categories give 19.9 + 15.0 = 34.9%. With 24.9 the five categories would sum to 90%; with 34.9 they sum to 100%. So the 24.9 is probably a misprint, either in the 1961 text or in the psychclassics transcription; which one is unconfirmed [derived]. In-group solidarity rose (ch. 8 p. 207).
- **Stage 3 results (ch. 7 pp. 187-192).** After superordinate goals, out-group friendship choices rose from 6.4% to 36.4% (Rattlers) and from 7.5% to 23.2% (Eagles); rejections that fell on the out-group dropped from 75% to 15% (Rattlers) and from 95% to 47.1% (Eagles); unfavourable ratings of the out-group fell from 53% to 4.5% (Rattlers) and from 76.9% to 22.6% (Eagles). Contact without interdependence did not reduce friction and was used for further hostility (ch. 8 pp. 208-209).
- **Consent or manipulation.** **Manipulation, covert.** Stage 2 and 3 situations were to be "designed in such a way that the subjects cannot assign the source of these conditions to the staff" (ch. 2 p. 45); staff posed as counsellors; the boys "were not aware that each step was especially designed" (ch. 8 p. 203). Parents gave permission after an explanation of the study's purpose (ch. 3). *Validation, ch. 3 pp. 61-63:* this was the same announced purpose given to principals and teachers: a study of interaction within and between teams, including competition with another group, winning and losing, and frustration. Parents were told visits were barred.
- **Scale.** Group (inter-group hostility rising, in-group cohesion rising), measured through member-level choices and ratings.
- **What it cannot separate.** (1) Competition from the mere existence of two groups: there is no pre-competition cross-group baseline, since the groups had not met. (2) Imitation of desire from imposed desire: the staff made the prize attractive to both groups, so no desire was acquired from the other group. (3) Frustration from competition. The authors' own text records that the 1953 attempt "was terminated as an experiment owing to various difficulties and unfavorable conditions, including errors of judgment in the direction of the experiment" (ch. 1 p. 18) and that a 1949 study had "positive results" (ch. 2 p. 45).
- **Replication.** Tyerman & Spencer (1983, *Small Group Behavior* 14(4): 515-531, doi:10.1177/104649648301400407, Crossref-confirmed) per file 18 [abs]: with scouts who already knew each other, in-group solidarity fell under competition while other hypotheses were largely supported. Not read. Later criticism of the design (Perry 2018) is not read.
- **Group size and composition.** Two groups of 11; homogeneous by selection; no prior acquaintance. The law is stated at the level of groups and says nothing for n = 2 or for crowds.

## 6. The Newcomb fraternity signature of "competition for k" [104]

Not re-read. 104 (validated) reports that in the 17-man, 15-wave fall-1956 data, Doreian & Krackhardt (2001, doi:10.1080/0022250X.2001.9990244) find that when i dislikes j and j likes k, the completion "i likes k" is *over*-represented against a row-shuffle null from about week 6 (type DLL) and "i dislikes k" is under-represented from week 5 (type DLD). They read this as a competition mechanism (i and j are rivals for k's attention) and cannot separate it from a popularity mechanism (k is liked by everyone); actor attributes are lost, and signs are a recoding of ranks. Era 1956; medium face-to-face housing; scale membership/group; n = 17; composition strangers, all men. It is the only field-data signature of rivalry as a sign generator the repository holds, and it is confounded.

## 7. Formal and empirical works read

### 7.1 d'Artigues & Vignolo (2003): imitation of the leader's payoff produces harm to the leader [read in full]

- **Vocabulary.** "Mimetic rivalry" is the frustration of a desire to obtain what a leading country has; the behaviour that results is "the deliberate choice of a strategy degrading the situation of the leading country." Authors' framing is terrorism; the formal object is a 2x2 coordination game.
- **Operands.** n >= 2 asymmetric countries; strategy E (integrate) or A (autarky); payoffs a_i for mutual E, lambda_i for A, 0 for E against A, with 0 < lambda_i < a_i.
- **Equations (restated).** Average payoffs with z countries playing E: u_i(E, z) = ((z-1)/(n-1)) a_i and u_i(A, z) = lambda_i. Selection rule (3): each country that gets a revision opportunity (probability theta) picks the strategy minimising mu - u_i(s, z), where mu is the largest payoff realised ("the leader's"), plus mutation with small probability epsilon, giving a Markov chain on {0..n} analysed by the Kandori-Mailath-Rob / Young long-run-equilibrium method. The text writes mu as a fixed observed quantity but the proof of Proposition 2 uses the post-switch value of the leader's payoff; the paper does not reconcile the two.
- **Theorems.** Proposition 1: with symmetric countries and lambda < a/2, the long-run equilibrium is everyone integrating. Proposition 2: in an asymmetric world, a country j for which A is risk-dominant and a_j - lambda_j < mu/(n-1) switches to A from the all-E state. Proof idea [read]: j's switch lowers the leader's payoff by mu/(n-1), and j prefers it when its own loss from leaving trade is smaller than that.
- **Group size and composition.** The condition has 1/(n-1) in it: the larger the population, the less one defector can reduce the gap to the leader, so the rivalry behaviour is "hard to fulfil" unless mu is large (the authors' remark). With symmetric countries and lambda < a/2 the long-run equilibrium is full integration (Proposition 1); the authors say the result holds for low asymmetries. Larger cross-country differentials produce more defectors and, at the extreme, two blocs. (*Validation:* this last claim is stated informally after Proposition 2 and not proved.)
- **Standing.** Theory only; no data; a single paper (cited 5 times per OpenAlex), no replication. It is the only formal model I found in which *imitation of a leader* produces a negative act toward that leader, but the negative act is an externality, not a tie, and "deliberate" holds by construction of the rule. Missed by 17 and 18, which list three formal models of mimetic theory; this makes at least four. *Credit, checked in validation:* the paper credits its rule (3) to a variation of Smallwood & Conlisk's satisficing dynamics (1979, *QJE* 93(1): 1-23, doi:10.2307/1882595, Crossref-confirmed; the paper misspells the second author "Consisk"). It takes the long-run method from Kandori, Mailath & Rob (1993) and Young (1993). Its only Girard citation is "Eating disorders and mimetic desire" (*Contagion* 3, 1996), which is not about terrorism. The terrorism reading of Girard in its footnote 3 carries no citation.
- **Era, medium, scale.** Written 2003; abstract game; scale group (countries), outcome split into two blocs.

### 7.2 Hiller (2017): signs from payoffs, and all-against-one as the strong-Nash outcome [read, main text]

- **Vocabulary.** Friends (reciprocated positive link) and enemies (any negative link); strength; bullying; payoff extraction; structural balance.
- **Operands.** n >= 3 agents; each extends +1 or -1 to every other agent; the undirected link is positive iff both directions are positive; strength of i is its own intrinsic strength plus that of its positive friends (1 + number of friends for homogeneous agents).
- **Payoff (restated).** i's utility is the sum over its enemies j of f(n_i, n_j), minus a cost eps per negative link it extends, minus a cost kappa per negative link it is party to. f is increasing in own strength, decreasing in the enemy's, f(n_i, n_j) + f(n_j, n_i) = 0, so f = 0 between equals; the paper's examples are the ratio and logistic contest success functions normalised by 1/2. Positive links pay nothing directly; their value is added strength and avoided conflict.
- **Theorems.** In any pure Nash equilibrium no negative link is reciprocated and the stronger agent extends the negative link; agents of equal strength are friends and agents of unequal strength are enemies (Proposition 1; the PDF text drops the not-equal sign, read from the paper's own gloss). Every equilibrium is weakly structurally balanced: all friends, or cliques of friends with enemies across. All-friends and (n-1 agents against one) are always equilibria for homogeneous agents (Proposition 2; for heterogeneous agents only an informal sketch is given). For sharp contests (delta small), multi-clique equilibria exist only if each clique is larger than all smaller ones combined, so sizes grow at least geometrically with ratio 2 (Proposition 3); for smooth contests there are at most two cliques (Proposition 4). **Any strong Nash equilibrium has two maximal cliques and a single agent as everyone's enemy** (Proposition 5; it may not exist, Examples 1-2). With a constant extraction value, for any n >= 4 there is a group size n' with |n' - n| <= 2 at which an unbalanced equilibrium exists (Proposition 6; the construction uses n = 2 + 4k). *Corrected in validation* from "exist for n >= 4". Propositions 3-7 are all stated for homogeneous agents. Nash equilibria cannot be Pareto ranked (Proposition 7). All-friends maximises total payoff.
- **Translation for D599.** There is no shared desire here; the object both parties contest is the extractable surplus between them, which is zero when they are equal. The model is nonetheless the one in this file that *derives signs from a payoff rule with a stated equilibrium concept*, and it reproduces the scapegoat configuration (n-1 against 1) without imitation, crisis or unanimity of belief. So that configuration alone does not identify Girard's mechanism. (Hiller's proofs do use "imitation" deviations, in which one agent copies another's linking strategy. That is a proof device about strategies, not copied desire.)
- **Empirical standing.** Theory. The paper cites Huitsing et al. (2012) as finding balance in school bullying relations (like and bullying, not general like and dislike) and Doreian & Krackhardt (2001) as showing movement toward balance; neither read here. The model's own predictions are untested.
- **Group size and composition.** n >= 3; homogeneous strengths give the clique-size rules above; heterogeneous strengths give equilibria by intrinsic strength (informal sketch only, proofs "upon request").

### 7.3 Antunes, Nunes & Coelho (2014) [read in full]

A four-page BDI-agent position paper proposing that desires be acquired by a mimetic mechanism (envy, imitation, competition) alongside intrinsic motivation, to be implemented with Cartesian genetic programming; **no equations and no reported results** (confirms 17). *Corrected in validation* from "no simulation": section 5 mentions "a preliminary exploration" in partially observable fitness landscapes, with one qualitative observation (contextual information is decisive for adaptation), but gives no method, numbers or figures. Its useful content is two passages on p. 1170. The first is a block quotation of Dupuy (2011, the paper's ref. [6]) that states the Girardian sign law in one line: desire "may not only cause rivalry - my mediator automatically becomes my rival since we desire the same object -, but also depend on it - to the point that without rivalry, desire itself threatens to languish." The second, from their section 3, has *both* signs at once. (*Validation:* the empathy-and-rivalry paragraph carries no citation. The "not acknowledged" sentence opens the next paragraph, on double mediation, which ends by citing Dupuy [6], so it may paraphrase him.) Between subject and mediator "there is empathy ... since they share a passion for the same object", which "does not go without some rivalry, because often the object can be obtained by only one of the coveters", and the rivalry "is not acknowledged by either actor." Operators proposed in the paper's own terms (section 5): an imitation mechanism that steers an agent toward a mediator, a competition mechanism in which one agent tries to overcome its mediator, and criteria for choosing between imitation, competition and cooperation. Standing: design paper, no test.

### 7.4 Paes (2025): the scapegoat stage as a tension-discharge model [ODD and page read; code in part]

- **What it models.** A 30-100 agent small-world network; agents carry an integer tension 0-3; tension rises by a 1% spontaneous chance per tick and by contagion (an agent at tension 3 raises each neighbour by 1 with about 49% chance per tick); health falls with 25% chance per tick when all neighbours are saturated; an agent at tension 3 whose neighbours are all saturated may start an accusation through a cascade of probabilistic branches (25%, then 100 minus the skepticism setting, then 50%, in the order met); the victim is chosen first among agents with fewer than 2 ties; when more than a tenth of living agents are marked and a pole exists, one marked agent is expelled, all tensions reset, and others tie to the pole with a probability set by the "closure" parameter. Links are unsigned. [read, code to about line 520; the ODD is looser than the code and I took the code as authoritative] *Corrected and added in validation, from the code through the cascade and from the project page.* (i) Tension resets for the whole network after **every** transfer that the network follows, not only at the expulsion; the page calls this the sawtooth. (ii) In the cascade, the 25% branch and the (100 - skepticism)% branches make *failed* transfers: accuser and target are marked as failed, and the accuser loses health. Only the branches taken with about 49% chance (`random 100 > 50`) make a transfer that the network follows: the accuser becomes the amplifying pole, the target is marked. This cascade runs only while poles number fewer than a twentieth of living agents. (iii) Targets are taken in order: agents with fewer than 2 ties, then previously targeted agents at network distance 3 to 5, then anyone at distance 3 to 5, then anyone. (iv) After the expulsion, the chance of tying to the pole falls both with "closure" (called `friendliness` in the code) and with network distance to the pole. The expelled agent's slot is later refilled by a new agent with four ties. Links are also deleted and added at random, each with 6% chance per tick.
- **What it does not model.** Desire, objects, rivalry or imitation. The project page says "ties have neither direction nor weight. The tension transfer has no content: it is a change of role." Target choice is by network position (few ties first, then prior marking, then distance); the page says it is not by what the target did. Negative standing is a node's role (marked, expelled), not a tie sign.
- **Reported runs (author's page, simulation not data; 20 seeds, ceiling 1,500 steps).** Base (n = 60): 20/20 expulsions, median step 546.5 (IQR 225-1,020); closure 19: 10/20, median 419; closure 0: median 279.5; skepticism 95: median 278; **n = 30: median 190**. Modularity falls and local cohesion does not recover after the expulsion.
- **Standing.** Unreviewed, MIT; "simulation results, not empirical data" (the page's own words). It supplies the *discharge* half of a fill-and-discharge drive model (cf. 081), not a sign generator. The page proposes extending it to online harassment but reports no calibration. (*Validation:* the page belongs to a site, O Mecanismo, that documents online pile-on cases with daily mention-network measures: Gini of attention, degree centralisation, modularity. It compares only the shape of the model's curves with those cases. It is an online, recent source for the expulsion stage, not read here.)

### 7.5 Copying a desire: Forgeot d'Arc et al. (2016), with Lebreton et al. (2012) [read; Lebreton read in the validation pass]

- **Definition.** Mimetic desire (MD) is "the tendency to pursue goals pursued by others." (*Credit corrected in validation:* Forgeot d'Arc et al. give that sentence citing Girard's *Violence and the Sacred*, their ref. 16, not Lebreton. Lebreton et al. credit the concept to Girard, call the phenomenon "goal contagion", and define the measured quantity, the mimetic desire effect, operationally, as below.) It is measured per subject as the difference in session-wise z-scored desirability ratings (0-10, "how much they would like to have the object") of an object when shown in a 2-5 s video as the goal of another person's action (face never shown) versus when not. The reference list cites Girard, *Violence and the Sacred*.
- **Results.** n = 20 adults with autism and 19 controls, a Montreal clinic and advertisements, 37 of 39 male. MD was positive in both groups: ASD mean 0.081, SD 0.18, one-sided t(19) = 2.08, p = .026, d = 0.46; controls 0.082, SD 0.18, t(18) = 1.99, p = .031, d = 0.46; no difference between groups (d = 0.006). MD did not correlate with social anhedonia, depression or social judgement (all |r| <= .21 within the ASD group). The power calculation takes the original amplitude as mean 0.18, SD 0.17; the authors say MD was already positive in five independent samples (Lebreton 2012, abstract: fMRI, mirror-neuron and valuation systems, connectivity predicts susceptibility).
- **The original, read in validation (Lebreton et al. 2012, Table 1 and behavioural results).** 116 healthy adults aged 18-39 in Paris: four behavioural groups (n = 24, 25, 24, 24) and one fMRI group (n = 19, 18 analysed for imaging), roughly half women. The effect in z-units (mean ± SEM) was 0.13 ± 0.07, 0.13 ± 0.03, 0.12 ± 0.06, 0.14 ± 0.05 and 0.18 ± 0.04, with paired t of 1.88, 3.82, 1.97, 2.67 and 4.28, all p < .05. Pooled, rated desirability was 5.13 against 4.69 on the 0-10 scale (t(115) = 8.50). The rating question changed across groups (like, use, acquire), and the non-goal controls were tightened from group to group (moving objects, an agent present but not acting). The effect held in every group and survived all controls. At debriefing most subjects could not say whether they preferred goal or non-goal objects, which the authors read as an implicit effect. The effect did not correlate with age, empathy (EQ) or the Reading the Mind in the Eyes test. The manipulation was not mentioned to subjects, and all gave written informed consent.
- **Shrinkage [derived from read numbers; rewritten in validation].** The first pass compared 0.081 with "the original" 0.18 and called it 0.45 of the original mean, and about 0.46 against about 1.06 in standardised units. With Lebreton read, 0.18 (SD 0.17, the values in the power analysis) is the fMRI group, the largest of five samples. Against all five:
  - The replication's mean of 0.081-0.082 is below every original sample mean (0.12-0.18): about 0.59 of the n-weighted mean of 0.138, and 0.68 of the smallest.
  - Its standardised effect, d = 0.46, lies inside the original range. From the reported paired t values, d_z = t / sqrt(n) gives about 0.38, 0.76, 0.40, 0.55 and 0.98.

  So the shrinkage is in the raw mean, and is overstated by comparing with the largest sample. It happened even though the 60 stimulus pairs were those with the largest goal-versus-non-goal contrast in the original data, which predicts a larger effect, not a smaller one. The 2016 paper does not discuss it. Possible causes are not separable from the text: regression of stimulus effects, a different population and country, language, noise, or the tightened controls of the later groups.
- **Era, medium, scale.** The original was published in May 2012 (received 21 September 2011, so its data were collected by then) and the replication in November 2016 (received 14 June 2016). Neither paper states its data years. Computer video task in a quiet room. Scale: an individual's valuation of an object, the nearest analogue of *adoption/opinion* at crowd scale; there is no group, no second person present, no rivalry.
- **Consent.** Participants were clinic patients and volunteers; the manipulation is a stimulus contrast; whether participants were told its purpose is not stated. (*Added in validation:* all signed informed consent, under local ethics approval, project 12-03P, Hôpital Rivière-des-Prairies.)
- **A second operationalisation** (Treinen, Corneille & Luypaert 2012, "L-eye to me: the combined role of Need for Cognition and facial trustworthiness in mimetic desires", *Cognition* 122(2): 247-251, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2011.10.006, PMID 22082622; title and DOI added in validation) is closed and not read. Its abstract [abs] reports that paintings looked at by a trustworthy face were preferred, but the effect *reversed* for untrustworthy faces, more so in participants low in Need for Cognition. If that holds, the sign of the copying coupling itself depends on the model. Being [abs], it feeds nothing.

## 8. What decides the sign when both want X (cross-check)

| Rule | Predicted sign | Condition the source states | Source and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 shared evaluation of X draws people together | + | X is a shared attitude object, not contested | Heider 1946, Newcomb, Byrne: not read here (D601, file 084); Heider is in Sack's reference list |
| R2 shared desire for an unshareable X | - | cannot both enjoy; parity of hope; no common power | Hobbes [read]; Sherif, group scale [read]; Newcomb DLL [104] |
| R3 imitated desire makes the model a rival, automatically | - | desire acquired from a near model (internal mediation, standard account); the object matters less and less (Grande) | Girard [quoted in Grande]; Dupuy in Antunes [read] |
| R4 gap to the leader's payoff | - (an act that harms the leader) | asymmetric coordination game; a_j - lambda_j < mu/(n-1) | d'Artigues & Vignolo [read] |
| R5 strength differences | stronger extends - to weaker; equals are + | contest success function; strength = own + friends | Hiller [read] |
| R6 a competitor's success | emulation (0/+) or envy (-) | direction of endeavour | Hobbes ch. VI [read]; van de Ven 2009 not read |
| R7 a third term | - becomes + | sovereign power; a goal both need and neither can reach alone | Hobbes ch. XIII [read]; Sherif Stage 3 [read] |

**What is decided.** For groups competing for an unshareable prize with no acquaintance, R2 holds (one camp, 1954) and R7 reverses it (the same camp, covert manipulation). **What is not.** (1) Whether imitation without excludability produces R2's sign: no test; the quoted critics and Hobbes say no, Girard says yes. (2) Which of R1 and R2 applies to a given pair: the sources name excludability, parity and distance, none measures them together. (3) R3's distance claim: untested. (4) Whether the rivalry feeds back into desire (R3's escalation): untested in anything read here; the only feedback evidence read is anecdotal, in Girard. (*Validation:* the competitive-arousal studies are candidate tests of rivalry raising the desire to win, both Crossref-confirmed, closed, not read: Ku, Malhotra & Murnighan 2005, "auction fever" in live and Internet auctions, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2004.10.001; Malhotra 2010, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.11.005. So "untested" is a limit of this file, not a finding.) Two more texts state imitation without scarcity: Lebreton et al. and Forgeot d'Arc et al. open with an uncited playground anecdote, children running after the same toy when identical toys are nearby. It is an illustration, not data.

**What the algebra would need [derived, not a source's claim].**
- **An Object sort** with an excludability parameter rho_X, because R1 and R2 differ only in it. v0.1's sorts are Person, Relation, Group, Time (section 2); there is nowhere to put "the same thing".
- **Two signs per ordered pair, not one.** Under R3 the influence weight of the model's desire on the subject's is positive (copying; MD above) while the affect weight is negative. v0.1 uses one signed a_ij both as the influence weight (Altafini form a_ij x_j - |a_ij| x_i, under which a negative weight repels the state) and as the balance sign in (4.2b). Mimetic rivalry cannot be written in that: it is attraction on the desire variable and repulsion on the relation variable. (Treinen et al. 2012 [abs] further suggest that the influence sign itself can reverse for an untrustworthy model. That is unread and feeds nothing, but it is a reason not to fix the influence sign at +.)
- **A third term** (power or superordinate goal) that can flip the sign by changing an object's interdependence (R7).
- **Variables as editable soft settings, not walls:** rho_X, a parity band, a distance threshold between subject and model, and an era tag on each (section 12).

## 9. Operand and operator translation

- **Operands.** Person (n); Object X with excludability rho_X (new sort); desire level d_i(X) for each person and object; strength n_i (Hiller) or payoff u_i; tension t_i in {0..3} (Paes); tie sign s_ij; a marked/expelled role (Paes, Hiller's single enemy).
- **Operators read.** `copy` (MD; d about 0.46 for the subject-level mean effect over 60 object pairs in one lab replication, and about 0.4 to 1.0 across Lebreton's five samples. *Corrected in validation* from "per exposure": d is not a per-trial quantity); `contest` (Hobbes; Hiller's f); `extract` (Hiller); `degrade-the-leader` (d'Artigues & Vignolo); `transfer` and `expel` (Paes); `insert-superordinate-goal` (Sherif Stage 3); `sovereign` (Hobbes).
- **Operators implied, not tested.** `oppose` (Girard, automatic at small distance); `escalate` (rivalry feeds desire).

## 11. Group size and composition, collected

- Hobbes: n = 2 suffices ("any two men") provided parity and no common power; the war of all against all needs many and no sovereign.
- Girard: the triangle is the minimal structure (Grande's footnote on groups of four is Grande's); unanimity of the many against one is the closing stage.
- Sherif: two groups of 11; matched, homogeneous, strangers; effect measured between groups; in-group solidarity rises as hostility rises.
- Newcomb/D&K: n = 17, strangers meeting; fixed 4 positive and 3 negative out-ties per man by construction.
- d'Artigues & Vignolo: harm needs a_j - lambda_j < mu/(n-1), so it fades with n; needs asymmetry; symmetric groups integrate.
- Hiller: n >= 3; multi-clique equilibria need geometric size growth (sharp contests); a strong Nash equilibrium, where one exists, is n-1 against 1 at any n (homogeneous agents; "coalition-proof" in the first pass was the wrong concept).
- Paes: expulsion threshold is a tenth of the agents, so it arrives faster at n = 30; the 30-100 range is the model's, the page's scenarios use 30 and 60.
- Forgeot d'Arc: single persons, n = 39. Lebreton: single persons, n = 116 in five samples of 19-25.

## 12. Datedness and the fluid reading

- **Form versus magnitude.** The form of the sign law, conditional on excludability, parity and a third term, is structural and does not date (Hobbes 1651 and Sherif 1954 state it; Hiller 2017 derives a version). The magnitudes do: every number above is from a named era and medium. *Corrected in validation:* the one number that has a replication, mimetic desire, came back below all five original sample means. Its mean is about 0.59 of the five-sample mean, not the 45% reported against the single largest sample. Its standardised effect (d = 0.46) lies inside the original range. The original's own five samples vary about 1.5-fold in mean and 2.5-fold in d under one lab and protocol family. That variation within one era sets a floor on any era correction: a later estimate inside that band is not evidence of drift [derived]. For the fluid approach this argues for carrying the copying gain as a band, not a point, with its era and medium tag.
- **Girard's own time-varying claim.** Rivalry grows as prohibition and social distance collapse (Things Hidden 93, via Grande). If a medium collapses perceived distance between model and subject, the share of near-model (internal) mediation rises and, on his account, the share of rival-type ties with it. No source I read tests this on any digital medium, so the algebra should carry the distance threshold as an editable setting with an era tag, not fix it.
- **Where the old evidence may not transfer.** Strangers meeting in a 1954 camp and a 1956 house are tie-formation settings with no media. Online rivalry among people who see each other's wants continuously is exactly where Girard's claim is strongest and where nothing here measures. This is for D606 and D608.

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

**What reduces.** (a) The appetite for X itself, d_i(X), is a person-level drive state, the one drive-like primitive in any of these sources. (b) The negative tie does not need a hostility drive: in Hobbes, Sherif, d'Artigues & Vignolo and Hiller it follows from positive appetites or payoffs meeting an unshareable or zero-sum structure. Hobbes lists three motives that can each produce enmity: gain, safety (fear) and reputation. (c) The relative-standing concern in d'Artigues & Vignolo's gap-to-the-leader rule, Hobbes's glory and Hiller's strength is one status-type drive (file 055). (d) Emulation versus envy is one drive state (grief at a competitor's success) read out through a branch variable, endeavour.

**What does NOT reduce.** (1) **Imitation of desire** is measured as a propensity (d = 0.46 in one lab replication; about 0.4-1.0 in the five original samples), not a deficit with a set point. It is unrelated to anhedonia and social judgement in the one sample that checked those (n = 39, so weak evidence), and to empathy and mind-reading scores in Lebreton's fMRI group (n = 19, added in validation). It survives in autism and is largely implicit. It behaves as a *coupling between persons' drive states*, not as a drive. (2) **Excludability of the object and the payoff function** are structure, not drives; v0.1 has no sort for them. (3) **Rivalry feeding back into desire**, Girard's obstacle-as-stimulant, is a tie-to-drive coupling that nothing read here tests (unread candidates in section 8). (4) **All-against-one** is a group-level equilibrium or threshold (Hiller's strong Nash equilibrium, Paes's tenth-of-the-group rule, Girard's unanimity), not a drive of any person; Paes's version takes tension as given and has no desire at all. (5) **Third terms** (a sovereign, a superordinate goal) are inserts into the situation. (6) **Girard's own framing**, as Grande reads it, treats desire as "something distinct from instincts", so his theory sets desire apart from biological need rather than testing a drive account. That is a secondary reading of books I did not read. *Corrected in validation:* the first pass wrote that Girard was "unhappy with the word 'drive'". Grande says the reverse. In the late 1990s Girard was unhappy with the word *desire*, as too sexual, and himself loosely suggested "drive", "élan vital" and "project" in its place (footnote 13). It is Grande who objects that "drive" blurs the link Girard draws between desire and scapegoating. So Girard's own vocabulary does not set itself against drives; his separation of desire from instinct does.

**Verdict.** Partly. A negative tie reduces to positive drives *plus* an object structure *plus* parity or distance; the drives alone do not fix its sign, and the mimetic amplification is a relation between drives. If "drives as primitives" means the primitives of the algebra, it needs at least one non-drive primitive (the object's excludability) and one coupling (copying).

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

- **Predicted outcome.** For rivalry as a sign generator, the outcome is the sign of a tie, or the hostility between groups, given a shared object. The one strong qualitative prediction is Hobbes's, tested at group scale by Sherif: groups competing for an unshareable prize become hostile, and a goal that needs both reverses it.
- **Out-of-sample evidence.** Thin. Sherif is one camp of 22 boys (1954), with no pre-competition baseline and a terminated 1953 predecessor; Tyerman & Spencer (1983) partly failed to replicate (in-group solidarity fell, per file 18 [abs]; not read). Newcomb is in-sample and descriptive (104). The copying effect replicated in sign in a small sample. Its mean was below all five original samples (about 0.59 of their mean, 0.45 of the largest), and its d was inside their range (corrected in validation). Hiller, d'Artigues & Vignolo and Paes have no data.
- **Effect sizes.** Sherif: out-group friendship choices 6.4% to 36.4% (Rattlers) and 7.5% to 23.2% (Eagles) after superordinate goals; unfavourable out-group ratings 53% to 4.5% and 76.9% to 22.6%; no intervals, no test statistics extracted. Copying: d = 0.46 per group, mean 0.08 z-units on a 0-10 rating scale; in the original five samples 0.12-0.18 z (paired t 1.88-4.28). Paes: median expulsion step 190 at n = 30 against 546.5 at n = 60 (simulated, 20 seeds). The rest are theorems.
- **Control.** Only Sherif tried to steer a tie sign: superordinate goals raised cross-group choices within Stage 3 (at least six days; ch. 7 numbers its days), **by covert manipulation** of the boys' situation (parents consented to the camp, the boys were not told its purpose). Contact without interdependence failed. Hobbes's remedy, a common power, is by covenant in theory and untested. Nothing here controls a tie sign with the knowledge and consent of the people it acts on.
- **Failures and gaps.** No source predicts *which* pairs become rivals from their observed desires; the Girardian chain from copying to rivalry to escalation to unanimity is untested end to end; the only field-data signature (Newcomb) cannot be told apart from popularity; the single replication of the copying measure shrank in mean, though not outside the original range in d; and no test read here uses online data.
- **For the algebra.** What can be carried as admissible: the conditional form of R2 and R7 with their era tags, and the existence of two separate signs (influence and affect). What cannot: any rivalry magnitude, any distance threshold, or Sack's theorem. *Added in validation:* the copying gain is now [read] in both the original and the replication, so it may be carried as an era- and medium-tagged band rather than a point: 0.08-0.18 z-units, d about 0.4-1.0, lab video task, Paris and Montreal, collected by 2011 and by 2016. It is a coupling on the desire variable, not a sign.
