3. **The law is a signed chain:** structure of dependence, then frequency of agreement, then positive emotion (pleasure/satisfaction, interest/excitement), then perceived cohesion, then commitment behaviour (staying, token gifts, joint ventures). Downstream of structural cohesion every sign is fixed by the axioms, so the sign of the total effect needs no magnitudes [derived]. Upstream it does not: axiom A3 adds a product term, and the one triad test found that term with the opposite sign (section 2.5).
4. **What makes a tie negative, in this tradition:** failed exchange; unequal power (the low-power partner reports negative emotion and the chain stops at frequency-to-emotion, the first step of the endogenous process); forced exchange with no alternative (cohesion can then lower staying); negatively connected networks (feeling stays inside dyads, the network fragments); generalized exchange (lowest giving, cohesion declines). After failure, anger at the other survives even when the unit is credited (Prop. 8). The negative side is far less tested than the positive.
5. **Size:** same lab, near-identical procedures, dyads to triads: agreement .62 to .42, pleasure/satisfaction 5.56 to 5.19, cohesion 5.12 to 5.01 (n.s.), token gifts .49 to .32, joint-venture investment .50 to .36 (cross-experiment, not randomised). The cohesion-to-investment link fails in triads (beta .12, R-squared .01). Humans never above n = 5 in anything read; simulation to n = 13.
6. **Strength:** the middle links are strong but self-reported on the same occasion (frequency to emotion beta .58; emotion to cohesion beta .78, R-squared .65, 128 triads); the first link, manipulated structure to frequency, is weak (R-squared .06). ## 1. The Washington paradigm: Cook & Emerson (1978) and Cook, Emerson, Gillmore & Yamagishi (1983)

### 1.1 Cook & Emerson (1978): not read

Citation and DOI in section 0. What the abstract says [abs], restated: (a) power is an attribute of a position in a network structure and shows in the occupant's behaviour even though the occupant does not know the position or its power; (b) equity or justice concerns constrain the use of that power; (c) emerging interpersonal commitments impede the use of power; (d) when power is unequally distributed, women form stronger commitments to their exchange partners than men do. The paper also reports a laboratory method for studying exchange networks as bargaining structures and closes by arguing that commitment is what separates social from economic exchange theory.

### 1.2 What the 1983 paper (read in full) says about the 1978 experiment [read-au]

- The 1978 experiment used a four-person star (positions A and B, "fig. 1a") and a four-person all-channel network (one position, C, "fig. 1b"). Result: power concentrated at the central A, absent at peripheral B, evenly spread over the C positions (1983, p. 281).
- The 1978 outcome could have been predicted from power-dependence reasoning, but also, more parsimoniously, from structural centrality; the 1983 study was designed to pull those two apart (p. 281).
- Equity concerns were removed in 1983 by not letting actors know others' profits (p. 281 fn 7; p. 290), citing 1978 for the point. The 1983 text does not say how the 1978 study handled equity; that 1978 varied what actors knew about others' profits is an inference, not a statement in anything read (the 1978 abstract says only that equity concerns constrain the use of power [abs]).
- Era and medium: NSF grant SOC75-04059 (a 1975 award, judging by its number); "computerized laboratory" with methods set out in Cook & Emerson (1977, 1978) (p. 290). Participants were undergraduates recruited from classes and campus newspaper ads.

### 1.3 Definitions and equations in the 1983 paper [read]

- **Definition 1 (p. 277), connection.** Exchange relations A-B and A-C are connected to the degree that exchange in one is contingent on exchange (positive connection) or on non-exchange (negative connection) in the other. If B and C are substitutable sources for A the connection is negative (competition, p. 278); if what A gets from B is needed to deal with C (a broker) it is positive. Dating and friendship networks are typically negatively connected throughout (p. 278). Footnote 6 notes connections vary in strength, and the paper develops no quantitative concept of it.
- **Definition 2 (p. 279), position.** A set of points whose residual graphs (the graph minus that point) are isomorphic.
- **Definitions 3 and 4 (pp. 284-285), power and dependence.** Power of A over B is A's potential to obtain favourable outcomes at B's expense. Dependence of A on B in an exchange relation is a joint function that rises with the value to A of what B supplies and falls with the availability of that to A from alternative sources. **P_AB = D_BA** (p. 285), taken from Emerson 1962 and 1972 (03 section 8 read the 1962 text). Footnote 13 stresses that the operative term is availability, not the number of alternatives.
- **Maximum-exploitation equilibrium (p. 287).** With solid lines worth 24 points and dashed lines 8, the best alternative of a peripheral F is 8/2 = 4, so full power use gives E 20 points and D and F 4 each per exchange.
- **Vulnerability (p. 300).** Reduced maximum flow RMF when a position is removed: for network 1c the maximum flow per period is 48; removing D leaves 48, removing E gives 32, removing F leaves 48, so RMF = 0, 16, 0 and only E is a point of vulnerability.
- **Hypotheses (pp. 284-288).** H1: if power follows closeness or betweenness centrality, D > E > F in network 1c. H2 to H4: power-dependence applied one relation at a time gives E > D = F; E's gains appear first against F, then against D, and end equal. H5: effects stronger under high exchange incentive. H6 to H8: speed of E's rise falls with more alternatives for D and rises with more alternatives for E.

### 1.4 The 1983 experiment and simulation [read]

- **Design.** Network 1c: five persons, positions D (centre), two E and two F, the solid lines forming the path F-E-D-E-F (my reading of the text and of the RMF figures); the 8-point dashed line joins F1 and F2, as the labels of Figure 1c show and as F's best alternative of 8/2 (p. 287) and RGe = 32 (p. 300) require [derived at validation]; each person may complete one transaction per 180-second period, so exchanges are negatively connected. 27 periods (81 minutes) in three blocks of nine. Each pair negotiates by sending offers and counteroffers over private terminals joined to a minicomputer, dividing a constant 24 points the subjects do not know. 2 x 2 between-subjects design (incentive high or low, sex), five groups per cell, **100 university students (50 men, 50 women) recruited from undergraduate classes and campus newspaper ads, 20 groups**. High incentive: $0.25 per hour fixed wage and 2.5 cents per point; low: $3.00 per hour and 0.5 cents per point (pp. 290-291).
- **Results (pp. 291-295).** E's profit rises across blocks (E with D: F(2,32) = 3.34; E with F: F(2,32) = 5.89; both p < .05). In the last block E averages 15.26 with D (SD 5.16; t = 2.75 against 12, df = 19, p < .01) and 16.18 with F (SD 2.82; t = 6.46). Under high incentive the last-block figures are 17.19 (D) and 16.91 (F) and E's gains over D and over F do not differ (difference .28, n.s.), the predicted equal powerlessness. The incentive prediction (H5) held for E's exchanges with D (t = 1.72, p < .05) but not with F. Sex was a design factor; no sex effect is reported among the results read.
- **Realised power use [derived].** Taking 12 as an equal split and 20 as the maximum, the last-block means are 41 per cent (E-D) and 52 per cent (E-F) of the way to full exploitation; under high incentive 65 and 61 per cent; under low incentive 17 and 43 per cent. Human subjects used about half of the available power. The authors say that restraints on power (equity concerns, less than fully rational bargaining) would stop equilibrium short of full exploitation (p. 287), and that the high-incentive rate settled near 16 to 8, approaching but not reaching the maximum (p. 293 fn 17); they do not attribute the shortfall to one cause.
- **Simulation SIMNET (pp. 296-298).** Written by Toshio Yamagishi in FORTRAN on a PRIME 300 minicomputer (fn 18); simulated actors accept the better of two offers, raise their demand after an accepted offer and lower it after an unaccepted one; initial demands random in 1-23; 54 trials; 50 replications per network; networks of 5, 7, 10 and 13 actors. By block six E's profit is 18.47 to 20.58 of 24 (lowest the 7-actor network with D, highest the 13-actor with F; Table 2, p. 297), at or near the 20 maximum; the low-incentive condition was not simulated (p. 298); the 13-actor network is nearly there by block two (D 19.56, F 20.06) while the 5-actor network is at 15.36 and 16.33. H6 to H8 (speed ordered by the number of alternatives) hold in the simulation.
- **Conclusion (pp. 302-303).** Closeness and betweenness centrality fail in negatively connected networks; power sits at multiple "regional centres" (the E positions) and the authors propose vulnerability as a dependence-based centrality, predicting that positively connected networks centralise power.

### 1.5 House-format summary for the Washington paradigm

- **Their vocabulary.** Exchange network, exchange relation, opportunity structure, connection (positive, negative), position, power as potential versus power use, dependence, availability of alternatives, equity, commitment, centrality, vulnerability.
- **Drives/operands.** Operands are actors (persons or corporate groups), network positions (classes of actors with isomorphic residual graphs) and exchange relations carrying a value and a set of alternatives. The only motivational variable is the value to A of what B supplies (Emerson's "motivational investment", 03) against availability elsewhere; the experiment manipulates it with money (incentive high or low). No emotion is measured in the 1983 study. Commitment appears only in the 1978 abstract and in a footnote (p. 277 fn 2) that assumes members of an exchange relation have some commitment relative to alternatives.
- **Operators and equations.** P_AB = D_BA and Definitions 1 to 4 [read]; the full-exploitation split (20 and 4 of 24) [read]; RMF [read]; the SIMNET bargaining rules [read]; Emerson's four balancing operations (withdrawal, network extension, status-giving, coalition) are in 03 section 8 and not in these papers.
- **Theorems or derived predictions.** None proved. H2 to H4 are derived verbally by applying the dyadic definition one relation at a time (the authors call this application cumbersome, p. 288); the simulation shows rational agents reproduce the ordering and its speed.
- **Empirical standing.** One human experiment (20 groups of 5, one network shape) plus a simulation; all primary power-dependence hypotheses supported, the incentive hypothesis partly; centrality predictions failed. Humans realise about half of the maximum exploitation; "rational" simulated agents nearly all of it. Replications read: none independent (1978 is the same lab). Later network exchange theory experiments by other labs exist (03 section 8) and were not read here.
- **Era / medium / scale / size.** Late 1970s (grant 1975; data years not stated); anonymous offers on computer terminals in private rooms; **group** scale (distribution of benefit, a kind of efficiency); n = 5 humans, 5 to 13 simulated; participants were paid volunteers who were not told the network or the constant sum.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: actor, position class, relation with (value, alternatives). Operators: negotiated exchange (a transfer with a split), connect(+/-) acting on pairs of relations, the map P = D transposed. Sentiment is not an operand here. The connection sign is the structural analogue of a tie sign, but it signs links between relations, not between persons.

## 2. The relational-cohesion programme: Lawler & Yoon (1996) and the studies around it

### 2.1 Lawler & Yoon (1996): not read

Citation and DOI in section 0. Closed access; nothing in it is [read]. What follows is what the authors' own later accounts say it contains (Thye, Yoon & Lawler 2002, ms. pp. 3, 8-18; Lawler & Thye 2006; Lawler, Thye & Yoon 2000), all [read-au].

### 2.2 What the 1996 paper contains, according to its authors [read-au]

- **Concept.** Relational cohesion is the perception, by those in an exchange relation, that the relation is a unifying element or force in the situation. Commitment is the strength of a tie between a person and a social unit, shown in staying despite alternatives, giving unilateral benefits, or collaborating on joint goods (2002 ms. p. 3). The name is older than the concept: Lawler, Yoon, Baker & Large (1995, ms. p. 3) note that Emerson (1972, 1981) called mutual dependence (average power) "relational cohesion"; Lawler & Yoon (1996) re-used the name for a perception [read, 1995].
- **Model (a causal diagram, not equations).** Exogenous structure: relative power (equal or unequal alternatives) and total power or mutual dependence (the sum or average of the two actors' dependence). Endogenous chain: frequency of agreement, then positive emotion (pleasure/satisfaction and interest/excitement), then relational cohesion, then commitment behaviour of three kinds: staying in the relation despite equal or better alternatives, unilateral token gifts, and taking part in a new joint venture that exposes one to malfeasance (a two-party social dilemma). Power as structural potential is kept distinct from power use (tactics) and power outcomes (the division of profit) (2002 ms. pp. 12-14).
- **Design.** Three experiments, one per commitment behaviour. Two subjects in separate rooms, buyer and seller for two companies, negotiate for twelve episodes ("years"); 2 (total power high or low) x 2 (relative power equal or unequal); measures taken at points that follow the chain (2002 ms. p. 16).
- **Results as described.** High total power and equal relative power produce more agreements; frequency raises both emotions; emotions raise cohesion (each alone; with both entered only pleasure/satisfaction is significant); cohesion is the strongest and a significant predictor of all three commitment behaviours with everything else in the model (2002 ms. p. 17). **No coefficients, sample sizes or effect sizes appear in the reviews.** The only size I could recover is from the 2000 paper's Table 4 note: N = 240 dyads for "the dyad study" [read]; my reading is three experiments of about 80 dyads, which is an inference, not a statement.
- **Dyad means from the 2000 paper's Table 4 [read, p. 647], which that paper says are Lawler & Yoon's (1996) data (pp. 637, 646):** agreement frequency .62 (SD .20); pleasure/satisfaction 5.56 (1.28); interest/excitement 6.34 (.98); positive emotion 5.95 (1.01); relational cohesion 5.12 (.91); token gifts .49 (.20); investment in the joint venture .50 (.27); nine-point bipolar scales for the emotions and cohesion, proportions for the behaviours.
- **Predecessor, Lawler & Yoon (1993)** ([read-au]; *ASR* 58(4): 465-481, doi:10.2307/2096071, Crossref-confirmed, not read): dyads negotiating the price of iron ore and zinc, equal versus unequal power crossed with integrative versus distributive bargaining. Agreement frequency was related to interest/excitement but, unusually, not to pleasure/satisfaction (a non-finding the 2006 chapter says has rarely recurred since), and interest/excitement predicted staying and gift giving (2002 ms. pp. 10-11; 2006 chapter, section on evidence). Four mechanisms were proposed for commitment: uncertainty reduction, an expanded range of goods, attraction to the partner, and the relation becoming a salient object to which positive emotion is attributed.
- **Era and medium.** About 1992 to 1995 (inferred from grant numbers and publication dates; data years not stated in anything read). Separate rooms, anonymous; whether offers were typed or written is not stated for 1993 and 1996 (the 1995 paper says written offers).

### 2.3 Lawler, Yoon, Baker & Large (1995): the second dyad experiment [read]

- **Design.** Two focal actors, each with one alternative partner, negotiate the price of iron ore (one buyer, one seller, anonymous, "in the next room") over eight independent episodes of up to five rounds of written offers. 2 x 2: mutual dependence (expected value of the alternative 275 under high, 375 under low; the focal optimum 400) by uncertainty about the alternative (peaked versus flat payoff distribution, same mean). **52 dyads, all female, 13 per cell.** Gifts (a voucher exchanged for candy; the other's gifts unknown until the end) could be given at the end of episodes 5 to 8 (pp. 16-20 of the manuscript).
- **Results.** High mutual dependence raised agreement frequency (F(1,51) = 10.27, 6.35 and 11.73 for first half, second half and overall; eta-squared .17, .11, .19 [derived from F and df]). The predicted uncertainty effect did not appear (F < 1). There was a dependence-by-uncertainty interaction (F(1,51) = 4.75, 3.96, 6.21): more agreement under uncertainty at low mutual dependence (marginal, one-tailed), the reverse at high (n.s.); the authors call it weak and say it "might be interpreted" as a framing effect after Kahneman and Tversky (1979) (ms. pp. 22-23, 29).
- **Mediation.** Mutual dependence to early frequency beta .674; early to later frequency beta .421 (indirect .284); later frequency to gift giving the only significant path, indirect effect of mutual dependence on gifts .157. The same chain holds for attitudinal commitment (beta .465), pleasure/happiness (.421) and perceived closeness (.541), all measured once, after episode eight. Zero-order correlations with gift giving: pleasure/happiness .45, closeness .48, attitudinal commitment .21. Of three self-reported gift motives only "felt positive toward the other" correlated with giving (r = .41); expecting reciprocity (-.20) and raising own profit (-.15) did not.
- **Habitualization** (the authors' term; this line was headed "Ceiling" before validation). Agreement frequency rose from .55 in the first block to .69 in the second (F(1,103) = 11.15), with no block interactions (ms. p. 23).
- **Scope and caveat.** The questionnaire came at the end, so the emotion links cannot be sequenced; the authors say so (p. 28).

### 2.4 Lawler, Thye & Yoon (2000): three-person productive exchange [read]

- **Setting.** Three students each represent a research organisation (alpha, beta, gamma) deciding whether to form a joint venture that needs all three to assent. Claims on a pool of 1,625 points per "year" are made simultaneously and independently, in multiples of 25, up to five rounds; claims may be repeated or lowered, not raised; agreement if the claims sum to at most the pool; otherwise each takes a random draw from a simulated alternative group. Sixteen episodes (told up to twenty). Questionnaires after episodes 6 and 12; commitment options from episode 13: two gift vouchers per episode (candy, the others' gifts unknown), or investing 200 points in a joint account at 50 per cent return shared equally, a decomposed three-person prisoner's dilemma whose payoffs were hidden until the end (pp. 630-635).
- **Manipulations (pp. 632-633, fn 10).** Alternatives with a five-point payoff distribution (5, 15, 60, 15, 5 per cent) around a mean. High total dependence: mean alternative 200; low: 350. Unequal: 350, 200 and 50 (high total dependence) or 500, 350 and 200 (low). Dependence of member i is the equal-share maximum in the group (1625/3, about 542) minus the expected value of i's alternative; members knew only their own alternative (p. 639).
- **Subjects.** 384 undergraduates at "a large eastern university" (site not named; authors then at Cornell, South Carolina and Ajou; grant SBR-9514860; presented 1998, so data about 1996 to 1998), paid; 64 same-sex triads per experiment (32 female, 32 male), 16 per cell; two experiments identical until episode 13 (gifts, investment); analysis N = 128 triads (p. 631, p. 638).
- **Measures.** Nine-point bipolar adjective items: pleasure/satisfaction (pleased, happy, satisfied, contented), interest/excitement, predictability, group cohesion (close-distant, coming together-coming apart, solid-fragile, cohesive-divisive); alphas .77 to .97. A four-factor model fits (GFI .88, CFI .97, RMSEA .06); a one-factor model does not (CFI .63) (p. 638).

The ratio column is mine; for the nine-point scales it has no ratio meaning (no true zero), only for the proportions. The authors' reading: cohesion and commitment are more problematic in triadic productive exchange because of coordination problems; the chain starts lower and grows, while in dyads it is set early. ### 2.5 The one formalisation, and a sign calculus [read, derived]

**The appendix (2000, pp. 652-654), restated.** Let D_i be member i's dependence on the focal group. Total dependence TD is the mean of the D_i. Dependence inequality DI is the average absolute difference between members' dependences over the N-choose-2 pairs. Axiom 1: structural cohesion SC depends on TD, DI and their product INT, with partial derivatives SC_TD > 0, SC_DI < 0, SC_INT < 0 (A1 to A3). Axiom 2: group cohesion GC depends on positive emotion PE and predictability PR; PR and PE each depend on agreement frequency AF; AF depends on SC; with GC_PE, GC_PR, PR_AF, PE_AF, AF_SC all > 0 (A4 to A8). The paper says its path predictions follow by substituting A1 to A3 into A8.

**A printing slip [derived].** As typeset, DI sums |D_i - D_j| over all i and j from 1 to N (ordered pairs) and divides by N-choose-2; for D = (342, 192, 42) that gives 400, but the worked example in footnote 10 (p. 633) gives 200, which is the sum over unordered pairs. Only the signs of the axioms matter to the paper, so nothing downstream changes.

**The sign calculus [derived].**

1. *Downstream of SC the signs are determined.* dGC/dSC = (GC_PE PE_AF + GC_PR PR_AF) AF_SC > 0: two parallel paths, both positive, so no cancellation is possible.
2. *Upstream they are not, once A3 is admitted.* With INT = TD x DI, dSC/dDI = SC_DI + SC_INT TD < 0 always, so inequality always lowers structural cohesion. But dSC/dTD = SC_TD + SC_INT DI is positive only while DI < SC_TD / |SC_INT|: high mutual dependence raises cohesion only up to a threshold of inequality. The paper states only the three signs.
3. *The one triad test reversed A3.* Cell means of agreement frequency (fn 15, p. 639): low total dependence, equal .42, unequal .35; high total dependence, equal .43, unequal .46. Raising total dependence adds +.01 under equality and +.11 under inequality, so the cross-partial in (TD, DI) is about +.10. A3 and the paper's own "extra structural push" for high-total-and-equal groups (p. 628) imply a negative one. The paper reports the interaction between the two dummies (high total power, equal power) as beta -.27, p < .05, and glosses it as meaning that frequency was lowest when power was unequal and mutual dependence low; it adds in footnote 15 that the interaction was absent in four earlier dyad experiments. It does not call it a failed prediction; I do. The main effects (.33 and .21) survive.
4. *Series, parallel, and cancellation.* Along a chain the sign is the product of the link signs; across parallel paths it is the sum, which is indeterminate when path signs differ. The 2011 study shows the indeterminate case: cohesion raised resource sharing (b = .420) but the equal-power and weak-power dummies had negative direct effects, and the net effect of structure on sharing was nil (F(4,70) = .54) (section 2.6).
5. *What the axioms leave out:* group size beyond the averages in TD and DI (TD and DI are intensive, so the coordination cost of a larger n that the data show, .62 to .42, is outside the formalism), negative emotion, attribution, the last links to commitment (gift, stay, invest), time, and every parameter.

### 2.6 Extensions: read-au (1998, voluntariness) and read at source (2008, 2011)

### 2.7 House-format summary for the relational-cohesion programme

- **Their vocabulary.** Relational (group) cohesion as perceived unity; commitment (stay, gift, venture); total power (mutual dependence) and relative power; exchange frequency; the endogenous process; pleasure/satisfaction and interest/excitement; predictability; boundary-defining (uncertainty) versus social-bonding (affect) routes; micro social order; person-to-unit versus person-to-person ties; productive, negotiated, reciprocal and generalized exchange.
- **Drives/operands.** Persons with alternatives (outside options) and a payoff from agreement; state variables are self-reported scales (emotions, predictability, cohesion) on nine points; behaviours are proportions (agreement, gifts, investment). The motive assumed is self-interest plus a tendency to repeat good feelings; no named drive is measured.
- **Operators and equations.** Only the 2000 appendix (section 2.5) and the SEM paths, which are regressions, not laws. The 1996 paper's own formal content is unknown.
- **Theorems or derived predictions.** None proved by the authors; section 2.5 gives the signs that follow from the axioms and the conditional sign of total dependence.
- **Empirical standing.** About eleven experiments (publications 1993 to 2011, data to about 2005) by one network of three authors with internal replication of the exchange-to-emotion-to-cohesion chain; the cohesion-to-investment link, the uncertainty hypothesis (1995), the negotiated-over-reciprocal ordering and the structure-to-sharing link did not hold; A3's sign was reversed in the one triad test. Independent confirmation of the full chain: none read (Molm's group, Willer and others test neighbouring claims, section 4; independent lab tests of neighbouring links from 2011 to 2022 exist at abstract level, section 7).
- **Era / medium / scale / size.** 1992 to about 2005; anonymous, computer-mediated (typed or written offers in the earliest); **membership** scale for stay, gift and invest, **group** scale for cohesion and attachment; n = 2, 3 and 4; consent: paid volunteers, deception by cover story and simulated alternatives, debriefed (2000, p. 632).
- **Operand/operator translation.** New operand: a *unit* (relation, network, group) that persons can be attached to or detached from, nested inside larger units. New states: global affect with valence and an arousal-like second axis; specific emotions toward self, other and unit. Operators: *exchange* (a joint act that succeeds or fails with a probability set by dependence structure), *feel* (outcome to global affect), *attribute* (affect and task structure to a target among self, other, unit), *bond* (attribution to attachment sign and size). The chain composes by sign product along a path.

## 3. Lawler (2001), "An affect theory of social exchange" [read in full]

Citation, DOI and route in section 0. A theory paper: no data, no functional form. It gives the assumptions and propositions the 2008 and 2011 studies later test.

### 3.1 Their vocabulary

- **Emotion** is a short-lived positive or negative evaluative state with physiological and cognitive parts; **sentiment** is an enduring affective state about a social object; sentiments link emotions to social units (pp. 325-326).
- **Global emotion**: a "primitive", involuntary feeling along a positive-to-negative dimension (pleasant feelings are interest and joy, unpleasant ones stress and sadness; interest and stress carry higher arousal than joy and sadness, after Russell et al. 1989 and Larsen & Diener 1992, p. 331), outcome-dependent, attribution-independent, with an ambiguous source; **specific emotion**: attached to an object by an attribution (Weiner 1986) (pp. 326, 331).
- **Social objects**: task, self, other, social unit. The scheme pairs pleasantness/unpleasantness with the task, pride/shame with self, gratitude/anger with the other, and affective attachment/detachment (alienation) with the unit (Table 1, p. 332).
- **Joint task** and its two dimensions: **nonseparability** (individual contributions cannot be told apart; structural) and **shared responsibility** (credit or blame for success or failure is shared; perceptual), tied to **socially mediated self-efficacy** (pp. 333-334).
- **Forms of exchange**: productive (person-to-group, a common product), negotiated, reciprocal, generalized (indirect); **connection**: positive or negative; **nested units**; **network-to-group transformation**; **objectification** of a relation (Berger and Luckmann) (pp. 334-345).
- **Solidarity**: the strength and durability of person-to-group and person-to-person relations, indicated by expanding collaboration, unilateral gifts, accepting incomplete contracts, forgiving opportunism, and staying despite equal or better alternatives (p. 329).

### 3.2 The five assumptions, restated (p. 327)

1. Social exchange produces global emotions along a positive-negative dimension. 2. Those emotions are internal rewarding or punishing stimuli (Bandura's self-reinforcement). 3. Actors strive to reproduce positive and avoid negative global emotions. 4. The emotions trigger cognitive effort to find their causes; specific emotions tied to objects result. 5. In joint tasks such as exchange, actors explain their feelings partly by reference to social units. Scope (pp. 326-327): a network of three or more actors, exchanges dyadic and connected, ongoing and renegotiable at will; spot exchanges among strangers and market buying and selling are excluded.

### 3.4 Equations and formal content [read]

- **None with parameters.** Table 2 (p. 340) rates nonseparability, shared responsibility and global emotion qualitatively (high, medium, low) for the four forms: productive highest on all three (tied with negotiated on shared responsibility), direct forms in between, generalized lowest; nonseparability is rated medium for negotiated and low for reciprocal (tied with generalized), and shared responsibility "medium to high" for reciprocal.
- **One notation line (p. 337):** the group-level outcome in negotiated exchange "A + B = G", described in the same sentence as the average of the negotiators' offers or demands, so sum and average are conflated.
- **The structure as a chain:** nonseparability (set by the form of exchange) leads to shared responsibility, then to unit attribution, then to attachment or detachment according to the valence of the global emotion (P1, P2, P6, P9). Valence comes from success or failure of the joint task (A1).

### 3.5 Theorems or derived predictions

None stated. Derived from the text [derived]:

1. **A negativity asymmetry is built in.** By P8, under unit attribution success gives pride and gratitude together, but failure still gives more anger at the other than shame in self. So the person-to-person tie after failure stays negative even when the unit is credited; the person-to-unit tie is the one that unit attribution can protect.
2. **P9 fixes a sign only in two cells of a valence-by-stability-by-controllability table.** Positive, stable, controllable gives attachment; negative, stable, uncontrollable gives detachment. For negative feeling controlled by the other, footnote 9 (p. 343) says the result is a strong negative reaction to the other person, not necessarily to the relation, because intent does not apply to units. The remaining cells (unstable causes, controllable negative feeling at the unit level) are unspecified.
3. **The ordering ranks task structures, not persons.** The authors compose it with the 2000 chain in 2008 (their H7): a form sets nonseparability and shared responsibility and so how much of the frequency-to-emotion-to-cohesion chain ends up attached to the unit. The 2008 regressions are consistent with that but cannot separate it from same-occasion self-report inflation.

### 3.6 Empirical standing (blunt)

- The paper has no data. Tests: Lawler, Thye & Yoon 2008 (the authors' own, n = 3, null-connected triads) supports P3, P4, P6 and mostly P7, fails P5 and leaves P8 to P12 untested; Thye, Lawler & Yoon 2011 does not test the propositions directly. **No independent test of the affect theory was read.** (Before validation this said none was *found*; that was wrong.) Independent lab tests of its neighbouring claims exist at abstract level: Kuwabara (2011) finds cooperative bilateral exchange builds more cohesion than unilateral giving; Molm, Whitham & Melamed (2012) find the reciprocal form critical for trust and affective bonds, against P5 and P7; Whitham (2021) builds on affect theory and reciprocity theory together. Citations in section 7; none may feed an equation.
- The theory's escape hatch: Molm and colleagues find reciprocal exchange gives stronger trust, commitment and positive affect toward the partner than negotiated exchange, the reverse of P5 and P7. Lawler (p. 341) reconciles this by saying Molm's object is the other person and his own is the social unit, so cohesion is "more impersonal in negotiated exchange and more personal in reciprocal exchange". That distinction makes some tests ambiguous: the 2008 authors count negotiated exchange's higher anger and lower gratitude as contrary to their predictions (H5 and H6), while the 2001 text could accommodate it for person-directed emotion.
- The 2008 result also shows the chain does not travel across forms of exchange: under generalized exchange giving did not even produce positive emotion (r = .24, n.s.).

### 3.7 House-format summary for Lawler (2001)

- **Drives/operands.** Actors who seek benefits they cannot get alone (instrumental) and who also "emote": global feeling is a reward or punishment signal that actors want to reproduce or avoid. Operands are persons, tasks, and social units as attitude objects.
- **Operators.** Exchange of a given form (a joint act); feel; attribute (with self-serving bias, stability and controllability); bond or detach; connect (positive, negative); nest (relation inside network).
- **Operand/operator translation.** The theory supplies a sign-generator for person-to-unit ties (valence of the global emotion times unit attribution) and for person-to-person ties (gratitude or anger, via attribution to the other). It supplies no rates, no saturation, no decay, no n-dependence.

**What the sources say changes with n.**

- *Coordination cost.* Productive exchange needs everyone's assent and offers are simultaneous, so agreement falls from dyad to triad (.62 to .42) and positive emotion and gifts fall with it, while perceived cohesion hardly moves (2000, p. 647). The authors say the chain tends to start lower and grow in triads and to be set early in dyads (p. 648).
- *Which link breaks.* The cohesion-to-investment link holds in two-person dilemmas and fails in the three-person dilemma (beta .12); the authors point to trust being harder with a third person, and predictability then predicts investing directly (.30) (2000, pp. 643-645).
- *The theory's own size statements.* Lawler 2001 assumes three or more actors (p. 326). The 2000 paper argues triads are the smallest group where a unit is perceived as external to persons (Simmel; p. 625 fn 6) and that in a dyad the person-to-group bond is "wrapped up" with the person-to-person bond (p. 618). It predicted that a success in a triad should feel like a bigger accomplishment, hence stronger positive emotion (p. 625); the data show lower mean emotion, and that prediction was not tested directly. The 2013 test (abstract only) finds triads more cohesive than isolated dyads and swaps the mediator, uncertainty reduction in triads and affect in dyads [abs].
- *In the formalism n enters only through averages.* TD and DI (section 2.5) are means over members and pairs, so they are intensive and carry no n-dependence; the empirical n effect lives outside the axioms. In the Washington paradigm n enters through the structure of alternatives and positions, not through a count (1983 fn 13).
- *Humans never above five* in anything read; simulation to thirteen with programmed rational agents.

**Composition and definition.**

- *Sex.* The 1978 abstract reports stronger commitments by women under unequal power [abs]. Sex was a factor in 1983 with no effect reported in the results read; groups were same-sex or counterbalanced in 2000, 2008 and 2011, and none of those papers analyses sex in the passages read; the 1995 sample was all female.
- *Power composition within a group.* The stem network splits into one equal and one unequal dyad and the chain works only in the equal one [read-au]; in branch networks the central actor gave 42 to 44 per cent of a 100-point endowment away and low-power actors 18 per cent [read].
- *Shared identity.* An exogenous common identity ("departments of one organisation") shrank the central actor's profit-taking (1998) [read-au].
- *Definition of the unit.* The tradition defines the social unit (relation, network or group) by perception: the bounded entity that actors see as the principal context for their exchange (2001, p. 331), and a micro social order as recurrent interaction plus global emotions plus perceived unit-hood plus attachment (2008 ms. p. 4). Perceived cohesion is therefore an output measured by semantic-differential items, not an input; 087 discusses why this differs from structural cohesion (Moody and White).

## 6. What a sign law from this tradition would say, and where it holds (input to D604)

**The generators, with their conditions.**

| Generator | Sign it produces | Stated conditions | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Success of the joint exchange task | Positive global feeling, then attachment to the unit if the unit is credited | Frequent agreement, which rises with equal relative power and with mutual dependence (poorer outside options) | [read] E4, E6, E8, E9 |
| Failure of the task | Negative global feeling; anger at the other exceeds shame in self even under unit attribution | Low agreement rate; unequal power; no alternative | [read] E6 (episode-6 mean below the midpoint); [read-au] E10; [sa] E12, E13 |

| Voluntariness | The chain needs an alternative to exist; forced exchange can reverse the cohesion-to-staying sign | A worse alternative available | [read-au] E10, E11 |
| Connection sign | Positive connection: feeling diffuses to the network; negative: pockets inside dyads and fragmentation | Structure of alternatives | Theory [read] P10, P11; partly contradicted by E9 |

**The rule in one line [derived, qualitative].** The sign of the person-to-unit tie is the sign of the global feeling from the recent runs of the joint task, passed through a unit-attribution gate that opens with nonseparability and shared responsibility and closes under forced or unequal exchange. Positive outcomes are shared (pride rises with gratitude); negative ones stay partly with the other (anger above shame). No source gives a magnitude; the gate is ordinal.

**Where it plugs into v0.** v0.1 holds sgn(a_ij) at A(0) and moves only magnitudes. This tradition treats the tie as a slow variable moved by joint-task outcomes, and the only dynamics read are directional: perceived cohesion rose over a session under productive exchange (6.27 to 7.18 across 48 episodes) and fell under generalized exchange (4.60 to 3.42); in productive triads frequency, pleasure and cohesion rose between episodes 6 and 12 (E6). No rate constant, saturation, decay or sign-flip threshold is given anywhere. A monotone saturating update in the cumulative success rate would be consistent with all of it and is supported by none of it in particular.

**Link to the signed triad (hypothesis, not a result) [derived].** Emerson's negative connection (two substitutable partners for one actor, so exchange with one excludes the other; Cook et al. 1983, p. 278) is the same configuration as Doreian and Krackhardt's rivalry-for-k mechanism for first-tie-negative triples (104 section 3.4). Exchange theory says where such a configuration comes from (alternatives) but gives no sign for the tie between the competitors beyond the excluded partner's unhappiness and the claim that positive feeling stays inside the winning dyads. In the 2011 networks, competitors in equal-power, high-inclusion networks still formed network-level affiliation, which a pure rivalry sign would not predict.

**Validity domain** (what any rule built on E1 to E11 can claim): humans n = 2 to 5; data c. 1976 to c. 2005; anonymous computer-mediated exchange of numbers or short text with a simulated outside option; US university undergraduates, paid volunteers; single sessions (81 minutes in the one study that states it); outcomes are nine-point self-reports and small-stake behaviours; an exchange needs a mutually profitable agreement; no face-to-face comparator, no non-human members, no group larger than five.

**Admissible for v0.2 now** (read at source, with era and medium): the 1983 definitions and P_AB = D_BA; the dyad chain (E4); the triad chain, its R-squared values and the A3 reversal (E6); the form ordering and the lagged coefficient (E8); the network frequency ordering and the countervailing-path result (E9); the derived sign calculus (section 2.5). **Not admissible** ([read-au], [sa], [abs]): anything about the 1996 and 1978 papers, Molm, Willer, Weiner, the 1998 and 2006 results, the 2013 and 2019 tests.

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

Partly, and the part that reduces is the engine, not the content.

**What reduces to drives of persons.**

- *Exchange frequency under dependence.* Emerson's dependence is the value to A of what B supplies (motivational investment, a reward or desire term) against its availability elsewhere (exit options); with P_AB = D_BA that gives who gets what in a network. This is the best-supported reduction: 20 five-person groups reproduce E > D = F, and simulated agents that only maximise reproduce the ordering and its speed. One scalar reward value per actor and an outside-option set suffice for the ordering in that network.
- *Affect as reinforcement.* Assumptions 1 to 3 treat the global emotion as an internal reward or punishment that actors want to reproduce or avoid: a single hedonic regulation drive on a bipolar valence, with interest/excitement as an arousal-like second axis. Pleasure/satisfaction tracks realised agreement (frequency to emotion beta .58). In drive vocabulary, pleasure/satisfaction behaves like consummatory reward, interest/excitement like seeking or curiosity (it carried the 1993 result and lost to pleasure from 1996), and the below-midpoint early feeling in triads is at least compatible with a frustration-like negative valence (a hint, not a measurement).
- *Control and choice.* The programme's roots (Lawler 1992, 1997, in the 2002 review) say attachment grows where the unit gives a sense of control; the voluntariness results (forced exchange breaks the chain) fit a control or autonomy drive.
- *Uncertainty reduction.* Predictability leading to trust and to investing in a dilemma (E6) is a separate, safety-like pathway that does not run through cohesion.

**What does NOT reduce to drives of persons or groups.**

- *The structural inputs.* Outside options, connection sign, task nonseparability, form of exchange, voluntariness (the choice set) are properties of the interaction graph and the task, not of anyone's drives. The same persons with the same reward and affect drives give giving rates that differ by form with eta-squared about .5 (E8); in one structure (the branch) the powerful give away 42 to 44 per cent and the powerless 18. A drive vector does not determine the outcome; structure operates on it.
- *The attribution rule.* Where a feeling is placed (self, other, unit) depends on stability, controllability, self-serving bias and jointness, a cognitive operator with an asymmetry between success and failure (P8). It is not a drive, and it is what turns affect into a tie.
- *Equity and the restraint of power.* Programmed profit-maximisers use almost all available power (SIMNET). Humans in the 1983 design, where equity concerns were removed by hiding others' profits, still used only about half, and the authors give no single reason (incomplete convergence in 27 periods, or less than rational bargaining; the high-incentive rate settled near 16 to 8). The 1978 abstract adds that when equity concerns operate they constrain power use, and that emerging commitments impede it. Whatever restrains power is a norm, a commitment or bounded learning, not reward-seeking.
- *Commitment itself.* Attachment to a unit is an output of the chain and requires a new operand (the unit) that persons relate to; affiliation does not appear as a primitive drive. The tradition shows a bond can be derived from reward-seeking, affect regulation and a joint-task structure without positing an affiliation drive, but only if structure is a primitive too.
- *Negative ties from rivalry.* Failure produces unpleasant feeling, which a hedonic drive explains, but a tie to a competitor depends on whether alternatives are substitutable (negative connection), a structural fact. The 2011 networks show competition does not by itself produce negative affiliation.
- *Composition effects.* The reported sex difference under unequal power (1978 abstract) has no drive explanation in anything read.
- *Magnitudes.* No drive strength is estimated; every coefficient is a regression among self-reported scales. There is no state equation.

**Verdict on the working hypothesis "drives as primitives".** Supported as the transduction from structure to sentiment (reward-seeking plus affect regulation plus a control drive), not as the content. The explanatory weight lies in a structure-to-jointness map. An algebra built on this tradition needs at least two kinds of primitive: drive states of persons, and structural relations (dependence, connection sign, task form, nesting) as operands or operators over them.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**What it predicts.** (1) Who obtains what at each position of an exchange network (group scale, distribution): E > D = F confirmed in 20 five-person groups and in simulation, with humans realising about half the maximum. (2) Agreement frequency from dependence structure (group scale, efficiency): large between networks (F(4,70) = 61.19, eta-squared about .78, 3 to 4 actors) but weak within a manipulation of power in triads (R-squared .06). (3) Self-reported emotion and perceived cohesion from frequency (group and membership sentiment): beta .58 and .78 with R-squared .34 and .65 in 128 triads. (4) Token gifts and staying from cohesion (membership scale): beta .45 (R-squared .20) and .21; investing in a joint venture: beta .12, R-squared .01. (5) Form-of-exchange ordering of giving, pleasure, cohesion and attachment in triads (group scale): eta-squared .43 to .52. (6) Cohesion trajectories: rising under productive exchange, falling under generalized (about +.9 and -1.2 scale points over 48 episodes).

**Out-of-sample evidence.** Internal replication across about eleven experiments by one group (1993 to c. 2005) for the exchange-to-emotion link and for cohesion-to-gifts; no independent replication of the full chain read; independent labs disagree on part of it (negotiated versus reciprocal: Molm, and Molm, Whitham & Melamed 2012 [abs]), while Kuwabara (2011) [abs] finds cooperative bilateral exchange beating unilateral giving on cohesion (section 7). Nothing predicts individual behaviour; every analysis is at dyad, triad or network level, and the 2011 paper aggregated to the network "to circumvent data interdependencies".

**Failures and soft spots.** The 1995 uncertainty hypothesis (null); the cohesion-to-investment link in triads (beta .12, R-squared .01); the negotiated-over-reciprocal prediction (null, with negotiated worse on anger and gratitude); structure having no effect on dictator-game sharing in competition networks (F = .54); the sign of A3 reversed in the triad cell means (+.10 against a predicted negative); the dual-process revision that added three paths after the fact; forced exchange breaking the chain and possibly reversing cohesion's effect on staying; emotion-to-cohesion falling from beta .30 to .22 and from p < .01 to p < .10 under a time lag, which points to shared-method inflation in the same-occasion links; generalized exchange producing no giving-to-emotion link at all. The strongest steps (emotion to cohesion) are the most exposed to common-method bias: both are bipolar-adjective self-reports on one questionnaire.

**What it controls, if anything.** Nothing outside the lab. The levers are structural and demonstrated only as between-group lab manipulations: make power equal, raise mutual dependence, give the task a productive (joint, nonseparable) form, preserve a worse alternative so exchange stays voluntary, share responsibility. Consent: equal power, productive task design, voluntariness and shared accountability are design choices that can be disclosed and agreed to; the mutual-dependence lever works by worsening outside options, which is capture or lock-in if applied to someone and manipulation unless the parties choose it jointly (ledger `ethics`: manipulation for that lever, consent for the others). Time scale of the demonstrated effects: a single session (81 minutes where stated). Scale: dyads to five-person groups; no crowd-scale prediction or control.
