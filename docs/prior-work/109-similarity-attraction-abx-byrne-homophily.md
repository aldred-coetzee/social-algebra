## Summary (what this file finds)

1. **Newcomb's theory has no equation, and Byrne's line is unverified.** Newcomb 1953 contains no equation at all [read in full]; the A-B-X "theory" is a set of signed relations and verbal propositions. Byrne's line Y = 5.44X + 6.62 is [sa] only: the one source I could read states it without naming a paper (APS Observer memoir), and Byrne & Nelson (1965), the paper the map credits, is closed. **The map's seed DOI for Byrne & Nelson is wrong**: 10.1037/h0021198 resolves to Byrne, Griffitt & Stefaniak (1967); the right DOI is 10.1037/h0022073 (section 0.3).
2. **What Byrne's paradigm shows at source** (Byrne 1961 [read]): with an unseen "stranger" whose answers to 26 issues the experimenter fixes, liking at the extremes is enormous (Personal Feelings 6.53 vs 1.76 on a 7-point scale, 17 per cell, Cohen's d about 7.6 [derived]). It is a deception design, a dyad, no interaction, no group, 1959-60 US undergraduates, paper stimuli.
3. **The law behind the Newcomb data is not Byrne's law.** In the Newcomb house study (Newcomb 1956 [read]) similarity of attitudes toward issues was a weak predictor (p between .05 and .01; no issue was relevant to all 17 men). What worked was similarity in how two men liked the other men plus estimated reciprocation (combined contingency coefficient .53, in-sample), and the forward test, assigning roommates to maximise or minimise attraction, "received no support whatever". This is the same kind of regularity as the "agreement on k" mechanism D&K name in file 104 (agreement about third persons).
4. **In free-choice data the opportunity structure carries most of homophily.** Kossinets & Watts 2009 [read]: odds ratio 1.89 per similarity point overall, 1.03 within shared foci. Chetty et al. 2022 [read]: about half of the class gap in friendships is exposure, half friending bias. Currarini, Jackson & Pin 2010 [read]: meeting bias is significantly larger in large schools; preference bias is not significantly size-dependent. McPherson et al. 2001 [read]: structural sources dominate choice.
5. **The operand that predicts in existing relationships is perceived, not actual, similarity** (Newcomb 1956 [read]; Montoya et al. 2008 and Tidwell et al. 2013 [from abstract]), and attraction and perceived similarity are coupled in a loop the theory itself requires (Newcomb 1953, p. 402 [read]). Signs therefore become state, not input [ours].
6. **The one causal out-of-sample result is structural, not motivational**: Chetty et al.'s cross-cohort design (slope 0.89; predicted by the school's friending bias estimated from other cohorts). It steers by composition, openly, and works best where friending bias is low (the effect is positive everywhere but smaller where bias is high).
7. **Era and medium matter in this tradition more than in most**: the measured effect of similarity is largest in the 1960 information-only paper design and smaller in every free-choice setting I read (1950s house, 1994-2022 school, e-mail, Facebook and dating-site data), though the measures are not comparable (d, correlation, odds ratio); and the dimension that sorts people drifts (taste meaning depends on medium; partisanship; religion and education trends).
8. **Drive reading**: the originators' own mechanism is a learned need to be right and predictable (consensual validation, calculability), measured nowhere as a drive and covering only the choice half. See the closing sections.

## 1. Newcomb (1953): the A-B-X system [read]

**Citation.** Newcomb, T. M. (1953). An approach to the study of communicative acts. *Psychological Review* 60(6): 393-404, doi:10.1037/h0063098 (Crossref-confirmed 2026-11-01); manuscript received 15 December 1952; University of Michigan.

**Their vocabulary.** Communicative act, written AtoBreX (A transmits information to B about X); information as the occurrence of one of a set of discriminative stimuli (adapted from G. A. Miller); **orientation**, equivalent to attitude in its inclusive sense, "both cathectic and cognitive tendencies" (p. 393); **attraction** for orientation toward persons and attitude for orientation toward objects (p. 394); **co-orientation**; the **A-B-X system**; **symmetry** and asymmetry; **strain toward symmetry**; **perceived consensus** (A's judgment of B's orientation to X); autistic versus reality factors; constrained versus voluntary association; social reality (pp. 393-402).

**Drives/operands.** Operands: persons A and B, each both a communicator and an object of orientation, and an object X. Four relations (pp. 393-394): A's orientation to X (attitude with sign and intensity, plus beliefs and cognitive structure), A's orientation to B (attraction), B's orientation to X, B's orientation to A. A's phenomenal system adds A's *perception* of B's orientation to X (the broken lines of Fig. 2, p. 401), a second-order state. The motivational primitive is a demand for **co-orientation**: it is "an almost constant human necessity" to orient oneself to objects and to other persons oriented to the same objects, and to the degree A's orientation depends on B's, A "is motivated" to influence or inform himself about B's orientation (p. 395). No drive is named as a drive and no drive level is measured.

**Operators / equations.** **None.** Nowhere in the article is there a formula, parameter or numeric law [read in full]. The notation is signed: with respect to a given X, A and B are cathectically alike (++ or --) or different (+- or -+) in attitude and in attraction, and cognitively alike or different, with dichotomies of degree; lateral similarity of A's and B's orientations to X is called symmetry (p. 394). The one worked case writes an asymmetric system as +A:X, +A:B, -B:X, +B:A (p. 399). The operator is the communicative act, which can change any relation; for perceived asymmetry A's options are (p. 401): influence B toward one's own orientation; change one's own toward B's; cognitively distort B's; modify one's attraction toward B; modify one's judgment of one's own attraction to B; modify one's evaluation of oneself; modify one's judgment of B's evaluation of himself; tolerate the asymmetry. **The postulate (p. 395, exact):** "The stronger the forces toward A's co-orientation in respect to B and X, (a) the greater A's strain toward symmetry with B in respect to X; and (b) the greater the likelihood of increased symmetry as a consequence of one or more communicative acts." Its grounds (p. 395): symmetry brings "ready calculability of the other's behavior" (cognitive symmetry especially) and "validation of one's own orientation"; communicative acts that raise symmetry are therefore likely to be rewarded and symmetry "is likely to acquire secondary reward value".

**Theorems or derived predictions** (none proved; the author calls them testable propositions):
- P1 (p. 396): the likelihood of instigating and achieving symmetry rises with the valence of the objects of co-orientation, that is with attraction toward B and intensity of attitude toward X.
- P2 (pp. 396-397): perceived symmetry ("perceived consensus") rises with the same two variables, through a reality factor (frequency of communication) and an autistic factor (cognitive distortion toward symmetry, stronger with attraction and attitude intensity).
- P3 (p. 398): the likelihood of a symmetry-directed act is "a multiple function" of perceived discrepancy (inversely with perceived symmetry), valence toward B and valence toward X.
- P4 (p. 399): the less the attraction between A and B, the more strain toward symmetry narrows to the X's that the conditions of association require.
- P5 (p. 402): under low constraint a **circular** relation between attraction and perceived symmetry is assumed, with two resting states: great attraction and high perceived symmetry (association continues) or little attraction and low perceived symmetry (association ends); "the present theory demands this assumption of circularity". Under little freedom to leave there is no such circularity, and the independence of attraction from symmetry varies with *perceived* constraint (p. 402).
- P6 (pp. 402-403): group properties are outcomes of communicative practice: homogeneity of orientation, homogeneity of perceived consensus (pluralistic ignorance, citing Schanck 1932), and attraction among members, where Homans's liking-with-interaction proposition is amended to "varies with the degree to which the demands of co-orientation are met by communicative acts".
- The article itself lists exceptions that the propositions cannot absorb: people "agree to disagree"; a devoted husband avoids business talk; symmetry may be threatening under guilt or shame; role differentiation makes the *role system*, not X, the object of symmetry; multiple memberships can outweigh one tie (pp. 398-401). It says the propositions "rest upon the assumption of ceteris paribus" (p. 398), and cites Blake's curvilinear amendment to Homans: stresses toward interaction are stronger in both positive and negative feeling states than in neutral ones (p. 400).

**Link to Heider.** Symmetry "is taken as a special case of balance", with orientations that A attributes to B treated as Heider's acts (p. 399). Restated as our rule [ours]: write the signs of A:X, B:X and A:B; the system is symmetric when the product of the three is positive. The worked case has (+)(-)(+) = negative, asymmetric.

**Empirical standing.** The article reports **one table of its own**, and no test of the system: students in several university classes (institution not named in the passage) answered a questionnaire in April 1951 on President Truman's dismissal of General MacArthur (p. 397). Counts as printed, by own position then what the respondent attributed to "most of my closest friends" and to "most uninformed people" (pro-Truman / anti-Truman / neither): pro-Truman respondents (52) attributed to close friends 48 / 0 / 4 and to uninformed people 6 / 32 / 14; anti-Truman respondents (40) attributed to close friends 2 / 34 / 4 and to uninformed people 13 / 14 / 13. [derived] shares: 92% of pro-Truman and 85% of anti-Truman respondents said their closest friends shared their side, against 12% and 35% for "uninformed people"; 62% of pro-Truman respondents attributed the opposite side to uninformed people, and 13 of 40 anti-Truman respondents (about a third) did. N = 92 in all, one issue, one month; it supports "perceived consensus rises with attraction" and nothing about causation. The other support is cited, not shown: influence attempts vary with attraction (Festinger & Thibaut 1951; Schachter 1951; Back 1951); extremes of the attitude continuum chose friends like themselves in a replicated small-college study (Newcomb 1943); a correlation of +.72 between attraction and "conformity in attitude" in a housing project (Festinger, Schachter & Back 1950). The first real test of the system is Newcomb's own house study (section 2).

**ERA, MEDIUM, SCALE.** Data 1951 (the table), earlier community studies of the 1940s; face-to-face and questionnaire; the system is restricted to verbal, intentional, received messages between group members in continued association (p. 394). Scale: membership (attraction, influence attempts), with group properties asserted.

**Group size and composition.** The formal system is a dyad plus an object; Newcomb says the conditions and consequences "are not limited to groups of two" (p. 403) and offers no formalism for n. Composition enters only as constraint (enforced versus voluntary association), role differentiation and multiple membership.

**Consent or manipulation.** Descriptive; influence attempts are one of A's options (p. 401), with no stance on consent.

**Operand/operator translation [ours].** Operands: persons; an object or issue X per person-pair (an instrument in v0.1's sense: a per-person stance); the signed relation a_AB; and a **second-order state**, A's estimate of B's stance on X, which v0.1 lacks. Operators: *communicate* (A about X to B), changing B's stance and perceived consensus with a probability that rises with attraction, attitude intensity and perceived discrepancy; *perceive*, a noisy, attraction-biased reading of the other's stance; *resolve*, the option list above. Newcomb's postulate is a coupling between attraction and agreement that runs both ways, so the sign of a tie is state, not input (section 9).

## 2. Newcomb (1956): from A-B-X to the house study [read]

Not a seed, but the only account of the study that I could read at source, and the place where "similarity" is stated as a proposition about *perceived* similarity.

**Citation.** Newcomb, T. M. (1956). The prediction of interpersonal attraction. *American Psychologist* 11(11): 575-586 (APA presidential address, Chicago, September 1956), doi:10.1037/h0046141 (Crossref-confirmed).

**Propositions (p. 578).** First: communicators tend to become more similar to each other, at least momentarily, than before the communication. Second, in his words: "Attraction toward a co-communicator (actual or potential) varies with perceived similarity of attitudes toward the object of communication." Rationale: accurate communication has been rewarding far more often than punishing across a life history, so reward value attaches to the co-communicator and increased similarity becomes a goal of communication whose achievement is rewarding. Conditions: the object must be valued (either way), and the more so the more it is of common relevance to the two. He treats complementarity as a special case of similarity (p. 579), and says he regards similarity of attitudes as "a necessary rather than a sufficient condition" which he *believes* accounts for more of the variance in attraction than any other single variable (p. 579). He calls the general "birds of a feather" notion "not very useful" because indiscriminate: similarity predisposes attraction "to the degree that those characteristics are both observable and valued" (p. 577). Attraction and perceived similarity are said to "maintain a constant relationship because each of them is sensitive to changes in the other" (p. 579).

**Design (pp. 579-580).** A student house rented for one semester; male transfer students, all strangers to the University of Michigan, no two from the same city or school; **17** men (the house capacity), all arriving within 24 hours; free room in return for four or five hours a week of questionnaires, interviews and experiments; roommates drawn from a hat in year 1, assigned on experimental criteria in year 2; the whole procedure repeated a year later with a comparable group; findings reported mainly from year 2. Measures: "General Liking" (each man's direct ratings of the others) and a "clique" measure (each man's reports of informal subgroups, who spends time with whom). Project calendar years are not stated in the text. Data "at semi-weekly intervals", 15 weeks (Fig. 4 caption).

**Results.**
- Roommate assignment, the forward test (p. 580): half the year-2 pairs were assigned to give minimal attraction, half maximal. "Our predictions received no support whatever": roommates, including those predicted low, were more attracted than non-roommates from the start and through all 15 weeks; by the last week 90% of inter-roommate choices were in the upper three-eighths. In year 1 (random assignment) end-of-semester roommate attraction was only slightly above that between non-roommates. The criteria are not stated in this text.
- Reciprocated liking (p. 581): predictable from the fourth day; estimates of others' liking for oneself match own liking, with median rank-order correlations .75 at the start and .86 at the end; two of every three estimates are in the correct half; accuracy does not improve for most men with acquaintance.
- Perceived agreement about the self (p. 582): attraction is closely related to perceived agreement; only two subjects fail to show it in the predicted direction and only one of those reverses it.
- Similarity of how two men liked the other 15 men, within-pair, 136 pairs per year (pp. 582-583): barely significant on day 4, rising to two-thirds of within-pair attractions correctly placed in the upper or lower half by the end of four months; it predicts mutuality at the extremes, and pairs with exactly one member in the lowest quarter average r = .35 (44 pairs; not different from zero).
- The three rejects (p. 583): the three most rejected men of the second year, disliked as no one else was, liked each other even less than others liked them, yet agreed with each other about the remaining men slightly more than the average pair (their mutual profile correlations averaged .52 against .39 for the same men with everyone else but each other). Newcomb: attitudinal similarity can arise among people "in the same predicament facing the same objective world, quite independently of one another's influence". **Similarity without attraction.**
- Combined predictors (p. 583): estimated reciprocal and within-pair similarity correlate .60 (contingency); jointly with actual attraction the coefficient is .53, "almost one-third of the variance"; the estimated reciprocal alone selects 97% of the highest quarter of attractions in its upper half. In-sample.
- **Attitude similarity toward issues (p. 585):** "no single non-person objects of sufficient relevance for all members to account for very much variance"; agreement on Allport-Vernon values related to attraction over the 136 pairs with significance between .05 and .01 depending on the measures; semantic harmony related to at least one measure; a count of the objects on which two men agreed predicted attraction "for at least one of our two sets of subjects", though apparently not at levels below .05, "one of the few measures of pre-acquaintance similarity" that predicted later attraction. For outsiders who presented one viewpoint on a controversial topic, liking correlated closely with perceived agreement (as predicted).
- Newcomb's own conclusion (p. 585): "if one wants uncomplicated findings, one should stick to brief, laboratory-like, rather than long-term, 'natural,' situations". He also notes that the homogeneity of his men in age, sex and student status probably reduced attitude variance, so predictors "would prove still more satisfactory with more varied groups" (p. 585): a composition claim, untested here.

**Operators/equations.** None. The two propositions are verbal.

**Empirical standing.** In-sample, two cohorts of 17, one university, one decade; predictions that rested on attitude content were weak or failed; the strong predictors are relational (reciprocity, profile similarity), which are themselves ties. Era: 1950s; medium face-to-face with questionnaires; scale: membership within a group of 17; n = 17 and 136 pairs per year; composition fixed (young male transfer students). Consent: volunteers who knew they were in a study; assignment of roommates was by the experimenters (the criteria and whether the predictions were disclosed are not stated).

**Translation.** The strongest [read] sign predictor in the Newcomb house is **agreement about third parties** (similarity of liking profiles), the object-vertex form of the product rule with X a person. This is the same kind of regularity as D&K's mechanism 3 in file 104 (§3.4), and the rejects are the case where it coexists with mutual dislike.

## 4. Byrne: the paradigm [read] and the law [sa]

### 4.1 Byrne (1961) [read]

**Citation.** Byrne, D. (1961). Interpersonal attraction and attitude similarity. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 62(3): 713-715, doi:10.1037/h0044721 (Crossref-confirmed); University of Texas; received 25 May 1960; part read at the Southwestern Psychological Association, Galveston, 1960; supported by a University of Texas Research Institute grant.

**Mechanism as Byrne states it (p. 713).** Once interaction has begun, reciprocal reward and punishment decide (citing Newcomb 1956). A special case is perceived similarity: "persons in our culture have well established learned drives to be logical and to make a correct report of the environment", and it is "primarily through consensual validation that we determine whether we or anyone else is logical or correct". So agreement is a rewarding interaction and one element of a positive relationship; disagreement is punishing, raising the possibility that "we are to some degree stupid, uninformed, immoral, or insane" or that the other person is. He adds that the importance of the issue probably contributes.

**Hypotheses (p. 713).** (a) A stranger known to hold attitudes like the subject's is better liked; (b) is judged more intelligent, informed, moral and adjusted; (c) similarity on issues important to the subject, with dissimilarity on unimportant ones, beats the reverse.

**Design (pp. 713-714).** An attitude scale of 26 issues chosen from a pilot, seven-point scales, from extremely important (integration, God, premarital sex relations) to minor (western movies, television programs, classical music, politics); **64** introductory-psychology students at the University of Texas (36 male, 28 female); each marked the 13 most and 13 least important issues. Two weeks later they were **falsely told** the scale had been part of a study of interpersonal prediction and that they would receive another student's (name removed) answers; the questionnaire was made up by the experimenter. Four random groups: answers identical to the subject's (SA, n = 17), exactly opposite (DA, 17), similar on the most important issues and dissimilar on the least (SIA, 15), the reverse (SUA, 15). Outcomes: seven-point attraction ratings ("how well they felt they would like this person", enjoyment of working with him or her as a partner) and four evaluation scales (intelligence, knowledge of current events, morality, adjustment).

**Results (p. 714, Tables 1 and 2, as printed; means with the SD in brackets where legible).**

## 5. McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Cook (2001), "Birds of a feather" [read]

**Citation.** McPherson, M., Smith-Lovin, L., & Cook, J. M. (2001). Birds of a feather: homophily in social networks. *Annual Review of Sociology* 27: 415-444, doi:10.1146/annurev.soc.27.1.415 (Crossref-confirmed). A review; there is no new data and **no equation**.

**Their vocabulary.** **Homophily**: "a contact between similar people occurs at a higher rate than among dissimilar people" (p. 416). **Status homophily** (informal, formal or ascribed status: race, ethnicity, sex, age, religion, education, occupation) versus **value homophily** (values, attitudes, beliefs), after Lazarsfeld & Merton (1954) (p. 419). **Baseline homophily**: what the demography of the potential-tie pool produces; **inbreeding homophily**: homophily over and above that baseline, which includes choice, but also structure below the population level (foci), correlated dimensions (Blau's "consolidation") and personal preference, so "it does not in any sense indicate choice or agency purified of structural factors" (p. 419 and fn. 5). Foci of activity (Feld 1981): geography, family, organisations, isomorphic roles. Choice homophily, a concept the review credits to McPherson & Smith-Lovin (1987) (fn. 5, p. 419, where "induced" appears only as a verb); K&W credit the choice/induced pair to the same 1987 paper and use it for the same split.

**Drives/operands.** Operands: persons as nodes with attributes; ties of many types (marriage, confiding, friendship, advice, co-membership, mere contact). The one psychological mechanism offered (p. 435): people choose to interact with similar others for "ease of communication, shared cultural tastes", citing Carley's constructuralism (people who share knowledge are more likely to interact) and the experimental literature (Huston & Levinger 1978) in a sentence. No drive vocabulary.

**Operators/equations.** None. One counting identity: a group of n members creates n(n - 1)/2 co-membership ties (p. 432). Coleman's index appears in Currarini et al. (section 6).

**Results as stated (caveat, ours: the review does not give the data years of the studies it cites).**
- Rank of divides (abstract, p. 415): race and ethnicity strongest, then age, religion, education, occupation and gender, "in roughly that order".
- Race: in a national probability sample only 8% of adults with confidant networks of two or more name a confidant of another race, "less than one seventh the heterogeneity" random choice would give; excluding kin, a quarter of the potential (p. 420; Marsden 1987). Third graders form two thirds of the cross-race friendships chance predicts; by middle school 10% (Shrum et al. 1988; p. 421). Rank-order correlation -.821 between an ethnic group's size and its tendency to pick friends within the group (Laumann 1973; p. 422): smaller groups are more inbred beyond baseline.
- Sex: confidants about 70% as sex-heterogeneous as the population; 22% have no cross-sex confidant (Marsden 1987; p. 423). Age: 38% of Detroit men's close friends within two years of their age, 72% within eight (Fischer 1977; p. 424). Education: about half the diversity of the population in confiding networks (Marsden 1987; p. 426).
- Foci: 88% of third graders' friendships are within their own grade (Shrum et al. 1988; p. 431); organisational foci produce ties "twice as homogeneous as would be expected by chance" (Feld 1982; p. 433); 809 of 4,423 close confiding relations in the General Social Survey were formed at work, "roughly half of the nonkin ties" (Marks 1994; p. 432); an average male voluntary-group membership generates 37 co-membership ties, 8 of them female; a female one 29, 4 male (in a sentence citing McPherson & Smith-Lovin 1986; p. 432).
- **Selection versus influence** (pp. 428-429): longitudinal data from the 1970s shifted the reading of behavioural and value homophily to *selection* into similar friends over peer influence (Cohen 1977; Kandel 1978); "selection almost certainly trumps influence or attrition" for political orientation too; "much of what appears to be value homophily" is misperception of friends' attitudes (people assume friends are like them; disagreements are not discussed).
- **Tie dissolution** (pp. 435-437): cross-sex and cross-race school friendships are more likely to be dropped, especially when intransitive (Hallinan & Williams 1989; Tuma & Hallinan 1979); the more a member differs from a group's niche, the likelier they leave (Popielarz & McPherson 1995); dissolution mimics formation "in a somewhat weaker manner", "given the paucity of evidence".
- Time and medium: religious homophily is reported to be decreasing and educational homogamy increasing in the US (Kalmijn 1998; pp. 425-427); new technologies "may have allowed people greater latitude" for homophily on other dimensions but have not removed geography (p. 430).
- The review's stance (p. 435): it "focused overwhelmingly on the structural sources ... because the literature routinely shows the potency of such forces when compared directly with some type of personal choice or selection".

**Group size and composition (stated).** "The relative size of groups in the pool of potential contacts" powerfully affects the in-category share (p. 418); majorities have more homogeneous networks, minorities more heterogeneous ones in skewed workplaces, yet minorities show more *inbreeding* homophily than baseline predicts (pp. 420-422); co-membership ties grow as n(n - 1)/2 (p. 432); group size, consolidation of dimensions and diversity affect how much of a voluntary group's tie formation is homophilous (p. 433, McPherson & Smith-Lovin 1987).

**ERA, MEDIUM, SCALE.** The review spans studies from the 1920s to the 1990s, mostly face-to-face and survey reports of ties; the data years of individual results are not stated in the review (it mentions a General Social Survey network module in fn. 16, p. 437, without a year, and cites Marsden 1987 as a national probability sample). Scale: membership (who ties to whom) and population-level network composition.

**Empirical standing, blunt.** A literature map, not a test. Its main empirical claim, that structure explains most of homophily, rests on cross-sectional ties it calls inadequate ("cross-sectional data on networks can never answer" the formation-versus-dissolution question, p. 437) and on the few longitudinal studies it cites. Value and attitude similarity occupy one paragraph, said to be often "derivative of social positions themselves" (p. 419) and "more specific to certain types of networks and/or derived from the basic facts of sociodemographic homophily" (p. 429). The lab literature on attitude similarity is cited in one sentence and not weighed.

**Operand/operator translation [ours].** Operands: attribute vectors split into status and value blocks; foci (groups) with size and composition; ties. Operators: *expose* (foci put pairs in contact), *select* (bias toward similar within contact), *dissolve*. The baseline/inbreeding split is the algebra's first decomposition of a tie probability into an opportunity term and a bias term; the next section gives it numbers.

### 6.1 Kossinets & Watts (2009), "Origins of homophily in an evolving social network" [read]

*Am. J. Sociol.* 115(2): 405-450, doi:10.1086/599247. **Data:** one academic year of a large US university's e-mail (30,396 students, faculty and staff; 7,156,162 single-recipient messages over 270 days; 210 daily snapshots; a tie is reciprocal exchange within 60 days); merged with attributes and course registration. The calendar years are **not stated** in the AJS text; the full data span two calendar years (app. B). **Medium:** e-mail, communicative ties, *unsigned* (no liking is measured; no negative ties). **Scale:** membership (tie formation) in a community of about 30 thousand. **Similarity:** an additive count of matches on six attributes (gender, status, field, age, year, from the US), range 0-6, not attitudes.

**Model [read].** log[p/(1 - p)] = b0 + b1 S_ij + e, odds ratio exp(b1), case-control sampled (about 100,000 cases and twice as many controls). Average daily probability of forming a tie for an unconnected pair, 5 x 10^-6.
- Model 1, all pairs: b1 = .638 (SE .004), OR 1.89; the text: a highly similar pair (S = 6) forms ties about 50 times faster than a highly dissimilar one (S = 0) and about 13 times faster than an average one (S = 2) (1.89^6 is about 46 and 1.89^4 about 13 [derived]).
- Conditioning on a shared implicit focus: OR 1.03 (p = .05, b = .028). On network distance with no focus: d = 2: 1.26; d = 3: 1.34; d = 4: 1.64; d >= 5: 2.02. Focus and distance together: 1.06, 1.03, 1.11, 1.26. The authors' reading: "the higher average similarity of easily available options is the primary determinant of who forms a tie with whom—not a strong preference for similar others among the alternatives available"; similarity matters for distant pairs (at d = 4 a highly similar pair is about 20 times likelier than a dissimilar one, but the underlying rate is about one in 10^7).
- Structural channels: roughly 60% of new ties form by triadic closure and 30% by focal closure; sharing a class raises tie formation by a factor of about 50; adjacent pairs are about 40% more similar than average.
- Selection into the opportunity sets is itself similarity-biased: OR 1.55 for acquiring a mutual acquaintance (Table 4, all pairs; 0.99 within a shared focus) and 2.60 for sharing a focus next semester (Table 5, no focus the previous semester; 1.26 given one).
- Dissolution: OR 0.97 per similarity point overall, 1.01 within a shared focus (Table 6); the dissolution rate is about 0.01 a day.
- Their conclusion: choice and induced homophily "act as substitutes, each reinforcing" the other; a weak preference compounded over "generations" of biased selection into proximate positions can produce striking homophily (a cumulative-advantage argument they did not simulate). Limits they state: race and economic status not coded; a university community may be homogeneous on education; e-mail content not available; one academic year reported.

**Standing.** Observational, one institution, one year, e-mail; the strongest large-sample decomposition of choice and opportunity in this file, and it says the preference term is small once opportunity is held fixed. Consent: identifiers encrypted and message contents not recorded; the paper does not report individual consent (informed consent appears only as a possible future design, in its discussion).

**Vocabulary and operands.** Choice homophily, induced homophily, structural proximity, cyclic and triadic closure, focal closure, explicit foci (classes) and implicit foci (co-recipients of at least 140 bulk e-mails a semester, the 99th percentile), risk set. Operands: persons with six attributes, ties, foci. No drive vocabulary; a latent individual propensity to prefer similar others is the only motivational term. **Translation [ours]:** *expose* (focal and triadic closure) times *accept* (an odds ratio per similarity point), with the accept slope measured conditional on exposure; and exposure itself is similarity-biased, since people select into foci and acquaintance circles by similarity (Tables 4-5), a second-order *select* operator.

### 6.2 Currarini, Jackson & Pin (2010), "Identifying the roles of race-based choice and chance in high school friendship network formation" [read; model paper 2009 not read]

*PNAS* 107(11): 4857-4861, doi:10.1073/pnas.0911793107. **Data:** Add Health, grades 7-12, the 1994-1995 school year, friendship nominations in **84 schools**; face-to-face school friendship. **Scale:** membership and group composition. **Dimension:** race (Asian, Black, Hispanic, White, other).

**Model [read, as printed in prose].** A student's utility from friendships is increasing in same-type friends s_i and different-type friends d_i with a parameter gamma_i in (0, 1) (a different-type friend is worth gamma_i of a same-type one) and diminishing returns alpha; the net utility with search cost c is (t_i q_i + gamma_i (1 - q_i) t_i)^alpha - c t_i, t_i = s_i + d_i total friends and q_i the share of same-type meetings. Meetings are biased by beta_i >= 1 (beta = 1 unbiased): a group that is half of the pool with beta = 2, 3, 6 meets itself at about 0.7, 0.8, 0.9 (0.5^(1/beta): .71, .79, .89 [derived]). Identification: the more own-type-biased the preference (lower gamma), the more the number of friends responds to the same-type meeting rate, and that sensitivity identifies gamma; the meeting biases are identified from the gap between the types met and the stocks present. The Coleman homophily index is (q_i - w_i)/(1 - w_i), w_i the group's share of the school. [Validation, 2026-11-01: the typeset PDF, author-hosted on M. O. Jackson's Stanford page, prints Eq. 1 as U_i(s_i, d_i) = (s_i + gamma_i d_i)^alpha and Eq. 2 as q_i = (M_i / sum_k M_k)^(1/beta_i), matching the prose forms above.]
- Table 1 (all schools): alpha .55; gamma Asian .90, Black .55, Hispanic .65, White .75, other .90; beta Asian 7, Black 7.5, Hispanic 2.5, White 1, other 1. Both biases significant and race-dependent (F tests): Asians and Blacks meet their own race at rates the authors put at more than 7 times Whites'.
- **Group size:** the homophily index is non-monotone in the group's share of a school, **lowest for very large or very small groups and highest for intermediate ones** (Fig. 1). Splitting at 1,000 students, **meeting bias is significantly higher in large schools** (F = 4.71, 99% level; beta Black 9.0 against 6.0, Hispanic 6.5 against 2.0, Asian 3.0 against 6.5) while **preference bias does not differ significantly** by size (F = 1.39 against a 95% threshold of 2.23). Splitting by county income (1990 census, $30,000), preference biases differ significantly and meeting biases do not.

**Standing.** Revealed-preference identification from cross-school variation, no intervention; the authors' policy remark is that smaller schools offer integration advantages. Era 1994-95, race rather than attitudes.

**Vocabulary and operands.** Preference bias (gamma), meeting bias (beta), homophily index, revealed preference; operands: students typed by race, same-type and different-type friendships, a meeting pool; the only motivational term is a utility with diminishing returns in friends. **Translation [ours]:** the same factorisation as Chetty's below in two parameters: *expose* is beta (size-dependent), *accept* is gamma (not size-dependent).

### 6.3 Lewis, Gonzalez & Kaufman (2012), "Social selection and peer influence in an online social network" [read]

*PNAS* 109(1): 68-72, doi:10.1073/pnas.1109739109. **Data:** a diverse US college cohort on Facebook, **n = 1,640 at wave 1**, annual waves March 2006 to March 2009 (freshman to senior year); explicit mutual "friendships", read by the authors as "weak ties". **Medium:** online. **Scale:** membership. **Model:** stochastic actor-based coevolution of ties and tastes (music, movies, books) [objective function a weighted sum of effects; the display is an image].
- Friendship evolution (n = 1,001): log-odds of becoming and staying friends +0.98 for the same building, +0.56 for the same academic field, +0.10 per shared friend (and compounding); self-segregation by gender, race, region and socioeconomic status.
- Coevolution (n = 211 music, 201 movies, 191 books): **selection** on taste is significant for 4 of 15 taste clusters (lite/classic rock, classical/jazz, dark satire, raunchy comedy/gore), for none of the five book clusters; **influence** significant for 1 of 15 (classical/jazz), and "indie/alt" shows influence in the opposite direction. Tastes shared by "everyone" ("pop" music, "hit" movies, books on "the college bookshelf") show "the least evidence for social selection, positive or negative". A footnote adds that, restricted to the first period (freshman to sophomore year), there is "some evidence for selection and influence with respect to book tastes" [validation, 2026-11-01].
- The authors' reading: the social weight of a taste "may depend first on its medium ... and second on the particular content". **Vocabulary and operands.** Social selection (homophily) and peer influence (diffusion), taste clusters, stochastic actor-based model, micro-steps; operands: students, mutual friendships, sets of tastes. **Translation [ours]:** two separate operators, *select* (tie formation depends on taste overlap) and *influence* (taste adoption depends on friends' tastes), each estimated per taste cluster, both mostly null; the influence operator is the nearest empirical counterpart to Newcomb's first proposition (communicators grow more alike), with a very different tie (Facebook friendship, not conversation) and time-scale (years): little convergence.

### 6.4 Chetty, Jackson, Kuchler, Stroebel et al. (2022), "Social capital II: determinants of economic connectedness" [read, main text]

*Nature* 608: 122-134, doi:10.1038/s41586-022-04997-3. **Data:** 70.3 million Facebook users aged 25-44 in the US as of 28 May 2022 (82% of that age group), friendships allocated to six settings (high school, college, religious group, recreational group, workplace, neighbourhood); Facebook used "as a proxy for real-world friendships rather than online interactions per se", so the paper does **not** speak to effects of online networks. **Scale:** crowd/society with group-level (school) composition effects. **Dimension:** socioeconomic status (a machine-learned index), above versus below the median.

**Definitions [read].** Exposure of a group = 2 x its share of high-SES members. Friending bias = 1 - (share of one's friends who are high-SES) / (share of the group that is high-SES); positive means fewer high-SES friends than random mixing in the group would give. Economic connectedness (EC) of a low-SES person = 2 x the share of high-SES friends. From these, friend share = group share x (1 - bias), so **EC = exposure x (1 - friending bias)**, which the paper's Table 1 note states for ZIP codes [identity].
- Given that high-SES people have more friends, the no-homophily value of friending bias is -11%.
- **Decomposition** (Fig. 3a): average EC 0.826 for low-SES and 1.534 for high-SES (gap 0.708; Fig. 3a bar labels, which the text rounds to 0.83, 1.53 and 0.7). Equating friending rates across settings closes 12% of the gap; also equating exposure raises EC to 1.209, 54% in all; equating friending bias closes the other 46%. Across ZIP codes (Fig. 3b): 73% exposure, 16% friending bias, 11% friending rates. Setting means for low-SES: friending bias +0.17 in neighbourhoods, -0.03 in religious groups.
- **Group size and composition:** friending bias is "generally lower in smaller schools"; higher with more academic tracking; higher in more racially diverse schools; **non-monotone in the high-SES share, highest near an equal mix**; higher in larger groups in all six settings (Supplementary Fig. 1, not read); in colleges it also rises with size and diversity.
- **Intervention-grade result** (Fig. 6): across cohorts within a school, a 10-point rise in the high-SES share of a cohort raises the high-SES share of low-SES students' friends by 8.9 points (slope .89, SE .005); the marginal friending bias .11 equals the cross-sectional mean of bottom-to-top parental-SES-quintile friending bias (also 11%). The effect of exposure falls with friending bias estimated from *other* cohorts (slope -.61, SE .12; attenuation-corrected about -.91). A regression discontinuity on school-entry cut-offs gives a jump in high-SES friendships .06 larger in low-bias than high-bias schools. Reliability of school-level friending bias estimates: .58 (exposure .99).
- Policy examples the paper discusses (not tested here): smaller learning "houses" at Berkeley High from 2018, merged cafeterias at Lake Highlands High, programmes creating new venues for cross-class contact. The paper stresses that friending bias is "a statistical sense", not a statement about preferences: it may come from tracking or buildings.

**Standing.** The best out-of-sample and quasi-experimental evidence in this file, for a structural lever. Era 2022 snapshot with birth cohorts 1990-2000 for schools; the interventions work openly, by changing composition and group structure.

**Vocabulary and operands.** Economic connectedness, exposure, friending bias, SES, setting; operands: persons with an SES rank, groups in six settings with size and composition, friendships. No motivational term; friending bias is explicitly a statistical description. **Translation [ours]:** *expose* times *accept* with groups as operands carrying size and composition; the lever is composition (and group structure), not persuasion.

### 6.5 Anderson, Goel, Huber, Malhotra & Watts (2014), "Political ideology and racial preferences in online dating" [read]

- Reading: similarity operates below stated preference and survey self-report. - **Published critique** [added in validation, 2026-11-01]. Lewis, K. (2015). Studying online behavior: comment on Anderson et al. 2014. *Sociological Science* 2: 20-31, doi:10.15195/v2.a2 (Crossref-confirmed) [abstract and opening page read]: the article "inadequately describes the study sample", how the website operates and its central measures, so that its claims are hard to evaluate and, on Lewis's reading, "impossible to replicate". The comment's abstract objects to reporting, not to a specific estimate; nothing further was read.

**Vocabulary and operands.** Stated versus revealed preference, "nice-to-have" and "must-have", relative risk and odds ratio of viewing a profile; operands: users (sex, race, ideology and about fifteen profile attributes), candidate profiles; no drive vocabulary. **Translation [ours]:** *accept* measured by revealed choice among a given pool; a stated preference is a poor parameter for it (people who state none still show odds of 2 to 3).

### 6.6 One-line summary of what these say together [read unless marked]

When similarity is the only available information about an unseen other (Byrne 1961) it moves liking enormously. When people choose among those who are in front of them (Newcomb 1956 house; K&W e-mail; Chetty), most of what looks like homophily is who is in front of them, and the part that is bias depends on the setting's size, tracking and composition. Where similarity does matter inside a setting, the operand is a perception, and the dimension that matters (race, class, party, taste cluster) is setting- and era-dependent.

## 7. What changes with group size and composition (collected)

- **Byrne:** dyad only; no n.
- **Newcomb 1953:** dyad plus object; "not limited to groups of two" with no formalism. **1956:** n = 17; the men knew so much about each other's attitudes that no single issue was crucial for all pairs (the inference that any one X's weight dilutes as the number of known issues grows is ours); homogeneity of the sample (age, sex, status) likely reduced attitude variance, predictors "still more satisfactory with more varied groups" [read].
- **McPherson et al.:** relative group size sets baseline homophily; minorities more inbred beyond baseline (rank correlation -.821 between group size and in-group selection, Laumann); group size, consolidation and diversity moderate how homophilous a voluntary group's ties are.
- **Currarini et al.:** the homophily index is non-monotone in a group's share of a school; **meeting bias, not preference bias, is significantly higher in large schools** (a split at 1,000 students, not a gradient; higher for Blacks and Hispanics, lower for Asians); preference biases vary by race and by local income.
- **Chetty et al.:** friending bias rises with group size in all six settings and with diversity and tracking, peaks near a 50/50 composition; the exposure effect is linear (slope .89) and shrinks where bias is high.
- **K&W:** n enters through the sizes of opportunity sets: roughly 60% of ties by triadic closure, 30% by focal closure.
- **Consequence [ours, consistent with v0.1 §5 and file 091]:** in the two sources that test it (Currarini, Chetty), n and composition act on the **meeting or exposure operator and on the friending-bias factor of the group's structure**, not on a person's preference; the one place that separates the two directly (Currarini) finds the preference parameter not significantly size-dependent. "n enters only through the composition operators" survives this tradition. What does not survive is the idea that composition is a *property of the person*: the same person has different friending bias in a school than in a religious group (Chetty Fig. 2d).

## 9. Translation for the algebra [ours; no source states these forms]

**Sorts v0.1 would need beyond person, relation, group:** (a) an **issue or object** X as an instrument, with a per-person stance (v0.1 already carries one scalar per instrument); (b) a **focus** g with size n_g and composition w_g (K&W, Chetty); (c) a **second-order state**, a person's estimate of another's stance (Newcomb 1953's broken lines, 1956's estimated reciprocals).

**Operators.**
- *Expose* (foci put pairs in contact): the probability that i meets a type-h peer is the focus's share of h, scaled by a meeting bias that grows with n_g and diversity (Currarini, Chetty).
- *Accept* (tie formation given contact): friend share = peer share x (1 - b), where b is a setting-specific friending bias (Chetty identity, [read]). This is where a similarity preference could sit; K&W says its slope is near zero within a shared focus, Byrne says it is steep with no foci.
- *Communicate* (Newcomb): shifts the receiver's stance toward the sender's with probability rising in attraction, attitude intensity and perceived discrepancy; this is v0.1's D2 coupling with an attraction-dependent weight.
- *Perceive*: perceived similarity is a noisy, attraction-biased function of actual stance; the loop a_ij <-> perceived similarity is Newcomb's circularity.

**What v0.1 can and cannot take from this.**
- A sign law of the form sign(a_ij) = sgn(g(perceived similarity) - theta) with g increasing is supported **qualitatively** by the lab (Byrne 1961) and the house (Newcomb 1956), and is flat given foci in the one large free-choice test (K&W). **No [read] number fixes theta.** The only candidate neutral point (X about 0.25 of attitudes agreeing) is [derived from sa] and cannot feed an equation.
- Because the sign now depends on perceived similarity, which depends on stance, signs are **state**. v0.1's Altafini-type dichotomy (§4.2) holds the signs fixed; that hypothesis is not supported at acquaintance. File 104 reports slightly above 30% of dyadic ties changing state (including to and from null; no sign-to-sign transition rate is printed) at the first transition in the Newcomb data, falling over about seven weeks; K&W find dissolution nearly independent of similarity (OR .97). So fixed signs are a hypothesis for *established* ties, not for new groups.
- The opportunity term is a **group-structure operand**, not a drive: it needs foci, size and composition as operands with their own update rules (assignment, tracking, buildings), which is the lever Chetty found to work.
- The formal cousins already in the repo: bounded confidence (file 05 §6-7) and Axelrod similarity (named as a guard in file 05's closing list, [sa] there; not read here), Schelling tolerance (file 05 §13), the homophily/contagion confound (file 064 §5), Blau-style size effects (file 091). Byrne's linear law and the bounded-confidence threshold are two functional forms for the same guard, "interaction weight as a function of similarity", with different empirical status: the threshold form is untested here, the linear form is the lab result.

**A test the Newcomb data could run [ours, when fetched per file 104 §5]:** within-pair similarity of the liking profile toward the other 15 men predicts within-pair rank beyond reciprocity, rises with time, and fails for the three rejects (Newcomb 1956 reports all three).

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

**Partly, and the part that reduces is the part least measured.**

*What reduces.* The originators' mechanism for the **choice** component is motivational in their own words. Newcomb 1953: a demand for co-orientation, with symmetry valued for two payoffs, **predictability of the other's behaviour** and **validation of one's own orientation**, and acquiring "secondary reward value" (p. 395). Byrne 1961: "well established learned drives to be logical and to make a correct report of the environment", satisfied by consensual validation, with disagreement threatening the self ("stupid, uninformed, immoral, or insane"). In the algebra's drive vocabulary that is a *learned validation or epistemic-security need* plus an *uncertainty-reduction (predictability) need*, with the aversive side a threat to the self's standing (compare files 41 and 056, not re-read here). Newcomb's dynamic form, attraction raising the strain toward symmetry and symmetry raising attraction, is already a two-variable coupling with a self-consistent resting state.

*What does not.*
1. **Opportunity and exposure.** Chetty: about half of the class gap is exposure; K&W: shared foci absorb nearly all of the similarity effect (OR 1.89 to 1.03); McPherson: structure dominates choice. This is a property of the group's composition and size, not of any person's drive.
2. **Friending bias as institution.** It is higher in larger and more diverse groups, with tracking, and differs by setting for the same person (Chetty); the paper insists it is statistical, not a preference.
3. **Perception.** The predictive operand in existing relationships is *perceived* similarity, which is distorted toward attraction (Newcomb's autistic factors; Newcomb 1956's near-self-correlation of estimates and own liking; Tidwell, Montoya [from abstract]). That is a belief operand with its own noise, not a drive.
4. **The content and weight of the similarity dimension** (attitudes, race, class, party, taste cluster) is cultural and era-dependent (Lewis; Iyengar & Westwood [from abstract]).
5. **Agreement on third parties** (the best predictor in the Newcomb house) is relational structure, and the rejects show it can coexist with mutual dislike.
6. **No drive is measured anywhere in this tradition.** The drive reading is the authors' interpretation, untested here (the effectance-arousal experiments are unread). So "drives as primitives" is neither supported nor refuted for the sign generator; what is refuted is the sufficiency of drives alone: a sign law needs an exposure operator and a perception operand as primitives beside any drive.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**Outcomes actually predicted.** First-impression liking for an unseen other from known attitude agreement; who forms ties with whom and the composition of friendships; liking within a small natural group from relational information. Nothing in these sources predicts a *group* outcome (cohesion, decision, split, efficiency) or a *crowd* outcome (mobilisation, opinion); Newcomb's group properties are hypotheses.

**Strength, by setting (era, medium, scale).**
- **Lab, 1959-60, paper stimuli, dyad, membership** (Byrne 1961 [read]): Personal Feelings 6.53 against 1.76; d about 7.6 [derived] for identical against opposite answers; the importance manipulation works only partly (Personal Feelings, Morality, Adjustment; not Desirability, Intelligence, Knowledge). In-sample. The linear 5.44X + 6.62 [sa] has no replication I could read; against the 1961 endpoints it is flatter at X = 0 [derived, assumption stated].
- **House, 1950s, face-to-face, 17 men x 2 years, membership** (Newcomb 1956 [read]): attitude similarity, p .05 to .01 and no issue relevant to all; relational predictors contingency .53 (about a third of the variance), in-sample; **forward test failed** (roommates assigned to maximise or minimise attraction, "no support whatever"); propinquity effect differed between the two years.
- **Meta-analysis, mixed eras and media** (Montoya 2008 [from abstract]): r = .47 actual and .39 perceived across 313 investigations; actual similarity not significant in existing relationships.
- **Free choice, e-mail, one academic year, membership** (K&W [read]): OR 1.89 per similarity point overall; 1.03 within a shared focus; dissolution OR .97.
- **Schools, 1994-95, race, membership/group** (Currarini et al. [read]): significant preference and meeting biases; meeting bias larger in big schools; preference bias not size-dependent.
- **Facebook, 2006-09, taste, membership** (Lewis et al. [read]): selection for 4 of 15 clusters, influence for 1 of 15.
- **Facebook, 2022 snapshot, class, society scale with school-level groups** (Chetty [read]): exposure and friending bias each about half the class gap; the best out-of-sample result in this file.
- **Dating site, 2009, race, membership** (Anderson et al. [read]): revealed same-race odds ratios 2.6 to 3.6; 2 to 3 even with no stated preference.

**Out-of-sample evidence.** Two designs that I would call forward predictions or held-out tests: Newcomb's roommate assignment (failed) and Chetty's cross-cohort and discontinuity designs (succeeded: the school's friending bias estimated from other cohorts predicts the effect of cohort composition; slope .89 against marginal bias .11 equal to the cross-sectional mean). The attitude-based forward test failed; the structural one held.

**What it controls, and with whom.** (i) Lab: perceived similarity moves liking, by **deception** (subjects falsely told the answers were a real student's); not a field lever. (ii) Field: **composition and group structure**, in the open and not by persuasion: raising the high-SES share of a cohort by 10 points raises the high-SES share of low-SES students' friends by 8.9 points on average, by less where friending bias is high (the authors: integration helps most where bias is low; where it is high, cross-class interaction within groups is needed); tracking, group size and buildings are proposed levers (Chetty), untested here. (iii) The strain toward symmetry gives A an influence option (Newcomb p. 401), but no source measures how well it works with or without the other's consent.

**Failures and limits.** Attitude similarity toward issues weak in the natural house; actual similarity not significant in existing relationships; similarity effect near zero given shared foci; taste selection null for most clusters and influence null for most; no signed (negative) tie measured in the large free-choice studies; magnitudes differ by an order of magnitude across settings, so a single coefficient is not defensible. The sign law is supportable as a qualitative, setting-conditioned module with an exposure operator, and as nothing more until the closed primaries (Newcomb 1961, Byrne & Nelson 1965) are read.

- **Selection over influence.** McPherson 2001 (selection "almost certainly trumps influence", 109 §5) and Lewis et al. 2012 (selection significant for 4 of 15 taste clusters, influence for 1; 109 §6.3) agree in direction.

### 5. Datedness and acceleration

- **Forms and magnitudes.** The forms look era-invariant given their hypotheses: K&W's logit and odds ratio, Currarini's utility and matching forms, Chetty's identity EC = exposure x (1 - friending bias), McPherson's n(n-1)/2 identity. The magnitudes are dated: 1.89 and 1.03 (2009 e-mail, one academic year); 7.6 (1959-60, one 64-student study); Currarini's bias parameters (1994-95 school nominations); Chetty's 0.89 slope (2022 Facebook). This is the split 112 §4 and 116 §5 use.
- **Shrinkage.** Single-study social-psychology effects shrink (113 §2; 113 §9, S1, proposed and not adopted: x0.5, slack 0.25-0.75). Byrne 1961 and the Newcomb house (17 men, one house) are in that position, so their magnitudes are upper bounds. The large free-choice studies (K&W, Currarini, Chetty) are not, but carry era and medium bounds.
- **Platform state.** Lewis (Facebook, 2006-09), Anderson (online dating, 2009) and K&W (one university e-mail year) do not record their platform's feed or recommendation state (114 §11 on the hidden parameter). Their magnitudes cannot be carried to 2022 without a re-test. Chetty (2022) measures offline friendship through Facebook as a proxy, so it does not test an online mechanism.
- **Acceleration.** Collective attention turns over faster (113 §6, Lorenz-Spreen et al. 2019). No source in the prior-work set re-estimates a homophily or tie-sign magnitude across eras (113 headline 8). Every drift claim here is a single-source report: McPherson 2001 via Kalmijn 1998 (years not stated); Iyengar & Westwood 2015 (abstract).
- **The confidant measures.** 113 §4.3 reports the GSS network item at 1985 and 2004 (McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Brashears 2006, abstract), with the fall from 2.94 to 2.08 contested as an interviewer artefact (Paik & Sanchagrin 2013, abstract). 109 §5 cites Marsden (1987) for the confidant results with no year. Whether the two share a survey wave is not established in either file. If they do, the 2001 review's confidant figures carry the same question.
- **Dimension weights.** Byrne's pilot judged politics a minor issue (109 §8). Iyengar & Westwood (2015, abstract) report party affect now as strong as race. The weight on each similarity dimension moves. 109 §8 and 113 §9 agree it must be re-estimated, not carried.
- **v0.2 implication.** Agrees with 112 §6.4 and 116 §5: each similarity law carries a validity record (era, medium, platform state, focus structure, dimension, scale, n range), and the exposure operator (foci, size, composition) is a dated input, not a constant.
