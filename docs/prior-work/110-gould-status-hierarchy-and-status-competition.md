## Summary

1. **Gould (2002) is closed and was not read; no equation of his is [read] here, and nothing in this file derives from it.** Unpaywall says closed, OpenAlex lists no repository full text, and the publisher, JSTOR, ResearchGate and Semantic Scholar are walled. The open green copy of its main follow-up (Manzo & Baldassarri 2015) sits on hosts I could not reach or that answer with bot challenges, and I did not go round them. What is known is the abstract [abs] and a user-written wiki summary [sa]: a formal network model (Takikawa's 2014 ISA abstract describes it as built "using game theory and social network theory"; Gould's own abstract does not use those words) in which each actor adjusts status-conferring gestures to the aggregate of everyone's gestures (self-reinforcing) and dislikes unreciprocated gestures (the brake); the ranking is the equilibrium; six propositions; tested, per the abstract, on task groups, a fraternity and infant quintuplets.
2. **The person-level terms in every summary I could read are an attraction to the most attractive others, a susceptibility to collective attributions and a symmetry concern. A desire for one's own status is not among them** [abs, sa]. Hierarchy there is an emergent fixed point, not the output of a status drive. If the full text says otherwise it must be read (section 1.6).
3. **Three layers exist and no source joins them.** L1, the motive: Anderson et al. 2015 [abs] (055 has the rest). L2, rank as an emergent fixed point of conferral or contest: three formal models read here (Bonabeau-type, Lacasa & Luque 2006; talent plus reinforcement, Pósfai & D'Souza 2018; triad dynamics, Chase & Lindquist 2016), and none contains a status-seeking drive. L3, the sign of ties given rank: Górski et al. 2025, which says it does "neither explain nor model the emergence of social hierarchy", and the stereotype content model.
4. **The one large human test of the central mechanism is Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006** [read, with the supplement]: 14,341 people online, 7 Oct 2004 to 8 Mar 2005, about 700 per world. Showing download counts raised the Gini coefficient of success from about 0.26 to 0.29-0.42 (counts in a grid) and from about 0.20 to 0.45-0.56 (counts in a popularity-sorted list) [fig], and raised unpredictability with it. The loop gain was set by the interface. Van de Rijt et al. 2014 [abs]: arbitrary early success helped, with decreasing returns.
5. **Group size changes the level at which hierarchy is explained.** In fish, attributes and loser effects predict dyads (the same fish dominant on re-meeting in 94% of pairs; prior losers losing 87% of contests) but not groups of four (75% of pairs; 59%, indistinguishable from chance) [sa: these are Chase, Tovey & Murch 2003's results as reported by Chase & Lindquist 2016; the 2003 paper is closed]. Groups of four cichlids separated and reassembled re-formed the identical hierarchy in only 27.3% of 22 groups (Chase et al. 2002) [read]. A random tournament is linear with probability 0.75 at n = 3 and 0.0024 at n = 7 [derived]. The formal models state their own size dependence: the egalitarian-to-hierarchical critical contact density is 4 mu (N-1) / (eta (1-mu) N (1+F)); the talent needed to pass a higher-ranked rival is delta / (mu (N-1)).
6. **Status competition producing negative ties is strong evidence at the level of group stereotypes and thin at the level of member-to-member ties.** Perceived status tracks perceived competence (r .63-.88 across twelve samples; .89 for the seven EU samples pooled) and perceived competition tracks lack of warmth (r -.02 to -.48, negative in all twelve, significant in nine) [read, undergraduates rating societal groups, ten non-US nations plus US]; envied groups draw Schadenfreude [read: Cikara & Fiske 2012, 20 students, summarised in Fiske 2010]. For ties, the status-versus-balance model of Górski et al. reproduces the under-representation of nonhierarchical ego-triads seen in the data (BN1 in 32 of 33 school networks, UN1 in 28; all three online networks) and the online over-representation of the hierarchical triad UH0 [read], but it is fitted in-sample with three strongly correlated parameters.
7. **Era.** Gould's three test datasets are, as far as I can infer, 1936-37, 1956 and an unidentified task-group set, all face to face and, where the size is known, small. The online results are 2003-2009 (the SNAP sites, via 106), 2004-05 (Salganik) and 2018-19 for part of the Spanish school data. The animal data behind the hierarchy-formation results were undated here before validation: the hens are from before 1982, and the cichlids were published in 2002-03. The reciprocity brake and the information gain are medium parameters: one is a symmetry cost that plausibly shrinks where deference is cheap [inf]; the other was moved by a layout change in the one experiment that tested it.
8. **No out-of-sample test of any status-to-hierarchy law was found.** Gould's abstract claims accurate prediction on three datasets that, per the wiki, were chosen because they fit the model's assumptions; Manzo & Baldassarri's abstract says the earlier models only partly reproduce three macro-patterns; the online results test the direction of an effect, not an equation. Two data tests of hierarchy laws do exist, and both go against the law tested: the prior-attributes law failed a reassembly test in cichlids (27.3% of 22 hierarchies came back identical; Chase et al. 2002 [read]); and the mathematical components of three widely cited winner-loser models, the family to which the section 3 models belong, "do not show satisfactory agreement" with hen data (Lindquist & Chase 2009 [abs]; the three models are not named in the abstract). Neither is a held-out prediction of a ranking.

## 1. Gould (2002): what is known without the text

### 1.1 The abstract [abs]

Paraphrase of the publisher's abstract, with a few words quoted. The article offers a formal model of how hierarchy emerges, aimed at bridging individualist and structuralist accounts of inequality. Actors reproduce a status hierarchy by adjusting their own status-conferring gestures according to collective attributions; those attributions are only the aggregate of individual gestures, which yields a "self-reinforcing status ranking". Winner-take-all hierarchies are discouraged when people prefer reciprocation of their gestures, so a ranking is an equilibrium of individual responses to the trade-off between social influence and distaste for unreciprocated gestures. Analysis of the model yields several precise predictions about network patterns at equilibrium. Data on interaction in task groups, friendship ratings in a fraternity, and play among infant quintuplets are said to show that the theory makes "unusually accurate predictions about network structure". Copyright: University of Chicago Press; paraphrased.

### 1.5 Operand and operator translation, structure only

Implied by the abstract and the wiki, not by any equation: operand i (actor); relation a_ij >= 0 (deference or attachment intensity from i to j, directed, valued, **unsigned**); a derived operand, "choice status" c_j = the aggregate of a_ij received by j. Operators: *confer* (i sets a_ij in response to c_j, to a_ji and to j's attractiveness); *aggregate* (column sums); *reciprocate* (a_ij responds to a_ji). The ranking is a fixed point of the conferral map. Two behavioural parameters (a weight on collective attributions, a distaste for asymmetry) would be the person-level inputs; their names, form and values are unread. As summarised, the model has no negative tie, so negative ties would need a separate mechanism (section 4).

## 2. Anderson, Hildreth & Howland (2015) [abs]

**Their vocabulary.** Status = the respect, admiration and voluntary deference individuals are afforded by others; distinct from power, financial success and social belongingness. The "status hypothesis": the desire for status is a fundamental motive.

**Drives and operands.** One motive; one other-held state (the status accorded). Nothing about a set point, satiation or a dynamics law is in the abstract.

**Operators and equations.** None [abs].

**Derived predictions [abs].** Wellbeing, self-esteem and mental and physical health depend on the status accorded; people vigilantly monitor status dynamics, strive to appear socially valuable, prefer environments offering higher status and react strongly to threats to it; the motive does not look like a mere derivative of the need to belong; it was observed across culture, gender, age and personality. Conclusion: "indeed fundamental". Press coverage names dissenters [sa]: Leary, Jongman-Sereno & Diebels 2014, "The pursuit of status: a self-presentational perspective on the quest for social value", in *The Psychology of Social Status*, pp. 159-178, doi:10.1007/978-1-4939-0867-7_8; and Sheldon 2011, *Psychol. Rev.* 118(4): 552-569, doi:10.1037/a0024758. Both exist (Crossref); that they dissent is not confirmed from their texts.

**Empirical standing.** A review of diverse literatures, so as strong as its sources; no effect sizes or sample sizes in the abstract; the claim of "fundamental" rests on the authors' chosen criteria. Era and medium mixed and unstated; scale: membership (individual wellbeing and behaviour). Not read beyond the abstract, so 055's reading stands unchanged.

**Translation.** `status_i` is an others-held quantity, i.e. an aggregate over deference conferred: in the Gould-type reading it is c_i, a column sum. The motive is a separate person-level scalar of unknown dynamics.

## 3. How rank emerges: the formal models read at source

All three below describe contests or conferral among agents who have no status-seeking drive of their own. Each contains a positive feedback and a brake.

| Model | State | Feedback | Brake | Control parameter | Regimes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonabeau-type (Lacasa & Luque) | status h_i | winning raises, losing lowers h; win probability logistic in h-differences | forgetting (1-mu) | contact density rho | egalitarian below rho_c, hierarchical above |
| Talent + reinforcement (Pósfai & D'Souza) | score x_i plus fixed talent b_i | winner effect | forgetting | additional talent needed, delta/(mu(N-1)) | talent-dominated, crossover, experience-dominated |
| Triad dynamics (Chase & Lindquist) | directed tournament graph | none at the individual level | intransitive triads convert to transitive much faster than the reverse | alpha / (alpha + beta) | mostly linear when alpha >> beta; stricter as n grows |

### 3.1 Lacasa & Luque (2006): the Bonabeau model in mean field [read in full, arXiv v1]

**Their vocabulary.** Agents with a "fitness" h_i they call status; competition with feedback; relaxation; egalitarian and hierarchic phases; density rho; asymmetry F.

**Operators and equations (restated).**
- Contest: agent i attacking j wins with probability P_ij = 1 / (1 + exp(eta (h_j - h_i))), eta > 0. The winner's status rises by 1, the loser's falls by F (F >= 1; F = 1 symmetric). After each sweep every status is multiplied by (1 - mu): fading memory.
- Mean field, rho read as the probability that an agent leads a contest in a step: h_i' = (1 - rho)(1 - mu) h_i + [rho (1 - mu) / (N - 1)] sum over j != i of [P_ij (h_i + 1) + (1 - P_ij)(h_i - F)].
- The egalitarian state (all h equal) is stable iff rho < rho_c(N) = 4 mu (N - 1) / [eta (1 - mu) N (1 + F)]; for N >> 1, rho_c = 4 mu / [eta (1 - mu)(1 + F)]. The transition can occur only if mu < eta (1 + F) / [4 + eta (1 + F)].
- Two agents: the steady difference h* solves h* = [rho (1 - mu)(1 + F) / mu] (1 - 2 / (1 + exp(eta h*))); one solution (0) below rho_c(2) = 2 mu / [eta (1 - mu)(1 + F)], three above (0, +a, -a): a pitchfork.
- Their figures show: raising F lowers rho_c and raises inequality; raising mu raises rho_c and lowers inequality; lowering eta raises rho_c and leaves inequality unchanged.
- Additive relaxation h -> h - mu tanh(h), the original form, gives rho_c = 2 mu / [eta (1 + F)] for two agents, with the status differences growing excessively (a numerical explosion) unless a winning increment Q is tuned.
- Stauffer's variant, with eta replaced by the status-inequality order parameter sigma: the egalitarian state is stable at every rho (eigenvalues 0 and 1 - mu) and a saddle-node bifurcation at rho_c creates a second, hierarchical stable branch. A "transition" is then seen in simulation only if the run starts inside the hierarchical basin; the authors read earlier numerical claims of a phase transition in that version as an initial-condition effect.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** The critical-density formula; the bifurcation type (pitchfork for Bonabeau, saddle-node for Stauffer); the parameter dependences above. Theory only.

**Empirical standing.** None in this paper. The model descends from wasp data (Theraulaz, Bonabeau & Deneubourg 1995, *J. Theor. Biol.* 174(3): 313-323, on *Polistes dominulus*) and was re-used as sociophysics. Credit: Bonabeau, Theraulaz & Deneubourg's 1999 abstract attributes the "double reinforcement" idea (winners reinforce their probability of winning, losers of losing) to Hogeweg & Hesper (1983, 1985) [abs]. **Bonabeau, Theraulaz & Deneubourg 1999** [abs, Europe PMC record read in validation] re-analyse earlier wasp data and show that it is *impossible* to distinguish the double-reinforcement assumption from a competing assumption based on pre-existing individual differences (before validation this file said the opposite, "can be distinguished", from a fetch-tool summary). They propose experiments to tell the two apart and caution that winner and loser effects are equally consistent with the correlational assumption. **Chase et al. 2002** [read in validation, was abs]: 22 groups of four female *Metriaclima zebra*, separated two weeks and reassembled, were linear both times (90.9% and 95.5%) but came back identical in only 27.3%. In a second experiment linear hierarchies were 56.2% (n = 16) and 50.0% (n = 12) under pairwise round-robin meetings against 92.0% (n = 25) and 90.9% (n = 11) under group assembly, for sets of four and five, against chance values of 37.5% and 11.7%. Attributes matter, but group interaction is needed for reliably linear orders. **Lindquist & Chase 2009** [abs]: the two main mathematical components of three widely cited winner-loser models were tested against hen hierarchy-formation data and "do not show satisfactory agreement"; key assumptions lack conclusive experimental support. The abstract does not say whether a Bonabeau-type model is among the three.

**Translation.** Operand: status as a real number h_i. Operators: contest (a Bernoulli draw with a logistic probability), update (+1, -F), relax (x (1 - mu)). Control parameters rho (contact density), eta (how decisive status differences are), F (loss asymmetry), mu (forgetting). Group size enters only through the factor (N - 1)/N.

**Group size [derived, eta = 1, mu = 0.1, F = 1].** rho_c = 0.111 (N = 2, matching the paper's 0.11), 0.148 (3), 0.178 (5), 0.200 (10), 0.220 (100), 0.222 (infinite N). So beyond about ten agents the model's critical density no longer depends on size; contact density does the work.

### 3.2 Pósfai & D'Souza (2018): talent plus social reinforcement [read, main text of arXiv v1]

**Their vocabulary.** Social reinforcement (the winner effect), intrinsic ability or "talent" b_i, forgetting mu, inverse temperature beta, a "fair" ranking (rank follows talent) against an "unfair" one, open populations.

**Operators and equations (restated).**
- Win probability with talent: Q_ij = 1 / (1 + exp(-beta (x_i + b_i - x_j - b_j))); each step the winner's score rises by delta, scores decay by mu: x_i' = (1 - mu) x_i + [delta / (N - 1)] sum over j != i of Q_ij (the talent-free form is Bonabeau's).
- Two individuals: 0 = -mu dx + delta [2 / (1 + exp(-beta (dx + db))) - 1]. With dx_bar = beta dx, db_bar = beta db and epsilon = mu / (delta beta): 0 = -epsilon dx_bar + 2 / (1 + exp(-dx_bar - db_bar)) - 1 (their Eq. 5; before validation the epsilon was missing here, which made the equation independent of the one parameter it is about; checked against the PDF's raw text). At db = 0 a pitchfork at epsilon_c = 1/2 (linearising, epsilon dx_bar = dx_bar / 2); at db > 0 a single "fair" solution above a critical epsilon_c(db_bar), below which a second, "unfair" solution (the less talented on top) appears through a discontinuous transition. Small differences: (epsilon_c - 1/2) scales as db_bar^(2/3); large: epsilon_c = 1/db_bar.
- The stability of a dominant-subordinate relationship (the probability Q that the dominant wins) rises with the strength of reinforcement, so stability and fairness trade off.
- Open populations, beta -> infinity, a random member replaced each step by a newcomer at the bottom: consecutive individuals reverse rank iff b(k+1) - b(k) > dx, where dx = delta / (mu (N - 1)) is the extra talent needed to overcome the advantage of rank.
- Rank-talent Kendall tau (tau_tal) and rank-experience tau (tau_exp) cross at dx_c about 0.36 for N = 100; for large N dx_c scales as 1/sqrt(N). Locally, in the crossover regime, rank and talent are anti-correlated, with universal bounds tau_tal(2) >= 1 - 2 ln 2 and p_rr <= 0.294...; removing a member can trigger a chain of rank reversals only when both effects matter.

**Empirical standing.** Theory, no fit. The authors cite a macaque study (Tung et al. 2012, not read) for a Spearman correlation of 0.61 between rank and experience, taken to show real groups near the crossover [sa], and Salganik et al. 2006 for the stability-fairness trade-off. They call the local anti-correlation and removal-induced reversals "testable through experiments"; no test is reported.

**Translation.** Adds a quality operand b_i that is not social to the reinforcement loop, and shows the loop can overrule it. Group size enters through dx = delta / (mu (N - 1)): the bigger the group, the smaller the talent gap that suffices to reorder it.

### 3.3 Chase & Lindquist (2016): hierarchies as dynamic networks, with animal data [read in full; CC BY 4.0]

**Their vocabulary.** The "standard approach" (a static distribution, individual-based explanation, an inverse problem); the "prior attributes" and "individual action" hypotheses (the latter being winner-loser models); tournament graph; transitive and intransitive triads; linear hierarchy; "dynamic network motifs".

**Operators and equations (restated).**
- The probability that a random tournament on n animals is linear is n! / 2^(n(n-1)/2).
- Rule 1: every pair interacts. Rule 2: intransitive triads convert to transitive ones at a much higher rate than the reverse. With alpha = P(intransitive -> transitive) and beta = P(transitive -> intransitive), the fraction of time a three-animal hierarchy is linear is P(T) = alpha / (alpha + beta). For n = 3 to 7 the simulated P(T) depends only on alpha / (alpha + beta); the function f_n is unknown. Keeping hierarchies linear 90% of the time needs beta to fall as n grows (n = 5, alpha = 0.95: beta = 0.008 gives 90.5%; n = 7: beta = 0.002 gives 90.9%).

**Results.**
- **Cichlids, pairs against groups [sa: read in Chase & Lindquist 2016, which reports them from Chase, Tovey & Murch 2003, *Behaviour* 140(10): 1193-1217, doi:10.1163/156853903771980558, closed; credit belongs to the 2003 paper].** Isolated pairs re-formed after two weeks: the same fish dominated in 94% of pairs; in groups of four, in 75% of pairs. A loser effect: prior losers lost 87% of contests in pairs but 59% in groups of four (not different from 50%). The figure that 73% of groups formed a different hierarchy the second time is cited to Chase et al. 2002 and the 2003 paper; Chase et al. 2002 (read in validation) gives 27.3% identical of 22 groups, i.e. 72.7% different [read].
- **Hens [read in Chase & Lindquist 2016; the analysis is Chase & Lindquist 2009, *Oxford Handbook of Analytical Sociology* pp. 566-591, on data collected for Chase 1982, *Behaviour* 80: 218-239, doi:10.1163/156853982x00364; neither opened, so [sa] for the primary].** 14 groups (of four, 56 triads) observed for about 12 hours: about half developed a non-linear hierarchy at least once; 15 of 56 triads (27%) became intransitive and all but one reverted; an intransitive triad lasted a mean 5.2 interactions against 54.3 for a transitive one, medians 2 min 4 s against 49 min 22 s (23.9 times).
- The authors call the evidence for Rule 1 excellent and that for Rule 2, from one hen dataset, tentative.

**Derived [mine, from their equation].** P(linear tournament) = 0.75 (n = 3), 0.375 (4), 0.117 (5), 0.022 (6), 0.0024 (7), 0.00015 (8): linear hierarchies stop being plausible by chance almost at once.

**Predictions.** Larger groups follow the two rules at lower levels (a smaller alpha-to-beta ratio, some pairs not interacting); animals can infer intransitivity from watching others.

**Era, medium, scale.** Hens and cichlids in the laboratory; the paper does not state data years. Found in validation: the hen data were collected for Chase 1982 (*Behaviour* 80; *Science* 216: 439-440), so **before 1982**; the cichlid data were published in 2002-03, and Chase et al. 2002 acknowledge NSF grant SES-9424006, which suggests the mid-to-late 1990s [inf]. Group scale (n = 2 to 5 measured, 3 to 7 simulated). Haba, Schwark, Weinreb, Ogundare, Foster, Tsai, Lyu, Mohamed, Chang, Arnold, Schaffer, Rajan, Datta & Abdus-Saboor 2026 (openRxiv/bioRxiv preprint, posted 2 Oct 2026, doi 10.64898/2026.09.27.754730) [abs, not peer reviewed]: machine-vision profiles of naked mole-rats' spontaneous behaviour in isolation predict pairwise dominance and rank within a colony; models trained in one colony predict rank poorly in others; ranks were stable over months to years. The authors read this as animals *expressing* an existing social position through colony-specific behaviour. Because the ranks were already established, it is not a test of whether attributes before hierarchy formation decide rank, and it does not bear on the prior-attributes result above. (Before validation this file read it as "individual traits may carry rank information that group size dilutes rather than removes".)

**Translation.** Operand: a directed tournament graph, not a person-level state. Operator: triad conversion with asymmetric rates alpha and beta. No individual drive appears.

### 3.4 Salganik, Dodds & Watts (2006): the central mechanism in a human crowd [read in full, with the supplement]

**Design.** An online "music market" (MusicLab): 48 songs by unknown bands drawn from the premium members of a band-hosting site; participants recruited mostly from a teen and young-adult site (bolt.com; about 80% from the US), told it was a study of musical taste, with informed consent; each could listen, rate 1 to 5 stars and download or not. Independent condition: no information on others. Social-influence conditions: download counts shown, in eight parallel "worlds" that never mixed. About 20% of participants went to the independent condition (about 1,400 people) and 10% to each world (about 700). The framework of the design was unknown to the subjects.

| | Experiment 1 | Experiment 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Dates | 7 Oct to 15 Dec 2004 (69 days) | 15 Dec 2004 to 8 Mar 2005 (83 days) |
| Participants | 7,149 (5,708 influence; 1,441 independent) | 7,192 (5,746; 1,446) |
| Presentation of counts | random grid, counts beside each song | one column sorted by current downloads |
| Downloads | 8,203 | 10,298 |
| Women; US residents; under 18 | 36.4%; 79.8%; 39.3% | 73.9%; 81.8%; 50.9% |

**Measures (restated).** Market share m_i = d_i / sum_k d_k. Inequality = Gini G = (sum_i sum_j |m_i - m_j|) / (2 S sum_k m_k) for S songs. Unpredictability of song i: u_i = the mean over pairs of worlds of |m_i,world a - m_i,world b|; U = the mean of u_i over songs; for the independent condition the single world was randomly split in two, 1,000 times.

**Results [read; plotted values are [fig]].**
- Gini of success, influence worlds against independent: Experiment 1 about 0.285 to 0.42 (eight worlds) against about 0.255; Experiment 2 about 0.445 to 0.56 against about 0.20. Pooled differences significant, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001 (SOM).
- Unpredictability U: Experiment 1 about 0.008 against 0.005; Experiment 2 about 0.013 against 0.004 [fig]. Validation measured the bars in pixels against the axis frame: 0.0083 and 0.0049; 0.0127 and 0.0038. The Experiment 2 independent value was given as 0.003 before validation. The Gini bars measured the same way: 0.285-0.419 (mean 0.342) against 0.258; 0.450-0.564 (mean 0.501) against 0.201, consistent with the values above.
- Quality (a song's share in the independent world) is positively related to success, but "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible" (abstract; quotation corrected to the printed wording in validation). The quality-to-success mapping is more convex in Experiment 2; the authors read part of the convexity as coming from the strength of the social signal, not from similar preferences.
- Stronger signal (a sorted list) gave more inequality and more unpredictability than a weaker one (a grid).

**Authors' limits and conjectures.** Participants could take part in one experiment only and could not share opinions; the signal was anonymous; real-world influence is "far stronger". Untested conjectures in the SOM: a larger font for counts would raise both effects; an identity-linked signal could weaken or strengthen the process; more songs would raise both; songs of more similar quality would give less inequality and more unpredictability. Also in the SOM, and relevant to era and population: re-run on a different subject pool, they expect different songs to succeed but "the overall amount of inequality and unpredictability would be similar", while suspecting the process "may be more pronounced" with their pool of US teenagers (untested). **Scale and consent.** Crowd scale (adoption). The intervention (what to display, in what order) was a design manipulation of the information environment; consent covered the study, not the conditions.

**Translation.** Operands: items with counts; operators: display (counts, order) and choose; observables G and U. The loop gain is an interface setting, so it belongs in the algebra as an input with a provenance, not as a person constant. No choice rule was fitted.

## 4. From rank to ties: status competition and negative ties

### 4.1 Górski, Sulik, Andres, Vaccario & Hołyst (2025) [read, main text; CC BY 4.0]

**Their vocabulary.** Structural balance theory (SBT) and status theory (ST) as two forms of cognitive-dissonance minimisation in signed triads; "ego-based triads"; a hierarchical triad is one from which the focal agent can build a consistent ranking; "paradise" (all links positive); quasistationary state; q (probability of judging a triad by ST rather than SBT); p_SBT ("preference for forming friendly relations"); p_ST ("preference for respecting others").

**Operators (restated).** N agents; a directed signed network with fixed edges and changing signs. At each step a random agent A takes two of its neighbours B and C and one directed tie between them (B to C), forming an ego-triad of three signed links. With probability q the triad is judged by ST, otherwise by SBT. Under SBT a triad is unbalanced if it has one or three negative links. Under ST, positive ties go from lower to higher status and negative from higher to lower, and a triad is stable iff A can place B and C in a consistent order with itself. If unstable, A changes one of its own two outgoing links (either one, when the two have the same sign); when the two have different signs the negative one is turned positive with probability p_ST (under ST) or p_SBT (under SBT), otherwise the positive one is turned negative. Agents have no attributes, know only the one extra link, and have no knowledge of the ties directed at them. The paper says explicitly that it does "neither explain nor model the emergence of social hierarchy"; it studies how existing status shapes sign formation.

**Equation 1 (restated, complete graph, quasistationary density rho of positive links).**
[2 (1 - q)(2 p_SBT - 1)] rho^2 - [2 (1 - q) + (1 - 2 p_ST) q] rho + (1 - q) = 0.
Conversion rates of nonhierarchical ego-triads under ST: BN1 -> UH0 at q p_ST and -> UH2 at q (1 - p_ST); UN1 -> BH2 at q (1 - p_ST) and -> BH0 at q p_ST.

**Stated results [read].**
- With q = 0 the agent-centred dynamics reach paradise above p_SBT* = 0.75, against 0.5 for the triad-centred dynamics of earlier models.
- Four regimes in (p_SBT, p_ST): a continuous transition to paradise when p_SBT < 0.75 and p_ST > 0.5; a discontinuous one (a fold, with a separatrix and a "fragile" paradise) when p_SBT > 0.75 and p_ST < 0.5; none when both preferences are large (always paradise) or both small (never).
- At p_ST = 0.5 the quasistationary density does not depend on q.

**Data, era, medium.** Online: Epinions (N about 44,000, about 11 million triads), Slashdot (about 27,000; 1.25 million), WikiElections (about 4,000; 745,000). The counts differ from the full networks in file 106 (131,828; 82,144; 7,118 nodes), presumably because links in no triad are dropped [inf]. If these are the SNAP files, the eras are those 106 recorded: Epinions to 12 Aug 2003, Wikipedia votes 17 Sep 2004 to 6 Jan 2008, Slashdot crawled 21 Feb 2009 [inf; read in 106]. Face to face: 13 Spanish high schools (ages 11-15), more than 3,000 students and 60,566 declared relationships, a computer survey in which each student could pick names from a list of all other students and rate each as very bad, bad, good or very good (coded -2, -1, +1, +2); 33 school-grade networks, N = 26-534, 1,015 to 143,112 triads. One school's two waves were December 2018 and May 2019 (Escribano 2021); the years for the rest are not in the main text.

**Results [read].**
- Online: nonhierarchical ego-triads are significantly under-represented and the unbalanced hierarchical triad UH0 over-represented; the status signature is strongest in Epinions.
- Schools: ego-triad BN1 is under-represented in 32 of 33 networks, UN1 in 28 of 33. In one network BN1 is 8% and BH2 73% of the relevant triads (nine times as many).
- Fitted parameters: q between 0.6 and about 1.0 for most schools; Epinions and Slashdot both p_SBT about 0 and high p_ST; WikiElections high p_SBT and intermediate p_ST. Correlations among fitted parameters: p_ST with q -0.90, p_ST with p_SBT -0.80, q with p_SBT +0.67.
- Composition: q rises with the density of weak negative and weak positive links and falls with the density of strong positive links (balance matters more where strong ties are dense; status where weak ties dominate); weak-negative density plus mean prosociality score explain 51% of the variance in q across 27 networks. The six networks dropped as outliers (high density in the triangle network) were the six smallest, four of them single classes. In a separate individual-level exploration, prosociality scores correlate 0.14 and 0.10 (both significant) with the densities of balanced and hierarchical triads, and the authors say this does not show how prosociality affects the balance-status competition. (Before validation this file attributed the 0.14 and 0.10 to mean prosociality.)

**Caveats [derived from the paper's own statements].** Three parameters are fitted per network to a handful of triad-density deviations by grid search and a mean-squared-error criterion, in-sample; the fitted parameters are strongly correlated and the authors themselves give a mechanical reason for the p_ST to p_SBT relation, so "people strive for the top when hierarchy matters" is an interpretation of a ridge in the error surface. Status here is an ordering inferred from the same signs it explains (106 raises the same circularity). The online data are the early web; the authors' claim that falling close-friendship counts and rising acquaintance counts in the US imply more negative ties in denser networks is an untested extrapolation.

**Translation.** Operand: a signed directed tie. Operator: ego-dissonance resolution with three propensities (q, p_SBT, p_ST). Propensities, not drives.

### 4.2 The stereotype content model: status and competition predict how groups are felt about [read: Cuddy et al. 2009 in full; Fiske 2010 in full]

**Their vocabulary.** Warmth (intent) and competence (capacity) as the two dimensions of social perception; perceived status and perceived competition (or interdependence); "ambivalent" stereotypes; "envious prejudice" for groups seen as competent but cold; pity for warm and incompetent; contempt or disgust for low on both; "scorn down" for low-status targets.

**Laws stated.** Perceived status predicts competence stereotypes; perceived competition predicts (lack of) warmth stereotypes; the quadrant predicts the emotion (admiration or pride, envy, pity, contempt) and the emotions predict active or passive help or harm.

**Evidence [read].** Cuddy et al. 2009: ten non-US nations, N = 1,028 by the abstract (the non-US samples in Table 1 add to 1,029 [derived]). Seven EU samples of undergraduates (41 to 199 each, 755 together, mean age about 21, 69% women, volunteers in classrooms) rated the 15 then-current EU nations on competence, warmth, status (prestige of jobs, economic success, education) and competition (special treatment making things harder for others, resources taken, power lost); three East Asian samples (Hong Kong 60, Japan 83, South Korea 91) rated indigenous groups. Status-competence r: EU combined .89 (individual samples .63 to .87 as printed), Hong Kong .87, Japan .75, South Korea .64; US sample (Fiske et al. 2002 Study 2, n = 147) .88. Competition-warmth r: EU combined -.25 (-.48 to -.02; four of seven samples p < .05), Hong Kong -.15, Japan -.17, South Korea -.39; US -.31. Table 1's note says its correlations are computed at the level of individual participants, not group means. The sentence about averaging across participants introduces the cluster analysis, not the correlations. Footnotes 3 and 6 give separate group-level (ecological) correlations, which are higher: status-competence .91 to .99 in the EU list and .88 to .99 in Study 2; competition-warmth -.32 to -.42 in Study 2. Footnote 3's EU list is garbled, naming six nations for seven values. (Before validation this file read the note and the text as unreconciled; they are not in conflict.) Fiske 2010 summarises "dozens of global samples" with status-competence about .70 or more and cooperation-warmth about .30; the paper she cites tabulates twelve samples.

Fiske 2010 reports, from her lab's studies (US undergraduate and online adult samples, 2000s), the following; credit belongs to the primary papers, named here in validation:
- **Sacrifice dilemmas** (Cikara, Farnsworth, Harris & Fiske 2010, *SCAN* 5(4): 404-413; abstract read, the percentages only in Fiske's summary [sa]): 69% found sacrificing one low-status person to save five acceptable, 77% saving five high-status people, 84% sacrificing one disgust-evoking outgroup member to save five pride-evoking ingroupers.
- **Medial prefrontal response** (Harris & Fiske 2006, *Psychol. Sci.* 17(10): 847-853 [abs]): weaker to low-warmth, low-competence targets in a scanner task; mPFC activated for all social groups except the low-low ones; 10 participants viewed 48 photographs, and 12 more viewed objects.
- **Schadenfreude** (Cikara & Fiske 2012, *Soc. Psychol. Personal. Sci.* 3(1): 63-71, author manuscript [read]): aimed at envied (competent, cold) groups, in online ratings and in zygomatic electromyography, with smiles larger to their bad events only for envied targets. Study 1 had 20 university students; the smile-muscle response to negative events was greater for envy targets than for pride, pity and disgust targets (F(1,19) = 5.33, 4.29 with p = .05, and 6.26), and participants did not report overt Schadenfreude. Counterstereotypic information, perspective-taking empathy and outcome dependency reduced scorn or envy in vignette and laboratory studies.

**Empirical standing.** Stereotype ratings by students of societal groups, self-report, twelve samples tabulated across the studies; the relations are among groups' perceived attributes, not among members' ties; the competition law is weak (about -.2 to -.3) and the status law strong (about .6 to .9). A publisher correction to Fiske et al. 2002 is recorded at Crossref (*JPSP* 126(3): 412, March 2024, doi 10.1037/pspa0000163); its content was not read. No replication after 2009 of the numerical laws was read here. Validation found that post-2009 multi-nation SCM data exist: Durante et al. 2013, *Br. J. Soc. Psychol.* 52(4): 726-746, doi:10.1111/bjso.12005, and Durante et al. 2017, *PNAS* 114(4): 669-674, with 49 samples in 38 nations and n = 4,344 [abs]. Their abstracts do not report the status-competence or competition-warmth correlations, so a later re-estimate exists but was not read. Scale: crowd (opinion) for the ratings; interventions are informational manipulations of informed laboratory participants.

**Translation.** Operand: a group g with perceived status s_g and competition c_g and two derived attributes (C_g, W_g). Laws: C_g rises with s_g, W_g falls with c_g, with slopes unknown (only correlations). Emotion is a function of the (C, W) quadrant: envy for high C and low W, scorn or contempt for low C. Envy runs up and scorn runs down.

### 4.4 What decides the sign: a synthesis [derived; no equation behind it]

| Condition | Sign | Evidence | Strength |

## 5. What changes with group size and composition (collected)

- **Lacasa & Luque.** rho_c(N) = 4 mu (N-1) / (eta (1-mu) N (1+F)); the critical density rises from half its large-N value at N = 2 and is flat beyond about ten.
- **Pósfai & D'Souza.** Rank reversal needs a talent gap above delta / (mu (N-1)); the crossover between talent-ruled and experience-ruled hierarchy scales as 1/sqrt(N); open populations (members entering and leaving) create local rank-talent anti-correlation and removal-induced reversals.
- **Chase & Lindquist.** Attribute and loser-effect explanations work in pairs and not in groups of four (same fish dominant 94% of pairs against 75%; prior losers losing 87% of contests against 59%; [sa], Chase, Tovey & Murch 2003 via the 2016 paper); reassembled groups of four re-form the same hierarchy 27.3% of the time (Chase et al. 2002 [read]); linear order by chance collapses from 0.75 at n = 3 to 0.0024 at n = 7; keeping hierarchies linear demands a stricter alpha-to-beta ratio as n grows; they expect larger groups to follow the rules at lower levels.
- **Salganik.** About 700 per world; the authors expect more songs to raise inequality and unpredictability and similar-quality songs to lower inequality while raising unpredictability (untested). Composition: teenagers and young adults, US; the sex mix changed from 36% to 74% women between experiments.
- **Górski.** Triad counts and fitted q vary across school networks of 26 to 534 students; no size trend is reported, but the six smallest networks were the outliers dropped from the regression; composition enters through tie strength (q higher with more weak ties) and mean prosociality.
- **Cuddy.** Unit is the target group; sample sizes 40 to 199; collectivist samples place reference groups differently; the status law holds in every sample (r .63 to .88 by sample, .89 for the EU samples pooled), the competition law is significant in four of seven EU samples and in all three Asian ones.
- **Gould.** Group sizes unknown (n = 5 and n = 17 are the two I can infer); the model as summarised has no explicit size term.

## 7. Datedness and the fluid reading

- **Gould's evidence is older than any of the media it would be applied to.** If my inference about the three datasets holds, the test rests on 1936 to the 1950s, in-person small groups where a deference gesture is costly and reciprocation is visible. Nothing in the abstract tests it on a medium where a gesture costs one tap and need not be returned. I read no online reciprocity rate at source (Kwak et al. 2010 is behind a bot challenge, and in validation the author's old KAIST copy answered 404). The face-to-face anchors are the Newcomb count in 104 (30 to 36 of 68 positive ties reciprocated against a null median near 17; via 104, not re-checked here) and, read in validation, a 2018-19 Madrid first-year class: 56.6% and 58.3% of positive nominations reciprocated in the two waves, against 22.6% and 24.6% after random rewiring (151 students, in-class survey; Escribano et al. 2021) [read]. The two are not directly comparable: one is forced ranks, the other free nominations.
- **The gain is a design setting that changes faster than anyone can re-estimate it.** In the one study that varied it, the mean Gini of the eight influence worlds was about 0.34 with counts in a grid and about 0.50 with counts in a popularity-sorted list, against independent baselines of about 0.26 and 0.20 (my means of the plotted bars [fig]): the ratio of influenced to independent inequality rose from about 1.3 to about 2.5. The two runs were consecutive, with a different sex mix of participants (36% against 74% women), so the layout is not the only difference. A platform can change that setting overnight. The algebra should therefore carry the loop gain as an input tagged with the interface version and date, not as a constant of human nature (D606).
- **Bounds depend on contact density and forgetting.** Van de Rijt's decreasing returns say the loop has a ceiling for a given endowment. Lacasa & Luque's critical density (egalitarian or stratified) and Pósfai & D'Souza's dx = delta / (mu (N-1)) (whether talent or experience orders the group) both depend on contact or win rate and on forgetting, which digital media alter. The 0.294 is a different thing: a universal ceiling, for any continuous unbounded talent distribution, on the probability that removing one member sets off rank reversals. (Before validation this bullet listed the 0.294 among the quantities that depend on contact density and forgetting.)
- **The animal premise is old data, not a moving one.** The hen data behind the two-rule model predate 1982 and the cichlid data were published in 2002-03. The 2026 mole-rat preprint (Haba et al.; machine-vision behaviour in isolation predicting existing dominance within a colony, poorly across colonies) is not peer reviewed and not human. Its authors read the behaviour as expressing a position already held, so it does not test, and does not move, the finding that prior attributes fail to fix rank in groups. (Before validation this bullet said the preprint showed "a premise of Chase & Lindquist moving".)
- **Proposed validity record for a status module.** Carry (i) the form (feedback, brake, threshold; structural), (ii) per fit: era, medium, interface, group-size range, population, (iii) the medium parameters as editable inputs with slack: signal salience (none, grid, sorted list), reciprocation cost, contact density, forgetting rate. The three L2 models differ only in which of these they hold fixed.

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

**Only the existence of a motive reduces to a drive; the hierarchy, its steepness and the sign of its ties do not, on everything read here (Gould's unread text excepted). "Drives as primitives" is the hypothesis under test, and for status it is supported in the weak sense that a motive exists and unsupported as a generator of structure.**

**What reduces to a person-level quantity.**
- **The desire for status** as a motive (Anderson et al. 2015 [abs]; 055): wellbeing and behaviour depend on status accorded, across culture, gender, age and personality. A scalar per person, dynamics unknown.
- **Preference terms in the models.** Gould's person-level terms as summarised (attraction to the most attractive, a symmetry concern, susceptibility to collective attributions) [sa]; Górski's p_ST ("preference for respecting others") and p_SBT, and q; Lacasa & Luque's eta (how decisive a status difference is) and mu (forgetting). These are propensities that can sit on a person, and they are the only drive-like content, but each is fitted or assumed, never measured as a drive.
- **Envy and scorn as affect** (Fiske; 41): a person-level response to a status gap and competition.

**What does not reduce.**
- **The rank order itself** is a fixed point of conferral or contest (a property of the pattern of gestures), and in all three L2 models it arises without any agent wanting status: Bonabeau-type agents only contest with feedback, talent-plus-reinforcement agents likewise, triad dynamics have no agent-level state at all.
- **Its steepness** is set by something that is not a drive in the experiment that tested it: the salience of the social signal (a layout) and, in the models, contact density, forgetting and group size.
- **Group size dissolves individual-level prediction in animals.** What explains a pair (attributes, loser effects: 94%, 87%) does not explain a group of four (75%, 59%) [sa: Chase, Tovey & Murch 2003 via Chase & Lindquist 2016], and reassembled groups of four re-form the same hierarchy only 27.3% of the time [read: Chase et al. 2002]: composition upward is not summation of dyads. The 2026 mole-rat preprint predicts an already-established rank from behaviour, which is a different question (section 3.3); it is also not peer reviewed and not human.
- **The sign of a tie** depends on triadic context and on the status gap together; the fitted model gives it to three propensities and no drive, and the stereotype laws govern perceptions of groups, not ties. Envy points up and scorn points down, so the sign is not a function of rank.
- **Quality is only partly what ranks.** Success follows quality loosely (Salganik) and arbitrary early success persists with decreasing returns (van de Rijt [abs]).

**What would count as a test.** A model in which a status-seeking parameter, measured on a person by an instrument independent of ties, predicts that person's conferral and rank in a group of known size, with the interface and medium held fixed and the parameter moved by an intervention. None exists in what I could read.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**Predicts.** The direction of effects at group and crowd scale; not a ranking, and no individual outcome.

- **Gould 2002.** Claims "unusually accurate" prediction of network structure on three face-to-face datasets (inferred 1936 to 1956) that the wiki says were selected because they fit the model's assumptions [abs, sa]; the fits, effect sizes and any held-out test are unread. Manzo & Baldassarri's abstract says models of this family only partly reproduce three macro-patterns [abs]. Verdict: unscored.
- **Social information.** Salganik: showing counts raised the Gini of success from about 0.26 to 0.29-0.42 and from about 0.20 to 0.45-0.56, and raised unpredictability, in a 2004-05 online crowd of about 700 per world; the best songs rarely did poorly and the worst rarely well, but any other result was possible [read]. Van de Rijt: arbitrary early success helps, with decreasing returns [abs]. These test direction and ordering, not a model; no gain was estimated.
- **Hierarchy formation.** Chase & Lindquist: the two-rule triad model fits one hen dataset (14 groups, collected before 1982) and the authors call the support tentative [read]. Lacasa & Luque and Pósfai & D'Souza give conditions and bounds but no test of their own. The winner-loser family they belong to was tested component by component against hen data and did not agree (Lindquist & Chase 2009 [abs]).
- **Signs.** Górski: the ego-dissonance model reproduces triad over- and under-representation in 36 networks with three fitted parameters per network, in-sample and with strongly correlated parameters [read]. The SCM: status tracks competence, competition tracks lack of warmth, in undergraduate ratings of societal groups [read]; the numerical laws have a publisher correction (content unread); the cross-cultural samples of Cuddy et al. 2009 are the replication, and no post-2009 one was read.

**Out-of-sample evidence.** None for any status-to-hierarchy equation. Gould: no held-out check appears in anything I read. Salganik: the independent condition is a control and the eight worlds are replicates, on one platform and one population; the second experiment changed the layout and the recruitment, so it is a robustness check on direction, not a second population. Chase & Lindquist: the move from pairs to groups of four is a size-generalisation test, and the individual-level explanations fail it [sa for the 2003 figures]. Chase et al. 2002's reassembly is a direct test of the prior-attributes prediction on new data, and it fails (27.3% identical) [read]. Górski: 36 networks, but each fitted in-sample, so the cross-network spread of q shows variation, not validation. The stereotype laws are the one result replicated across populations: the status-competence relation is positive in all twelve tabulated samples (r .63 to .88, all significant; .89 for the EU samples pooled), the competition-warmth relation negative in all twelve and significant in nine (US, Belgium twice, Germany, Portugal, Spain, Hong Kong, Japan, South Korea) [derived from the stars in Cuddy et al. Table 1]; all are student ratings of group stereotypes, not behaviour.

**Controls.** Only distributional properties of crowd outcomes: the layout of the information (Salganik's grid against a sorted list moved the Gini by a large amount) and informational manipulations of scorn and envy (counterstereotypic information, empathy, outcome dependency) in laboratory studies [read, Fiske]. Both work by manipulating the information participants receive, not by an appeal the person can accept or refuse; neither moves a particular person's rank.

**Failures and limits.** The central source is unread. Quality only partly determines success (Salganik). Individual-attribute models fail in groups of four. The status ordering in the sign models is inferred from the signs. The evidence is 1936-2009 except the 2018-19 school data and Górski's own simulations; no result I could read tests a status law on a platform or interface designed after about 2010 (the platforms and dates of van de Rijt's interventions are not in its abstract).
