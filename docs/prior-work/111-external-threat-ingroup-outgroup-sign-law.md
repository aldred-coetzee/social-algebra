## 1. Result in brief

1. **The named seed is not delivered.** Stein 1976 is closed. What is known without reading it: its abstract says the proposition that external conflict raises internal cohesion is "often assumed to be true and is easily illustrated" but that the studies show "a number of intervening variables" and that it "is not uniformly true" [abs]; Münster & Staal quote it as concluding that "under some conditions" external conflict does raise internal cohesion (p. 165) [quoted in M&S]. The list of conditions, the studies counted and any effect sizes are unconfirmed.
2. **The verbal law is really four laws, and they are not the same sign claim.** (L1) Solidarity inside and hostility outside are "correlative" (Sumner 1906) [read]. (L2) Groups in continued struggle claim "total personality involvement", suppress dissent and split when dissent breaks out (Coser 1956, concluding chapter) [read, excerpt]. (L3) A group can use external conflict to buy internal peace: Münster & Staal's commitment result, a theorem about payoffs [read]. (L4) Sherif's functional-relations law: attitudes toward another group follow the functional relation (competitive and frustrating, or shared-goal) between the groups, and in-group solidarity rises with them [read].
3. **The in-group half (solidarity, cooperation and enforcement rise under shared threat) has the broadest support, across eras and media, with small to moderate magnitudes.** Pooled over 16 war-exposure studies, +0.07 to +0.08 SD on a cooperation index and +0.24 to +0.25 SD on prosocial game behaviour toward in-group members (Bauer et al., working paper) [read]; 93.6% and 92.5% in-group friendship choices after the friction stage (Sherif, 1954) [read]; 97% of 179 Libyan battalion members (frontline fighters and logistical supporters together) fused with their own battalion, against 6% to 41% fusion with a nation in peacetime samples (Whitehouse et al. 2014; data 2011) [read]; higher costly punishment and reward in wartime (Gneezy & Fessler 2012; the 2006 war) [read]; Twitter solidarity words peaking the day after the Paris attacks (Garcia & Rimé 2019; data 2015) [read in validation; was fetch-extract].
4. **The out-group-negative half is the thin one, and the best-read sources say it is separable.** Brewer reports a correlation "essentially .00" between in-group regard and social distance toward out-groups across 30 East African groups [quoted in Brewer]; war exposure raised prosocial behaviour toward in-group partners by +0.24 to +0.25 SD and toward out-group partners by +0.04 SD, not significant, with "no indication of negative effects towards the out-group" (Bauer et al.) [read]; in the ethnocentrism simulation the out-group strategy is non-cooperation, not harm (Hammond & Axelrod) [read]. Where the two do couple, the coupling is conditional on a perceived source or obstacle: Black South Africans' identification correlated with hostility to Afrikaners but not to English-speaking whites or to whites in general (Duckitt & Mphuthing 1998) [quoted in Brewer].
5. **A one-line law "threat → + inside, − across" is therefore not supported as stated; a two-term conditional law is.** In-group + follows from shared exposure and interdependence; out-group − follows from an attributed source or a competitive functional relation (Sherif Hypothesis 1, Stage 2), pre-existing contempt (Brewer) or a leader's sanction (Sherif's raids) [all read]. Section 8 states the candidate forms with their validity domains.
6. **Conditions that null or flip the in-group half are in the same sources.** Defeat produced temporary internal friction in both Sherif groups (two Rattlers wrote letters asking to go home; the Eagle leader who walked away from the rope lost his rank) [read]. Tyerman & Spencer found no rise, and a fall, in in-group solidarity among well-acquainted scouts [abs]. In Tajikistan, where the war was neighbour against neighbour, exposure lowered trust, most where opposing groups were residentially intermixed (Cassar, Grosjean & Whitt, via Bauer et al.) [read, as reported]. Across 90 pre-industrial societies internal and external conflict had a moderate **positive** relationship, the opposite sign to the simple law (Ross 1985) [abs].
7. **Formal versions exist and none generates a tie sign.** Münster & Staal: in a symmetric contest between identical individuals, equilibrium conflict is internal or external but not both, and groups that can commit to external effort first choose enough of it to leave no internal conflict (a theorem; no data) [read]. Hammond & Axelrod: in-group favouritism (cooperate with own colour, defect on others) reaches 76% of agents on a 50 × 50 lattice with no reciprocity and no threat term [read]. Axelrod & Bennett: pairwise propensities (signs) plus sizes determine bloc structure; balance against threat is the special case "all propensities equal and negative", which predicts size-balanced blocs; the 1936 coding got 15 of 17 nations right in the Second World War [read]. Each takes the signs, or the payoff structure that makes them, as input.
8. **Leaders gate out-group aggression.** In Sherif's camp no raid happened without the leader's approval, and raids suggested far exceeded raids carried out [read]; in the lab, a first-mover (sequential decisions) raised aggressors' success from 20% to 35% and did nothing for defenders (De Dreu et al.) [read]. A leader-approval gate is not in v0.1.
9. **The common-enemy move is stated and not tested in anything I could read.** Sherif says a common enemy unifies the former rivals and "implies widening the area of conflict" and that his 1949 study used it "because of expediency" [read]; the only datum in this file is that Libyan battalion members were 96% fused with *other* battalions while only 1% were fused with non-fighting supporters of the same cause [read]. Brewer argues the opposite for anticipated positive interdependence with an out-group, and that common threat plus prior contempt yields scapegoating [read, assertion]. *Added in validation:* tests of shared threat on cross-group attitudes do exist and were not read: Feshbach & Singer (1957) and Burnstein & McRae (1962) (titles and Crossref metadata only, section 15), so "not tested" means "not tested in what this file read".
10. **Where there is an intervention, it is a manipulation.** Sherif's staff designed the frustrations so the boys could not trace them to the staff, inflated scores, and let the Eagles win to keep them from leaving camp (Perry, secondary); the 1953 attempt failed because the staff became the common target (Sherif's own text says the friction "was attributed to the camp administration"; Perry adds detail) [read]. Diversionary use of force is a leader's choice (Münster & Staal). The rally effect depends on how elites and the press frame a crisis (Baker & Oneal) [abs]. Nothing here tests whether the effect survives being known.
11. **Size and composition.** The formal results carry explicit size terms (section 6); the only human size contrasts are across studies, not within one. Sherif: 11 per group, strangers, matched, homogeneous. De Dreu: 3 against 3. Whitehouse: battalions built of micro-groups of 3 to 5. Animals: larger marmoset groups showed a bigger post-conflict affiliation rise (Radford et al.). Composition effects that are stated: cross-cutting memberships weaken the coupling (Coser, Brewer); the in-group boundary was set by shared risk, not by shared cause (Whitehouse).
12. **Era and medium.** Every human result I read directly on ties or cooperation is face to face, by in-person survey, or in an anonymous laboratory (De Dreu et al., run under 2014 and 2015 ethics files), from 1954 to about 2015 (the war-exposure studies give collection years only as years since the war); the later results read are Schraff's March 2020 Dutch online panel (trust in parliament, not ties) and the two online studies below (words and shares, not ties). *Corrected in validation:* this item first said "face to face or in-person survey, 1954 to roughly 2012", which left out Schraff and De Dreu. The online evidence, read at source in validation: in-group solidarity words after an attack (Twitter 2015, out-group not measured) and out-group words multiplying shares by about 1.67 per word (Twitter and Facebook, "the years leading up to the 2020 election" in the authors' words; the first draft's "2016-2020" is not in the main text). Nothing I read tests the sign law at crowd scale online; two crowd-scale online leads exist and were not read (Phan & Airoldi 2015 on ties after a hurricane; Magdy et al. 2016 on attitudes to Muslims after the Paris attacks; section 13).

## 2. The claim in its original words

### 2.1 Sumner (1906), sections 13 to 15 [read]

- **Citation.** Sumner, W. G. (1906). *Folkways: A Study of the Sociological Importance of Usages, Manners, Customs, Mores, and Morals*. Boston: Ginn. Sections 13 to 18. Project Gutenberg #24253.
- **Their vocabulary.** "We-group" or "in-group" against "others-groups" or "out-groups"; "ethnocentrism" ("the technical name for this view of things in which one's own group is the center of everything").
- **The law.** "The relation of comradeship and peace in the we-group and that of hostility and war towards others-groups are correlative to each other." The explanation is functional: "The exigencies of war with outsiders are what make peace inside, lest internal discord should weaken the we-group for war." These exigencies also "make government and law in the in-group" (section 14).
- **A dose-response clause.** "The closer the neighbors, and the stronger they are, the intenser is the warfare, and then the intenser is the internal organization and discipline of each." That is the only quantitative-looking statement: intensity of external hostility rises with proximity and strength of the other group, and internal organisation rises with it. No measurement, sample or equation accompanies it.
- **Everything moves together.** "Loyalty to the group, sacrifice for it, hatred and contempt for outsiders, brotherhood within, warlikeness without, all grow together, common products of the same situation." The "situation" is, in Sumner's terms, the struggle for existence between groups (section 13: "The size of the groups is determined by the conditions of the struggle for existence"; "conflict over scarce resources" is my gloss, not his phrase).
- **Evidence base.** An ethnographic compendium assembled by a sociologist and economist in 1906: names of peoples for themselves ("men", "real people"), epithets for others, war and plunder as virtue (sections 16 to 18). Era of the data: the 19th-century ethnographic record. Medium: text. No sample, no counts.
- **Reading by Brewer.** Brewer (1999) takes Sumner's sentence as the origin of the assumption of *negative reciprocity* between in-group and out-group attitudes, and notes that Sumner, like Allport, defined ethnocentrism through positive sentiments toward the in-group but held that these were "directly correlated" with hostility to outsiders (p. 430) [read].
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: persons in a we-group; the other group as a unit. Operators: none stated; the relation is a symmetric covariation of two group-level intensities (internal solidarity and organisation; external hostility), with a proximity and strength modifier. In algebra terms it is a constraint between two group quantities, not an update law.

### 2.2 Coser (1956), concluding chapter, pp. 151-157 [read, excerpt only]

I read only the concluding chapter. It does not state the bare proposition "external conflict raises cohesion". It states three things about external conflict, in the course of a summary of internal conflict:

- **Intolerance.** "Groups which are engaged in continued struggle tend to lay claim on the total personality involvement of their members", so such groups "are unlikely to tolerate more than limited departures from the group unity"; "in such groups there is a tendency to suppress conflict; where it occurs, it leads the group to break up through splits or through forced withdrawal of dissenters." (Pages around 153.) This is law L2: external struggle raises the cost of internal negative ties and makes the failure mode a split, not a milder disagreement.
- **Coalition and boundary.** "Conflicts with some produce associations or coalitions with others", and "once groups and associations have been formed through conflict with other groups, such conflict may further serve to maintain boundary lines between them and the surrounding social environment." The first is the common-enemy move (section 8, S-C); the second says conflict *defines* the boundary, so the boundary is an output, not a given.
- **Structure moderates.** "The closer the group, the more intense the conflict", and "multiplicity of conflicts stands in inverse relation to their intensity": groups whose members take part only segmentally and in many cross-cutting conflicts avoid a single cleavage. Brewer quotes the same cross-cutting passage (pp. 153-154) as the structural precedent for her argument [quoted in Brewer, confirmed in the excerpt].
- **Realistic against non-realistic conflict.** Realistic conflict is "means toward specific results" with functional alternatives; non-realistic conflict is tension release in which only the target is substitutable ("the chosen antagonist can be substituted for by any other 'suitable' target"). This is the nearest thing in the excerpt to a rule for *who* the out-group is: for non-realistic conflict, any suitable target.
- **Not in the excerpt.** The book's own statement of the external-conflict-raises-cohesion proposition and its conditions; I saw its gist only in search-engine snippets (a Conflict Research Consortium summary: intense conflict and group loyalty are "two aspects of the same relation") [sa], not used.
- **Evidence base.** No data and no equation in the chapter read. That Coser worked from Simmel (1908) and from illustrative cases, and that the cases run from the early 20th century to the 1950s, is the standard account [sa]: the concluding chapter never names Simmel (checked in validation), so neither statement comes from the text read. Medium: text.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: group, member, dissenter. Operators: suppress, split, withdraw (forced or voluntary), form coalition. The splitting and forced-withdrawal operators are the ones the Stein abstract and the defeat observations in Sherif both point to, and v0.1's partition Π_t and leaver set L_t can carry them.

### 2.3 Simmel, Dahrendorf and the diversionary tradition [quoted in open sources]

- Simmel ([1908] 1955), as summarised in Benard's abstract [abs]: "groups in conflict quell dissent by sanctioning group members and supporting centralized leadership systems"; Benard adds that this claim has "important implications, but little direct support" and tests it in two small-group experiments (section 7.7).
- Dahrendorf (1964, p. 58), quoted in Münster & Staal citing Levy (1989): "It appears to be a general law that human groups react to external pressure by increased internal coherence." [quoted in M&S]
- Münster & Staal trace the "diversionary" idea to Bodin, Swift and Shakespeare and say, of the quantitative literature on diversionary war, that "findings are mixed and no consensus has emerged" (their footnote 9, citing Levy 1989, 1998) [quoted in M&S].

## 3. Stein (1976): what could and could not be read

- **Citation.** Stein, A. A. (1976). Conflict and cohesion. *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 20(1): 143-172 (March 1976). doi:10.1177/002200277602000106, Crossref-confirmed, author Arthur A. Stein; the running head and citations give the subtitle "A review of the literature"; Crossref lists 141 references. Closed access.
- **[abs] What the abstract commits to.** It "reviews the theoretical formulations and the empirical tests of the proposition that external conflict increases internal cohesion", across sociology, anthropology, psychology and political science. "Though it is often assumed to be true and is easily illustrated, the empirical studies suggest that there are a number of intervening variables and that the hypothesis is not uniformly true. While hardly adequate, these empirical studies provide a subtler specification of the hypothesis."
- **[quoted in M&S] One sentence.** Münster & Staal (2005, footnote 5) write that Stein "concludes in his excellent survey of the literature up to the mid 1970s that, under some conditions, 'external conflict does increase internal cohesion' (1976, p. 165)", and that Dion (1979) and Fisher (1990) reach similar conclusions.

## 4. Sherif et al. (1954/1961) read for this question

Design, sample and the superordinate-goal stage are in 107 section 5; this section adds what bears on the threat sign law.

- **Design.** Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma, June to July 1954. 22 boys (11 per group), fifth graders, median age 11 years 1 month, strangers to one another, matched, homogeneous [read, chapter 3]. Planned stages: formation of two in-groups (5-6 days), friction through a tournament with a prize and engineered frustrations (4-6 days), integration through superordinate goals (6-7 days) [read, chapter 2]. The boys were not told it was a study of group relations.
- **The authors' general hypothesis (the sign law in Sherif's form).** "Intergroup attitudes and behavior are determined primarily by the nature of functional relations between groups in question", and *not* "primarily by the pattern of relations and attitudes prevailing within groups themselves" or by individual frustration (chapter 2) [read]. Negative attitudes toward an out-group "develop whenever any out-group is perceived as frustrating or as an obstacle". The *source of the sign is a relation between two groups and a goal*, not a drive of any member.
- **The relevant hypotheses [read, chapter 2, pp. 46-49].** Stage 2 Hypothesis 1: unfavourable stereotypes will arise and standardise, with social distance "proportional to the degree of negative relations". Hypothesis 2: "The course of relations between two groups which are in a state of competition and frustration will tend to produce an increase in in-group solidarity." Hypothesis 3: consequential intergroup relations change the in-group's own structure, for winners and losers ("disorganization" is allowed for the loser). Hypothesis 4: low-status members will show the most overt aggression toward the out-group to raise their standing.

### 4.1 What the boys did on first learning of the other group (before any contest) [read, chapter 4, pp. 94-95]

When the Rattlers learned another group existed they said "They better not be in our swimming hole"; when the presence of the other group was announced they "immediately wanted to challenge them, and to be the first to challenge", and performance in all activities that might become competitive "was entered into with more zest and also with more efficiency." Discovery of another group "brought heightened awareness of 'us' and 'ours' as contrasted with 'outsiders' and 'intruders', an intense desire to compete." The Eagles' first remark about the out-group used a racial epithet imported from outside the camp (not reproduced here). So the boundary and the appetite to compete appeared on **awareness alone**, before any frustration; the negative *attitudes* (stereotypes) came only after the tournament. That separates three things the single phrase "threat" bundles: boundary salience (on discovery), competitive motivation (on discovery) and out-group derogation (after friction).

### 4.2 End of the friction stage [read, chapter 6]

- **Sociometric.** Friendship choices from the whole camp: in-group 73 of 78 (93.6%) for the Rattlers and 62 of 67 (92.5%) for the Eagles; the questionnaire forced some out-group choices; "differences ... too large to be attributed to chance". There was **no** sociometric baseline before the contests in the 1954 design (the friendship-choice stage of 1949 and 1953 was dropped, chapter 2; "sociometric choices were obtained only in Stages 2 and 3", chapter 4, p. 87), so the 93% cannot be read as an increase. The 1949 and 1953 runs did have a pre-group friendship stage, and chapter 2 says choices then reversed "in the direction of the experimentally produced in-groups"; the figures are in the earlier reports, not in this text [read in validation].
- **Stereotype ratings of the out-group, six characteristics combined.** Rattlers' ratings of Eagles (66 ratings): 21.2% and 31.8% in the two most unfavourable categories, 53.0% unfavourable; Eagles' ratings of Rattlers (52 ratings): 36.5% and 40.4%, 76.9% unfavourable, 15.4% favourable. **Internal inconsistency in the text:** for the Rattlers the text says 24.9% favourable, but the table's two favourable categories (19.9% and 15.0%) sum to 34.9% (counts 13 and 10 of 66).
- **Solidarity, observational (Hypothesis 2).** "Supports this hypothesis with the qualification that in several instances defeat in a contest with the rival group brought temporarily increased internal friction in its wake." After the Rattlers lost the second baseball game, Brown and Allen wrote letters wanting to go home; the leader's joking made them tear the letters up. After the Eagles' first tug-of-war loss the group was "downhearted" until it burned the Rattler flag together. Solidarity recovered through "united cooperative action by the in-group against the out-group". Not every defeat did this; some were taken calmly because the group had not expected to win that event.
- **Hierarchy changes (Hypothesis 3).** The Eagle leader Craig walked away from the rope in the first tug-of-war, claimed the tournament was lost, "pretended to be asleep" during the first Rattler raid and fell from first to fifth in rank; Mason, the captain of baseball only, took over all group activities. Every Eagle then agreed in the group with Mason's estimate of 45 minutes for a tie, although only one other boy had given an estimate that high individually.
- **Leaders gate aggression (Hypothesis 4, "inconclusive").** "In no instance did the in-groups engage groups in aggressive action toward the out-group if this had not been approved by the leaders." "The number of raids suggested by various group members far exceeded the number actually carried out." The authors reformulate Hypothesis 4: aggression "in line with the trend of intergroup conflict sanctioned by high status members" is how low-status members raise their standing. The comparison test (low-status behaviour with and without high-status members present) was **not run**.
- **Time course and an outside norm [read, chapter 5, pp. 100-101].** The invectives began with the first contest, but "the norms internalized from the larger social setting concerning 'good sportsmanship' were clearly evident for the first two days", shown in the custom of three cheers for the losers; after the second day of the tournament sportsmanship "gave away, as event followed event, to increased name-calling, hurling invectives, and derogation of the out-group" until the groups were reluctant to have anything to do with each other. The build-up was "cumulative with rapid spurts", and the most effective encounters for these 11-year-olds were short ones with direct physical contact, the tug-of-war first. So the out-group sign was not set by the first threat but grew over days, against a norm imported from the wider society that initially held it near neutral.
- **Solidarity does not transfer.** At the end of Stage 2 the groups, taken to a crowded public beach, "stuck almost exclusively to activities within their respective in-groups. Psychologically, other people did not count" (chapter 8, p. 207). And: "In-group solidarity, in-group cooperativeness and democratic procedures need not necessarily be transferred to the out-group and its members." The *neutral* outsiders at the beach drew indifference, not hostility.

### 4.3 End of the integration stage, in-group side [read, chapter 7, pp. 187-192]

After the superordinate-goal stage, 36 of 99 Rattler friendship choices (36.4%) and 15 Eagle choices (23.2%) went to the out-group, up from 6.4% and 7.5%; the share of rejections aimed at out-group members fell from 75% to 15% (Rattlers) and from 95% to 47.1% (Eagles); unfavourable out-group ratings fell from 53% to 4.5% and from 76.9% to 22.6%. **In-group ratings fell too**: "The ratings of in-group members after cooperation with the out-group were not as favorable as the highly positive ratings of the in-group made after the intense intergroup rivalry of Stage 2 although the trend is not statistically significant"; the most-favourable category fell 9 points (Rattlers) and 25.9 points (Eagles). So within this one run in-group positivity and out-group negativity rose and fell together, the Sumner pattern, at group scale. **Table inconsistency:** the Eagle in-group count printed as 41 does not fit 76.8% when 15 out-group choices make 23.2% (that implies about 50 in-group choices); the percentages are used. *Added in validation:* the reverse reading is as likely: the printed counts 41 and 15 give 73.2% and 26.8%, so the Eagles' out-group share at the end of Stage 3 is 23.2% or 26.8% depending on which half of the table is misprinted.

- "The alternative of appeal to a common enemy, which was effectively used in our 1949 study because of expediency at the time, was not used. The unification of groups against a 'common enemy' necessarily implies widening the area of conflict." (chapter 7, p. 152)

### 4.5 Manipulation, the 1953 failure and consent

- Sherif's own text on 1953 (chapter 5, p. 96) [read]: "the source of friction was attributed to the camp administration. The 1953 study had to be terminated at this point for this reason." In 1954 the staff planned around that.
- The staff arranged conditions "in such a way that it is not possible for group members to ascertain by checking verbally with the members of the other group that someone (the staff) has been manipulating conditions" (chapter 2) [read]. Principals, teachers and parents were told it was a University of Oklahoma study of interaction within and between teams, including competition, winning and losing, and frustration (chapter 3); the boys were not told it was a study (chapter 8: they "were not aware that each step was especially designed" for it), and the engineering of the frustrations and outcomes was not disclosed to anyone acted on.
- Perry (2014) [read, secondary; draws on Sherif's archive and interviews with former campers]: in the 1953 attempt "Resentment towards camp staff intensified during the competition phase as both teams accused staff of bias and favouritism"; boys separated from friends they made in a joint first phase were aggrieved; a boy found a staff notebook and confirmed a rumour that the camp was an experiment; Sherif wrote that study off. For 1954 the organisers chose athletic boys, expecting them to be "more likely to identify with their team and derogate their opponents", inflated one team's scores to raise rivalry in both 1953 and 1954, and, in the words attributed to O. J. Harvey, "we deliberately let them win the competition" for fear the Eagles "would fall apart" and go home. Perry cites Billig (1976): the experiments had **three** groups, the two sets of boys and the staff.
- **Reading for the sign law.** In 1953 the attributed source of the frustration was the staff, and the boys' solidarity turned on them. The sign attaches to whoever is perceived to cause the frustration, whatever the designers intended; the designers' later success depended on hiding the manipulation, on selecting competitive boys and on controlling who won.

### 4.6 Replication and standing

Tyerman & Spencer (1983) [abs] tested the Sherifs' hypotheses in a natural setting, scout troops in a camping situation, with well-acquainted boys instead of strangers: "In-group solidarity showed no increase during camps, as would have been predicted by the Sherifs; indeed, a decrease was found." Their ego-involvement and responsiveness-to-appeals hypotheses were "substantially supported". Not read; I found no other replication in the searches I ran. *Added in validation (no new searches were possible, the web-search budget being spent; these are known works checked at Crossref):* Blake & Mouton (1961, *Management Science* 7(4): 420-435, doi:10.1287/mnsc.7.4.420) [abs] summarise investigations of intergroup competition under win-lose conditions and apply them to a management-union conflict, an industrial setting rather than a boys' camp; a 1963 Beirut replication with unacquainted boys is commonly reported (Diab 1970, *Genetic Psychology Monographs*) [sa, the validator's background knowledge], but it is not in Crossref and was not confirmed here (section 15). Standing: one camp, two groups, covert manipulation, a failed predecessor, descriptive data without significance tests for most contrasts, no pre-contest baseline, and one partial failure to replicate with acquainted boys.

- **Era / medium / scale.** 1954; residential camp, face to face; group (and membership for the ratings); manipulation, covert (parents told of a study of team competition and frustration; boys not told).
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: boy, group, leader, the prize or goal, the other group. Operators: `compete`, `frustrate` (by the other group, as perceived), `derogate`, `raid` (gated by the leader's approval), `sanction` deviants, `burn-flag`-type symbolic acts, and in Stage 3 `insert-superordinate-goal` (107). The "threat" is never a state of the group: it is a pattern of events (defeat, intrusion, frustration) attributed to the other group.
- **Size and composition.** Groups of 11; strangers; matched on skills; from established Protestant, on the whole middle-class families, chosen to exclude extreme or prolonged frustration in the boys' backgrounds. The authors expect the effect to depend on functional relations, not composition, but composition was held fixed, so the experiment cannot say.

## 5. Brewer (1999) and the decoupling literature [read in full]

- **Thesis.** "Ingroup identification is independent of negative attitudes toward outgroups and ... much ingroup bias and intergroup discrimination is motivated by preferential treatment of ingroup members rather than direct hostility toward outgroup members" (abstract). Allport (1954) is the precursor; Sumner (1906) and Sherif's functional theory are the sources of the assumption of "negative reciprocity" (pp. 430-431). Her conclusion: "Ingroup love is not a necessary precursor of outgroup hate", but the same factors that make in-group attachment matter "provide a fertile ground for antagonism and distrust" toward outsiders, most strongly in "highly segmented, hierarchically organized societies" (pp. 441-442).
- **Evidence she cites for decoupling [quoted in Brewer; the underlying papers not read].** Brewer & Campbell (1976), 30 ethnic groups in East Africa: almost all rated their own group above every out-group on trustworthiness, obedience, friendliness and honesty, yet "the correlation between degree of positive ingroup regard and social distance toward outgroups was essentially .00 across the 30 groups" (their p. 85; Brewer p. 432). In minimal-group allocation, the ingroup bias seen for benefits is "essentially eliminated" when the thing allocated is a cost (Mummendey et al. 1992, via Brewer). Patriotism or in-group pride is "conceptually and empirically distinct" from aggression against out-groups (Feshbach 1994; Struch & Schwartz 1989, via Brewer).
- **Her functional account.** Humans rely on "obligatory interdependence"; in-groups are "bounded communities of mutual trust and obligation"; indiscriminate altruism is not an effective strategy, so aid is limited to acknowledged members (p. 433). Optimal distinctiveness (Brewer 1991): two opposed needs, for inclusion (assimilation) and for differentiation, held in check by identifying with distinctive groups; a person who feels isolated has the inclusion drive aroused, a person immersed in a very large or undefined collective has the differentiation drive aroused (p. 434). "Ingroup loyalty ... is most effectively engaged by relatively small, distinctive groups or social categories" (p. 434), which limits how far a superordinate identity can carry.
- **How coupling arises, her list (pp. 435-438).** (a) *Moral superiority*, as institutions become depersonalised and absolute; contempt and avoidance, not necessarily attack. (b) *Perceived threat*: "the reciprocal relationship between ingroup cohesion and outgroup hostility may be limited to conditions in which groups are in competition over physical resources or political power", whether actual or imagined (pp. 435-436). (c) *Common goals*: she accepts that "loosely knit ingroups become more cohesive and less subject to internal factioning when they can be rallied to the demands of achieving a common goal", but argues that for *two differentiated groups* anticipated positive interdependence makes the absence of trust salient and "actually promotes intergroup conflict", and that "when negative evaluations of the outgroup such as contempt or fear are also already present, common threat in particular may promote scapegoating and blame rather than mutual cooperation" (p. 436, citing her own in-press argument; no data in this article). (d) *Common values and social comparison*, which turn shared goals into competition for positive distinctiveness. (e) *Power politics*: "perceived common threat from outgroups increases ingroup cohesion and loyalty", so leaders can exploit it (pp. 437-438).
- **The conditional coupling, her one concrete case [quoted in Brewer].** Duckitt & Mphuthing (1998, a longitudinal analysis in South Africa): among Black Africans there was "a substantial interrelationship between ingroup identification and negative attitudes toward Afrikaners", but no correlation between identification and attitudes toward English whites or whites in general. Their reading: two kinds of prejudice, one rooted in perceived conflict and reciprocal with in-group identification, the other independent of it. *Added in validation from Duckitt & Mphuthing's own abstract [abs]:* the sample was Black African students measured before and after the April 1994 election, and the longitudinal analyses "suggested causal impacts from attitudes to identification and not the reverse", so in this one panel the out-group attitude drove identification, the opposite direction to "identification produces hostility".
- **Composition and structure.** Dichotomous, single-axis categorisations (ethnicity or religion splitting a society in two) raise the coupling; cross-cutting memberships lower it. Silver & Brewer (1997, more than 200 US students): memberships in religious, ethnic, gender and political groups were "essentially uncorrelated", and strengths of identification were positively correlated, not competing (pp. 439-441). Collectivist societies show sharper in-group/out-group distinction (Triandis 1995, via Brewer).
- **Standing.** A conceptual review from a 1997 symposium address, not a meta-analysis; the quantitative core is one cross-group correlation (N = 30 groups, collection years not given in the article), one South African panel, and laboratory minimal-group work. Much of the argument is hers (common goals, moral superiority). It is evidence that the *two halves can come apart*, not that they usually do.
- **Closed-access quantitative companions [abs].** Balliet, Wu & De Dreu (2014, *Psych. Bulletin* 140(6): 1556-1581, doi:10.1037/a0037737): in-group favouritism in cooperation, d = 0.32 overall; d = 0.19 where categorisation carries no interdependence (dictator-type games) and d = 0.42 where there is interdependence (social dilemmas); stronger under common knowledge of membership and simultaneous exchange; "intergroup discrimination in cooperation is the result of ingroup favoritism rather than outgroup derogation." Halevy, Bornstein & Sagiv (2008, *Psych. Science* 19(4): 405-411, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02100.x): in a game that lets members give to a pool that helps the in-group or to a pool that helps it *and* harms the out-group, contributions "are made almost exclusively to the cooperative, within-group pool." Riek, Mania & Gaertner (2006, *Pers. Soc. Psych. Review* 10(4): 336-353, doi:10.1207/s15327957pspr1004_4): 95 samples; "5 different threat types had a positive relationship with negative outgroup attitudes", out-group status moderating some of them (six types are discussed: realistic, symbolic, intergroup anxiety, negative stereotypes, group-esteem and distinctiveness threat; which five is not stated in the abstract, and the effect sizes were not available to me). **Caution [derived]:** that is a correlation between *perceived* threat and attitude in the same respondents, not an effect of a threat manipulation on ties. Deschamps & Brown (1983, *Brit. J. Social Psychology* 22(3): 189-195, doi:10.1111/j.2044-8309.1983.tb00583.x) [abs], one of the studies behind Brewer's common-goals argument: 55 students did a cooperative task with a financial prize and a superordinate goal; when the two groups' roles were comparable, differentiation rose (explicit faculty categories) or liking for the out-group fell (ad hoc groups), so superordinate goals "may not reduce intergroup conflict" and distinct roles are the condition that does.
- **Era / medium / scale.** Cited work 1960s to 1990s; field survey, laboratory and panel; the 30-group correlation is at group scale, the rest at membership scale; observational or laboratory.
- **Size / composition.** In-group loyalty engaged by small distinctive groups; coupling higher for single-axis dichotomous splits and collectivist cultures, lower with cross-cutting identities.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Two separate quantities per group: in-group regard (loyalty, trust, preferential treatment) and out-group distance or hostility, with a **coupling coefficient that depends on perceived threat and on structure**. In v0.1 terms: one signed tie per pair cannot carry this; a pair needs an affect component, an out-group-regard component and a perceived-source flag.

## 6. Formal and quantitative versions

### 6.1 Münster & Staal (2005): "War with outsiders makes peace inside" [read in part]

- **Setting.** n identical individuals in G groups of equal size m = n/G. Each gives a unit of time to production e, intra-group rent-seeking x and inter-group rent-seeking y, e + x + y = 1. Output q = f(Σ e) with f(0) = 0, f' > 0, f log-concave. Shares are logistic ("difference form"): member i of group g takes r_ig = exp(a·x_ig) / Σ_j exp(a·x_jg) of the group's share, and group g takes p_g = exp(b·Σ_j y_jg) / Σ_k exp(b·Σ_j y_jk) of output; payoff u_ig = r_ig · p_g · q. a and b are the decisiveness of the internal and external contest. Write φ(z) = f'(z)/f(z), decreasing (the symbol is lost in the PDF text layer; the definition is theirs).
- **Cases (their Definition 1).** NO: φ(n) ≥ max{b(G−1)/G, a(m−1)/m}. INTRA: a(m−1)/m > max{b(G−1)/G, φ(n)}. INTER: b(G−1)/G > max{a(m−1)/m, φ(n)}. SPECIAL: b(G−1)/G = a(m−1)/m > φ(n).
- **Proposition 1 (simultaneous choices).** Conflict both within and between groups only in the non-generic SPECIAL case; "typically, if there is conflict at all, it takes place either within groups, or between groups, but not both." Internal conflict is more likely when a is large and G small (n fixed), and its intensity rises in a and falls in G; external conflict is more likely when b is large and G large, and rises in b and G.
- **Proposition 2 (diversionary use of force).** If each group first commits an external effort y per member and individuals then choose x and e, the symmetric subgame-perfect equilibrium has y = 0 in case NO, y solving a(m−1)/m = φ(n(1−y)) in case INTRA, and y from b(G−1)/G = φ(n(1−y)) in INTER and SPECIAL; "in any case, there is no internal fighting on the equilibrium path." Corollary 1: in INTRA more internal insecurity (larger a) means more external fighting, while b does not matter; "even if b = 0 there is a subgame perfect equilibrium where groups fight enough to prevent internal conflict." Their gloss: "Quite literally, war with outsiders makes peace inside!" *Added in validation:* at b = 0 the authors also note "additional equilibria with internal fighting", which "disappear as soon as b is strictly positive, however small"; so the b = 0 case is not unique.
- **Derived consequences, my working from the printed conditions [derived].** (i) With a = b and outside case NO (the larger of a(m−1)/m and a(G−1)/G exceeds φ(n); "both exceeding φ(n)" in the first draft was looser than the condition), INTRA holds iff a(1 − G/n) > a(1 − 1/G), i.e. G² < n: internal conflict wins when there are fewer than √n groups (m > G), external when there are more. *Confirmed in validation against section 5:* their Proposition 3 puts the optimal number of groups on exactly this boundary (equation 20, a(n − G)/n = b(G − 1)/G), and they state "if a = b, then G = √n ... there should be as many groups as there are individuals in any group" [read]. (ii) In INTRA, for fixed n and a, the external effort needed for internal peace rises with m, because a(m−1)/m rises with m and φ is decreasing: larger groups must buy peace with more outside fighting.
- **What it is and is not.** A theorem about effort allocation between identical payoff-maximisers; the "threat" is not exogenous but **chosen by the group** (it works even at b = 0), so it is a theory of *manufactured* threat. There are no ties, no signs and no drives; the out-group enters only as a contest share. The authors read post-Cold-War civil wars as a fall in b, as interpretation, not as a test. Footnote 9 says the quantitative diversionary-war literature is mixed (Levy) [quoted in M&S].
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: member with an effort allocation (e, x, y); group as a symmetric set of such members. Operators: the contest share functions r and p, and `commit(g, y)`. A group's state is its allocation, not a drive level; the "external threat" is an operator the group applies to itself.
- **Era / medium / scale.** Theory, 2005; model; group (organisation). The 2011 published version was not read. Their conclusion (read in validation) says the model "is static and thus one cannot study dynamics or repeated interaction", so it supplies no update law.

### 6.2 Hammond & Axelrod (2006): "The evolution of ethnocentrism" [read in full]

- **Model (restated).** A 50 × 50 torus (four neighbours each). An agent has a tag (one of four colours) and two actions: what it does to a neighbour of its own colour and to a neighbour of another colour, each cooperate or defect. In each period one immigrant with random traits arrives; each neighbouring pair plays a one-move prisoner's dilemma; an agent starts with potential-to-reproduce 0.12, pays 0.01 for each help given and gains 0.03 for each help received; it clones into an adjacent empty site with probability equal to that potential, with a per-trait mutation rate (0.5% in the text; the appendix lists 0.05, a printed inconsistency); each agent dies with probability 0.10 per period; runs are 2,000 periods. "Ethnocentric" = cooperate with the own colour, defect on others.
- **Results.** In the last 100 periods of ten runs, 76.3% ± 0.9 of agents are ethnocentric (25% under neutral selection) and 74.2% ± 0.5 of interactions are cooperative. The text says halving or doubling any parameter keeps at least two-thirds ethnocentric, but the table's doubled-cost row is 61.8% (a printed inconsistency); the other rows are 2 colours 69.4%, 8 colours 79.1%, mutation 0.25% 82.8% and 1% 67.1%, lattice 25 × 25 70.5% and 100 × 100 78.2%. A 10% chance of misperceiving colour still leaves more than two-thirds ethnocentric. At doubled cost, cooperation is 56% when agents can tell own colour from others and 14% when they cannot.
- **Reading for the sign law.** The pattern *positive inside, nothing outside* is an evolutionarily favoured outcome of local competition for space, with no reciprocity, reputation, or threat parameter; ethnocentric regions expand at the expense of adjacent regions of other strategies (their Figure 2). The out-group action is non-cooperation, not harm; the authors say the research they cite "differentiates, both empirically and causally, between in-group favoritism ('ethnocentrism') and out-group hostility ('xenophobia')" (citing, among others, Ray & Lovejoy 1986, Struch & Schwartz 1989, Cashdan 2001, Hewstone et al. 2002 and Brown 2004). They stress the boundary-defining trait is "continually shaped by culture" and not modelled.
- **Size.** Population size (lattice 625, 2,500 and 10,000 sites) raises ethnocentrism slightly (70.5, 76.3, 78.2%); number of colours (2, 4, 8) raises it (69.4, 76.3, 79.1%) because tags become more accurate indicators of relatedness.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: agent with a tag and two action bits; the group is an emergent region of one colour. Operators: `donate`, `clone`, `mutate`. No drive state; the in-group/out-group distinction is a function of tags.
- **Era / medium / scale.** Theory, 2006; simulation; population of agents; no data. The sign it supports (+ in, 0 out) matches Brewer and Bauer, not the strong Sumner law.

### 6.3 Axelrod & Bennett (1993): landscape theory [read in part]

- **Definitions (restated).** Actors i = 1..n with sizes s_i > 0; a symmetric pairwise **propensity** p_ij to be in the same coalition (positive if they get along, negative if they have many sources of conflict); a configuration X partitions the actors; the distance d_ij(X) is 0 within a bloc and 1 across (two-bloc case). Frustration F_i(X) = Σ_{j≠i} s_j p_ij d_ij(X): propensities are weighted by the size of the other actor. Energy E(X) = Σ_i s_i F_i(X) = Σ_{i,j} s_i s_j p_ij d_ij(X) over ordered pairs. Actors are myopic (pairwise assessments only) and move one at a time to lower their own frustration, so the system runs downhill to local minima, the stable alignments.
- **The threat special case, in their words.** Realist theory (states form alliances "to resist aggression by other powerful states") "is equivalent to saying all propensities are equal and negative", and "the predictions of landscape theory under realist assumptions are precisely balance of power alliances" (pp. 219-220). [derived] With p_ij = −c for all pairs, E = −2c·S_A·S_B for two blocs of total sizes S_A and S_B, minimised when S_A = S_B: any size-balanced split is a local optimum, so **equal-negative propensities do not say which split forms**. *Confirmed in validation by the authors' own test (p. 224) [read]:* with all propensities set to −1 and the same sizes, the seventeen nations have "not two but 209 different stable configurations, none of which was as accurate as the landscape theory prediction", and the five Great Powers have four stable configurations, none of them the actual alignment. A threat that is only "everyone fears everyone" therefore predicts almost nothing about who allies with whom.
- **The test.** Seventeen European nations in 1936; sizes from the Correlates of War capabilities index (six demographic, industrial and military components); propensity of each of 136 pairs an equal-weight combination of five indicators (*corrected in validation* from "five ±1 indicators"; their note 28: ethnic conflict, a border disagreement and a recent history of war count −1 each when present; religion +1 within and −1 across major categories, weighted by each country's religious proportions; government type +1 if the same and −1 if different, scored only between democratic, fascist and communist governments; economics omitted for lack of data); alliance data were not used. Of the 65,536 configurations, two are local minima. Configuration 1 matches the actual Second World War alignment except for Poland and Portugal (15 of 17 right, 96% of total size), and has the larger basin (47,945 configurations against 17,591); Configuration 2 is a pro- against anti-Soviet split. The authors compute a chance probability of 0.0047 for one of two predictions making so few errors.
- **Critique.** Galam (1998, *BJPS* 28(2): 411-412, doi:10.1017/s0007123498210325) argued that the model does not contain most of the claimed results, and Axelrod & Bennett replied (28(2): 412-414, doi:10.1017/s0007123498220990); I saw the titles and a search snippet, not the texts [sa]. My own caveats [derived]: the five-factor coding and equal weights were fixed with the outcome known; the indicators (border disagreement, recent war, ideology) already encode antagonism, so the test cannot separate threat from a pre-existing sign.
- **Context they cite [quoted in A&B].** Walt (1987) shows that states balance against particular threats; Snyder (1984) that states have general interests (security) and particular conflicts and affinities, which together form a "tacit pattern of alignment"; neither was read.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: actor with size s_i; symmetric pair weight p_ij (the sign input); a partition of actors. Operator: single-actor switch that lowers F_i. This is the closest existing structure to v0.1's A(t) with Π_t, with p_ij playing the role of the sign input and sizes the role of weights; it generates blocs from signs, not signs from threat.
- **Era / medium / scale.** 1936 data, 1939-41 outcomes; states, historical documents; group of groups (nation-states as operands); no intervention.
- **Size.** Size enters explicitly and linearly in both frustration and energy; balance-of-power equilibria are balanced by size, not by count.

### 6.4 Choi & Bowles (2007) [abs] and the balance-theory instance [derived]

- Choi & Bowles, "The coevolution of parochial altruism and war", *Science* 318(5850): 636-640, doi:10.1126/science.1144237 [abs]: "neither parochialism nor altruism would have been viable singly, but by promoting group conflict, they could have evolved jointly" (game-theoretic analysis and agent-based simulation of late Pleistocene and early Holocene conditions). That is the evolutionary packaging of the two halves as one trait. Closed; equations not read.
- **Balance theory.** Cartwright & Harary's structure theorem (as stated and checked in 096): a signed graph is balanced iff its points split into two mutually exclusive subsets, positive lines within each and negative lines across; a balanced group is a labelling x: V → {+1, −1} with sign(ij) = x_i x_j. [derived] A shared external threat is a candidate **generator of the labelling x** (exposed group against source), but the theorem does not pick the labelling: any bipartition is balanced if the signs follow it, and v0.1 takes the signs as input. One of the two subsets may be empty (all positive).
- **In-repo formal relative (cross-reference added in validation).** 072 reads Turchin's asabiya equation as restated by Cheon & Poghosyan (*Historical Dynamics* itself not accessed there): cohesion S grows logistically at a rate that is positive only while the polity is small (A < 2b), read as "mostly frontier", and decays otherwise. It is the repository's one formal "external pressure raises in-group cohesion" law; like the models above it yields a group quantity, not a tie sign, and 072 records that the frontier enters only through that size factor, with no frontier or contact variable.

## 7. Quantitative evidence, field, laboratory, animal and crowd

### 7.1 Bauer et al. (IZA DP 9997, 2016): war exposure and cooperation [read in full]

- **Design.** A review and reanalysis of observational studies. 23 papers on violence and social behaviour were identified; 19 concern war; 16 had the right outcome and available microdata and were reanalysed with the original designs, outcomes standardised to mean 0 and SD 1, fixed- and random-effects pooling (17 estimates, because one paper has two countries). Exposure is observational (rebel raiding patterns, household victimisation); the authors say no strategy "can fully eliminate concerns about bias from selection and omitted variables".
- **Pooled effects.** Summary cooperation index: +0.07 to +0.08 SD, p < 0.01 under both models, "only moderate magnitude". By outcome, fixed-effects estimates are positive and significant for social-group participation, community leadership and participation, prosocial behaviour in games, voting and political knowledge; random-effects estimates are significant only for game behaviour and marginal for participation and leadership; **trust is near zero**.
- **In-group against out-group (game outcomes).** Toward in-group partners +0.24 to +0.25 SD (p < 0.01); toward out-group partners +0.04 SD, not significant; "there is no indication of negative effects towards the out-group", yet significantly less prosocial behaviour toward them than toward the in-group. "Too few studies define 'out-groups' consistently (or at all), so this in-group bias remains somewhat speculative." In-group = same village, class, family or ethnic group; out-group = a distant village, another ethnic group or another part of the country.
- **Examples.** Sierra Leone (war 1991-2002), eight years on: exposed people were 23 percentage points less selfish and 25 percentage points more inequality averse toward in-group (same-village) partners, with no comparable effect toward a distant-village partner; stronger when exposed as children (Bauer, Cassar, Chytilová & Henrich 2014, as reported). Young Sierra Leonean footballers exposed to more violence were more altruistic to teammates and not to opponents, and **received more yellow or red cards** (Cecchi et al., cited in the paper as 2015; published as Cecchi, Leuveld & Voors 2016, *Economic Development and Cultural Change* 64(3): 405-435, doi:10.1086/684969, Crossref-confirmed in validation; the main-text out-group-antagonism datum). *Added in validation:* the paper's footnote 2 adds a second one without an in-group measure: professional players in the major European leagues who lived in conflict settings as children commit more violent card fouls against the opposing team (Miguel, Saiegh & Satyanath 2011, *Economics & Politics* 23(1): 59-73, doi:10.1111/j.1468-0343.2010.00372.x) [read, as reported]. Georgia 2008: children six months after the war, same in-group/out-group pattern. Tajikistan 1992-97 (neighbour against neighbour): exposure to violence *reduced* trust, most where opposing groups lived intermixed, while participation in local groups still rose (Cassar, Grosjean & Whitt). 35-country survey, about 40,000 respondents, Second World War and later wars: more community and political participation, less trust in central institutions (Grosjean).
- **Moderators.** No decline with years since the war (slope +0.01, not significant under random effects; *added in validation:* "the fixed effects estimate, reported in the online appendix, is significant", and the authors write that the effects "do not diminish with time; indeed, if anything, the opposite seems to be true"); crime victimisation studies give similar or somewhat larger effects (difference 0.03), so "the 'war' aspect may not be at the core"; personal-exposure measures give smaller coefficients than community-level measures; exposure as a civilian gives larger effects than as a combatant. Other survival threats (earthquake and tsunami damage; post-election violence) are reported to show the same direction [as cited by the authors].
- **Mechanisms.** Not decided: economic channels (insurance, protection), parochial altruism (genetic, or gene-culture), post-traumatic growth against distress, expressive preferences. Their reading: the evidence favours a change in social preferences or norm adherence, "suggestive evidence" that it is parochial.
- **Era / medium / scale / consent.** Wars 1991-2010; collection years are not stated in the text I read, and the paper's own time-since-war figures (for example "three to five years after" the Sierra Leone war, "eight years post-conflict", "nine years after" Burundi) put them at roughly 2005-2012 [derived]; in-person surveys and incentivised games in villages and schools; membership and community scale; observational, no intervention.
- **Size / composition.** Individual and household exposure against community-level exposure; children against adults; the village as the typical in-group; no population-size contrast.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: person with an exposure level (personal, household or community) and a social-preference parameter toward in-group and out-group partners. Operator: `expose` shifts the in-group parameter (+0.24 to +0.25 SD) and leaves the out-group one near zero; no dynamics or set point is estimated.
- **Caveat on version.** Working paper; the JEP text was not read.

### 7.2 Gneezy & Fessler (2012): the 2006 Israel-Hezbollah war [read in full]

- **Design.** The same housing facility for senior citizens in Tel Aviv; two sessions nine months before the war, two in the second week of the 34-day war, one a year after; each session 10 pairs; pen and paper, anonymous, real stakes, strategy method. Ultimatum game (endowment NIS 100) for costly punishment, trust game (NIS 50) for costly reward.
- **Results.** Pre- and post-war behaviour did not differ and were pooled as "peacetime" (30 Player As and 30 Bs) against "wartime" (20 and 20). Wartime second movers rejected unfair ultimatum offers below 40% of the endowment more often (Mann-Whitney p < 0.01) and, in the trust game, returned more after large transfers and less after small ones (regression slope contrast χ²(1) = 22.43, p < 0.05; the printed slopes 0.024 and 0.05 are given with an ambiguous "respectively"). The authors' reading: "rather than simply increasing within-group solidarity", conflict raises sanctioning of non-cooperators and rewarding of cooperators.
- **For the sign law.** Under threat, ties inside the group *polarise by compliance*: more positive to cooperators, more negative to defectors. That is Coser's intolerance (L2) in a game. The authors flag that war "may imperil cultural plurality and democracy as much from within as from without".
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: second mover with a rejection threshold and a return schedule. Operator: `expose` raises the threshold and steepens the schedule (a sanction gain).
- **Standing.** 100 participants in five sessions of 20, of whom the analysed second movers were 20 in wartime and 30 in peacetime; older adults not called up; one war, one site; the partner was an anonymous co-resident, so the game measures in-group enforcement only. Era 2005-2007 (sessions about nine months before, during and a year after the July-August 2006 war); face to face, pen and paper; membership scale; natural experiment, consenting players, no manipulation.

### 7.3 Whitehouse et al. (2014): Libyan battalions [read in full]

- **Design.** 179 male Libyan revolutionaries (mean age 28.0) in four Misratan battalions, surveyed in 2011 in under five minutes; 42 frontline fighters and 137 battalion non-fighters (logistical supporters). Pictorial identity-fusion scale for four targets: own family, own battalion, other battalions, ordinary Libyans who supported the revolution without joining a battalion.
- **Results.** Fused with own family 99%, own battalion 97%, other battalions 96%; **ordinary supporters 1%**. Peacetime national samples elsewhere range from 6% (Europe) to 41% (Spain) fusion with their nation. Asked which group they were most fused with: 68% family, 32% own battalion, none the other battalions or ordinary Libyans; among fighters 45% most fused with the battalion against 28% of non-fighters (χ²(1) = 4.13, p = 0.04). In later interviews many participants viewed non-combatants "as free-riders", which the authors think "may have sown the seeds for the dissension that emerged among the revolutionaries after the war."
- **For the sign law.** Shared risk, not shared cause, set the boundary: nearly everyone who bore risk (in any battalion) was inside, and fellow believers who did not bear risk were outside. The in-group extended to *allied* battalions (96%), a nested boundary, but that is fusion with fellow combatants and says nothing about enemies. The authors say causation may run either way (fusion leads to volunteering, or fighting produces fusion).
- **Era / medium / scale / consent.** 2011; in-person questionnaire during the war; group (battalions of micro-groups of 3 to 5 fighters); observational, voluntary participation.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: person with a fusion level toward each of four targets. Operator: shared risk (frontline service) sets fusion toward the battalion and its allies and leaves it near zero for non-risk-bearing co-believers.
- **Size / composition.** Micro-groups of 3-5 fighters inside battalions; fighters as against supporters; younger men more fused with the battalion than family (r = −0.15, p = 0.048).

### 7.4 De Dreu et al. (2016): defence against aggression in the lab [read, main text]

- **Design.** Intergroup aggressor-defender conflict game: three-person aggressor group against three-person defender group, each member with 20 experimental euros to contribute (wasted) to the group pool; if the aggressors' pool exceeds the defenders', aggressors take the defenders' remaining resources; otherwise both keep their remainder. 276 participants in 46 sessions (reported as 92 aggressor-defender contests), two experiments (median ages 21 and 22), anonymous in separate cubicles, consenting.
- **Results.** Contributions to in-group defence exceeded those to out-group aggression (baseline: aggression 2.4 below, defence 0.9 above the mixed Nash value; Nash predicts aggressor wins 32.45%, observed 22.5%); within-group dispersion was larger for aggression; defender groups survived about 70% of attacks. Experiment 1: peer punishment raised aggression contributions and cut non-contributors in aggressor groups from 23% to 13% but **did not raise success** (23.75%). Experiment 2: **sequential decision-making** (one member moves first and others see it) cut aggressors' non-contributors from 31% to 23% and almost doubled their success from 20% to 35%, with coordination (intraclass correlation) predicting success (r = 0.30); it did nothing for defenders. Archival comparison in the paper: group-hunting predators about one success in three, hostile takeovers about 40%, interstate aggressors under 30% of conflicts.
- **For the sign law.** Members randomly assigned to a defending group contribute more, and more evenly, than members randomly assigned to an attacking group. *Corrected in validation:* the first draft said "the same people" and called the "threat to us" frame the strong one, but the Materials and Methods say "the experimental instructions used neutral language throughout (e.g., groups were referred to as group A and group B, contributions were labeled investments, and terms such as in-group defense and out-group aggression were avoided)" [read]. The asymmetry is produced by the payoff role (defending one's own endowment against being taken, as against taking the other's), not by any verbal threat frame, which makes it a structural result. A minimal leader (first mover) matters for aggression and not for defence, the lab counterpart of Sherif's leader-sanctioned raids.
- **Era / medium / scale / consent.** Approved by the University of Amsterdam Psychology Research Ethics Board under files "2014-WOP-3451 and 2015-WOP-4531", so run about 2014-2015 (*corrected in validation* from "years not stated"); subjects recruited on the university campus for a study announced as "human decision making in groups"; written informed consent, "no deception", debriefing, €10 show-up fee plus a mean €3.62; laboratory, anonymous, in separate cubicles (no face-to-face contact between participants); group of 3 against 3. The manipulations are the role (aggressor or defender payoffs), peer punishment (Experiment 1) and sequential decisions (Experiment 2), all with neutral wording.
- **Operand/operator translation.** Operands: member with a contribution g_i to a pool C; two groups in a contest. Operators: `contest` (win if C_aggressor > C_defender), `punish`, and `lead` (first-mover sequencing).
- **Size.** Three per side by design; not varied.

### 7.5 Animals: Radford, Majolo & Aureli (2016) [read, sections 1-4]

- A *prospective* review that says "relatively little research has considered the behavioural impacts of between-group conflict". Findings reported there: no post-conflict change in within-group aggression or affiliation in ring-tailed lemurs and vervets; **increased** allo-grooming in blue and samango monkeys; experimentally induced intrusions raised within-group affiliation in a cooperatively breeding cichlid and in Wied's marmosets, "a greater increase in larger compared with smaller groups" in the marmosets; green woodhoopoes (all members take part in conflicts) allo-preened more afterwards, in proportion to the duration of the encounter, most after long lost conflicts, driven by the dominant pair preening subordinates (a reward reading); male bonnet macaques that fought showed *more* aggression to non-combatant females afterwards. Playback of **unfamiliar** groups raised woodhoopoe preening more than neighbours, while **neighbours** raised cichlid affiliation more than strangers: the sign of the rival-identity effect differs by species.
- Their predictions about group size are conditional and opposed: smaller groups may need more post-conflict affiliation to secure contributions, larger groups may have more free-riding and more partner availability.
- **Era / medium / scale.** Field and captive studies, various years; animal; group; some experimental intrusions (no consent question).
- **For the algebra.** The in-group rise is not universal even among cooperative group-living species and depends on who fought, who won, how long, and species social structure.

### 7.6 Crowd-scale opinion and the online medium

- **Schraff (2021) [read in part].** Representative Dutch LISS panel, about 1,800 respondents, March 2020, trust in parliament on 0-10 (constant about 5.3-5.4). Placebo lockdown dates 1 and 5 days before the real one give positive, significant "effects" (0.655, se 0.099, and 0.416, se 0.102; p < 0.01); the two placebos "have the same positive and statistically significant effect", from which Schraff concludes that the lockdown effect is a pre-existing time trend; with a spline in time the real-date term falls to 0.204 (se 0.294). *Corrected in validation:* the first draft attached the two numbers to "the actual date and" the placebos; both are placebos, and Table 1 prints no linear estimate at the actual date. Kernel-regularised estimates (n = 1,585): cumulative Covid-19 cases +0.154 (se 0.07), lockdown +0.24 (se 0.165, not significant), satisfaction with the economy +0.756 and social trust +0.602 (units of the case variable not stated in the table). The daily standard deviation of trust fell during March (more homogeneous). It is a rally in *institutional trust*, not an out-group tie. Online panel; March 2020; crowd (national opinion); observational.
- **Bol et al. (2021) [abs].** Web survey, March-April 2020, Western Europe, interrupted time series: lockdowns raised vote intention for the leader's party, trust in government and satisfaction with democracy, "while rallying individuals around current leaders and institutions" they had no effect on left-right attitudes. Schraff reproduces their method on Dutch data and finds the lockdown term disappears under a flexible time trend, so what drives the 2020 rally (the lockdown or the case counts) is disputed.
- **Baker & Oneal (2001) [abs].** All 193 militarised interstate disputes 1933-1992 and US presidential popularity: "minor, statistically insignificant rallies associated with uses of force", sizeable ones for particular subcategories; larger when the US is revisionist originator, when a full interstate war starts and with prominent headline placement; "rallies are more likely when they are associated with White House statements and bipartisan support"; the size of a rally "depends primarily on how the crisis is presented to the public in terms of media coverage, bipartisan support, and White House spin." Mueller (1970, *APSR* 64(1): 18-34) [abs, first paragraphs only] models Gallup's approval question for the incumbent (asked "some 300 times", 1945 to January 1969) with four predictors: time in office, "major international events", economic slump and war. *Corrected in validation:* the first draft called Mueller "the origin of the measure"; the question is Gallup's, and Mueller's contribution is the model of it.
- **Gallup 2001 (D. W. Moore, "Bush job approval highest in Gallup history", Gallup News Service, 24 September 2001) [read in validation; was fetch-extract].** Presidential approval 51% (7-10 September 2001), 86% (14-15 September), 90% (21-22 September, n = 1,005 adults, telephone, ±3 points), "the highest presidential job approval rating ever measured by Gallup", one point above the previous high (the elder Bush at the end of the 1991 Gulf War). Era 2001; telephone; crowd; no intervention. This is the benchmark rally; it measures approval of a leader, not ties among citizens.
- **Garcia & Rimé (2019) [read in validation, Europe PMC full text; was fetch-extract].** 62,114 Twitter users located in France; 1 April 2015 to 30 June 2016, 17,899,591 tweets; French-adapted LIWC; after the 13 November 2015 attacks, a collective emotion "kept the levels of negative affect above their baseline for more than a week", while "solidarity" measures (social-process terms, prosocial terms, shared-values terms) spiked "not exactly on the day of the attacks but the day afterward" and relaxed toward baseline; at the individual level, social-process, prosocial and positive-affect terms stayed higher "in the months after the attacks" for users who took part most in the collective emotion, with "weaker effects of emotions in shared values" (*corrected in validation* from "shared values returning fastest"). Out-group references, hostility and we-versus-they language were **not measured**, and the authors say they do not know when collective emotion yields broad solidarity or intolerance of out-groups. Online (one platform, one attack, active French-located users); crowd and membership; observational.
- **Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021) [read in validation, Europe PMC full text; was fetch-extract].** 2,730,215 posts from US news-media accounts and members of Congress on Twitter and Facebook (the most recent up-to-3,200 tweets per account; on Facebook, the 300,000 most recent posts from the media lists, plus the Congress members' posts); the authors date the data only as "the years leading up to the 2020 election" (*corrected in validation:* "2016-2020" in the first draft is not in the main text). In their random-effects meta-analysis over eight datasets, each word naming the political out-group multiplied the odds of a share or retweet by about 1.67 (printed 95% CI [1.43, 1.69], which sits oddly around its own point estimate; this is the inconsistency the extraction flagged, now confirmed in the text); in-group words had no significant effect (1.05, CI [0.90, 1.22], P = 0.563); across datasets, "an estimated 65 to 180% increase in the odds of being shared per out-group word"; out-group words predicted "angry" reactions, in-group words "love" reactions. Online; crowd (adoption); observational, US only, pre-election period. The authors flag their own datedness: because elite language changes with which party is in power and US polarisation is at a historic high, "it is unclear whether these results would generalize to different time periods or nations".
- **Ross (1985) [abs].** A sample of 90 pre-industrial societies "supports the proposition that the two [internal and external conflict] have a moderate, positive relationship"; "the strength and patterns of cross-cutting ties are very different in societies in which internal and external conflict covary and societies in which internal and external conflict are independent." The sign is opposite to the substitution law, and the moderator is the one Coser and Brewer name.

## 8. What the sign law would be: candidate forms and validity domains

Vocabulary from v0.1: persons i, j; the partition Π_t of persons into groups; `sgn(a_ij)` the influence-sign input. The ties measured in the sources above are **solidarity or affect ties** s_ij (friendship choice, cooperation, fusion, trust), not influence weights a_ij; whether they map to a_ij is open (107, 104). A threat T has a source h, an exposed set E, and a severity and duration. A functional relation F(g, h) between groups is competitive, independent or shared-goal. Each form below uses only [read] support; the other sources are named as counter-evidence or as unconfirmed.

**S-A. Shared-exposure solidarity (in-group half).** For i, j ∈ E, members of a group that existed before the threat and can act together: s_ij rises, and the intensity of norm enforcement κ_g rises (reward of cooperators, punishment of defectors). *Evidence [read]:* Sherif Hypothesis 2 with its defeat qualification; Bauer et al. (+0.24 to +0.25 SD toward in-group partners; +0.07 to +0.08 pooled); Gneezy & Fessler; Whitehouse et al.; De Dreu et al. (defence outperforms aggression). *Does not need an out-group:* the pooled effect is similar for crime victims in Bauer et al.'s own comparison, the same direction is reported at second hand for earthquakes and tsunamis (studies they cite), and the Dutch trust rally follows case counts (Schraff). *Fails or reverses [read]:* immediately after a defeat (Sherif, hours to days); when the war splits neighbours (Cassar et al.); animal studies without effect (lemurs, vervets). *Magnitude:* small to moderate at community scale, near ceiling among combatants. *Duration:* no decay over years in the pooled slope; within-camp recovery in days; online peak next day.

**S-B. Conditional out-group sign (out-group half).** For i ∈ g, j ∈ h ≠ g: s_ij < 0 only if g perceives h as the source of T or as an obstacle (F(g, h) competitive); otherwise s_ij ≈ 0, and the *default is zero, not negative*. *Evidence [read]:* Sherif (53% and 76.9% unfavourable ratings after friction, no baseline; indifference, not hostility, toward neutral outsiders at the beach); Bauer et al. (+0.04 SD toward out-group, no negative effect, plus football fouls in one study); Hammond & Axelrod (out-group action is non-cooperation); Brewer's argument (read, but an argument whose data I have at second hand). Context that does not count toward the grade under the rule above: Duckitt & Mphuthing (coupling only toward the perceived source) and Brewer & Campbell (r ≈ .00), both [quoted in Brewer]; Balliet et al. and Halevy et al. [abs]. *Competing reading [read]:* Coser's non-realistic conflict, where the target is any "suitable" antagonist, which predicts hostility without a named source; I found no test that separates the two, and the natural-disaster results in S-A suggest the in-group half appears without any target at all.

**S-C. Nested boundary (common enemy).** If a third group k is perceived as the common source of T for g and h, previously antagonistic, s_ij may become positive across g and h while s_ik and s_jk are negative: the boundary moves up one level (Coser: conflict may "maintain boundary lines"). *Evidence [read]:* Sherif's statement that the 1949 study used it (no data in the text read); the 96% fusion with other battalions (Whitehouse) as a single compatible datum; the all-negative-propensity bloc structure in landscape theory (a formal consequence, not a test; and the authors' own all-negative run gave 209 stable alignments for 17 nations, none as accurate as their coded propensities, so it barely supports S-C: added in validation). *Unread tests (added in validation):* Feshbach & Singer (1957) on personal against shared threats and prejudice, and Burnstein & McRae (1962) on shared threat in racially mixed groups, titles and Crossref metadata only (section 15). *Against [read, assertion]:* Brewer's claim that anticipated interdependence with an out-group raises distrust, and that common threat with prior contempt gives scapegoating (an abstract-level laboratory result in her support, Deschamps & Brown 1983, does not count toward the grade). **The weakest of the five.**

**S-D. Leader gate on cross-boundary aggression.** Aggressive acts by members of g toward h occur only if the leader or high-status members sanction them; proposals far exceed executions. *Evidence [read]:* Sherif (no raid without leader approval; "the number of raids suggested ... far exceeded the number actually carried out"); De Dreu et al. (first-mover leadership raises aggressors' success 20% to 35% and is irrelevant to defence). Benard's mixed result on support for centralised leadership [abs] does not count toward the grade, nor do two classic tests of threat or crisis and leadership that exist and were not read, Hamblin (1958) and Mulder & Stemerding (1963) (titles and Crossref metadata only, added in validation). *Gap:* v0.1's gates K⁻, K⁺ and θ are thresholds on incoming state; none is an approval variable of a third party.

**S-E. Substitution of internal by external conflict.** For identical contestants the internal and external conflict levels are substitutes at equilibrium, and a group that can commit externally first chooses enough to remove the internal (Münster & Staal, theorem). *Evidence [read]:* none beyond the model; Coser's excerpt (struggle suppresses dissent and splits) and Gneezy & Fessler (punishment of defectors) are compatible. *Against:* Ross [abs] (positive association in 90 societies) and the Tajikistan result (neighbour war lowered trust).

**What I would carry into v0.2 [derived].** (1) S-A and S-B as two separate laws with separate evidence grades: S-A tested in at least five independent designs (camp, lab, village surveys, battalions, a natural experiment), S-B tested directly in one field experiment (Sherif) and found near zero in the pooled analysis I read (Bauer et al.); second-hand and abstract-level sources agree (Brewer & Campbell via Brewer; Balliet et al. and Halevy et al. [abs]) but do not count toward the grade. (2) A perceived-source variable and a functional-relation variable as **inputs** (structure, not drive). (3) A leader-approval gate on cross-boundary hostile acts. (4) The default across a boundary is 0, with negative values requiring S-B's conditions. (5) S-C and S-E flagged "stated, not tested". (6) Every magnitude tagged with its era and medium (section 9).

**What would refute or sharpen them [derived].** S-A: matched groups at crowd scale online show no rise in within-group positive links after a shared external shock (D610's datasets could test this). S-B: out-group negative links rise after a threat with *no* attributable source (a disaster) as much as after one with a named source, which would favour Coser's "any suitable target". S-D: executions of hostile acts without any leader sanction at a size where a leader cannot sanction (online crowds). S-E: a population where internal and external conflict rise together without cross-cutting ties (Ross's covarying case).

## 10. Group size and composition, collected

- **Sumner.** "The size of the groups is determined by the conditions of the struggle for existence"; intensity of internal organisation rises with the proximity and strength of the other group. No numbers.
- **Coser.** The closer (more total-personality) the group, the more intense its conflicts; multiplicity of conflicts stands in inverse relation to intensity; groups in continued struggle claim total involvement. No numbers.
- **Sherif.** 11 per group, strangers, matched, homogeneous (Protestant, middle-class, 11 years old). Held fixed, so the experiment cannot say what composition or size change. Tyerman & Spencer's well-acquainted scouts [abs] are the one contrast and went the other way.
- **Brewer.** Loyalty is most readily engaged by small distinctive groups; single-axis dichotomous splits raise the coupling, cross-cutting identities lower it (more than 200 students: memberships uncorrelated); collectivist cultures show sharper boundaries.
- **Bauer et al.** In-group is the village, class, family or ethnic group; community-level exposure gives bigger coefficients than personal exposure; exposure as a civilian bigger than as a combatant; children exposed show larger and longer effects (Sierra Leone). Too few studies define the out-group.
- **Whitehouse.** Battalions built of micro-groups of 3 to 5 fighters; frontline fighters (n = 42) more fused with the battalion than supporters (n = 137).
- **De Dreu.** 3 against 3; leadership (first mover) matters for the attacking side only.
- **Radford.** Larger marmoset groups showed a bigger post-conflict affiliation rise; theory says small groups need more contributions and large groups have more free-riding; the species social structure (cooperative breeders against multi-male primates) changes the result.
- **Münster & Staal.** Exact: internal-conflict threshold a(m−1)/m rises with members per group m; external-conflict threshold b(G−1)/G rises with the number of groups G; switch at G = √n when a = b [derived]; larger groups need more outside effort to keep internal peace [derived].
- **Hammond & Axelrod.** 625, 2,500 and 10,000 sites give 70.5%, 76.3% and 78.2% ethnocentric agents; 2, 4 and 8 colours give 69.4%, 76.3% and 79.1%.
- **Axelrod & Bennett.** Size-weighted frustration; blocs balanced by size, not by count.
- **Gneezy & Fessler, Garcia & Rimé, Schraff, Gallup.** Sessions of 20; 62,114 users; about 1,800 respondents; 1,005 respondents: crowd-scale numbers are samples of a population, not groups of known size.

## 11. Datedness and the fluid reading

- **Form against magnitude.** The *form* of S-A (shared exposure raises solidarity and enforcement) shows in a 1954 camp, a 2006 city under rockets, a 2011 revolution, villages after wars of 1991-2010, a 2015 social-media shock and a 2020 national survey; the *magnitudes* run from +0.07 SD pooled to near-ceiling 97% fusion, and from a 93% in-group friendship share to Gallup's 51 to 90 swing. The magnitudes are medium- and population-bound and should enter the algebra as tagged, soft settings with slack, not constants.
- **The out-group half is where change is claimed.** The one online measurement of the out-group half (Rathje et al., an extraction when written, read at source in validation) has out-group words multiplying shares by about 1.67 and in-group words doing nothing; if so, the platform *rewards* the S-B pattern, and an observed rise in out-group hostility online may be made partly by the medium, not by threat. Its authors date it themselves: "the years leading up to the 2020 election", and "it is unclear whether these results would generalize to different time periods or nations". That is a D606 (file 114) and D611 question; this file only flags that the sign law measured offline is not the sign law distributed by a feed.
- **Rally effects depend on the elite environment.** Baker & Oneal's abstract makes the rally depend on White House statements, bipartisan support and headline placement, so a transmission step (elite framing) sits between threat and crowd opinion. Claims that polarisation shrank later rallies appeared in journalism I saw only in search snippets [sa]; I read no peer-reviewed test, and Schraff's and Bol's 2020 results are rallies in institutional trust, with their cause disputed.
- **Boundaries are era-dependent.** Hammond & Axelrod and Brewer both say the trait that defines "we" is shaped by culture; Brewer's cross-cutting argument predicts the S-B coupling should weaken as identity complexity rises and strengthen with single-axis polarised splits. Both are testable on D610's data.
- **Experimental conditions that cannot recur.** Sherif's groups were kept apart, strangers, and manipulated without knowing; today's groups are never isolated, and the design would not pass ethical review. The one acquaintance contrast (Tyerman & Spencer) failed to show the solidarity rise.
- **Known laws change behaviour.** Diversion by a leader is an old playbook (Bodin, Swift, Shakespeare, quoted in Münster & Staal); Baker & Oneal's "spin" is the law used on purpose. Whether members who know the law discount the rally, or the threat, is untested (93 for the general reflexivity argument).
- **Persistence against transience.** No decay of cooperative effects with years since war in the pooled slope (+0.01: not significant under random effects, significant under fixed effects, so if anything rising; the fixed-effects half added in validation) but temporary friction after a camp defeat (hours to days) and a Twitter peak the next day [read in validation]. The algebra needs separate time scales, with an era tag on each. For how far 1950s-1990s magnitudes such as Sherif's or Brewer & Campbell's can be trusted now, see 113 (D605, decline, period and cohort effects); this file does not repeat it.

## 12. Operand and operator translation

- **Operands.** Person i; group g (a cell of Π_t); threat T = (source h, exposed set E, severity, duration); **functional relation** F(g, h) ∈ {competitive, independent, shared-goal} (a new, non-drive operand, the pair-goal structure Sherif makes primary; compare 107's Object sort); perceived-source flag σ(g → h); solidarity tie s_ij (not v0.1's influence weight a_ij); norm-enforcement intensity κ_g (a group quantity); leader role ℓ_g; fusion f_ig (Whitehouse's pictorial scale, a relational state); boundary B_g (who counts as "we").
- **Operators read or implied.** `expose(g, T)` (S-A trigger); `rally(g)` (s_ij and κ_g up); `sanction(i)` and `reward(i)` (Gneezy & Fessler; Sherif's group sanctions); `attribute(T → h)` (the missing operator behind S-B and Sherif's 1953 failure); `raid(g → h)` gated by `approve(ℓ_g)` (S-D); `commit(g, y)` (Münster & Staal); `split(g)` and `withdraw(i)` (Coser, Sherif's letters); `join-against(g, h; k)` (S-C, stated only); `reach-across(g, h)` by a shared goal (107).
- **Mapping to v0.1.** The partition Π_t and leaver set L_t carry `split` and `withdraw`; the sign input `sgn(a_ij)` stays an input, now with two named generators (S-A for within-group, S-B for across) and a default of zero across a boundary; "threat" in v0.1 is a cost inside a gate (section 3, "Not forced"), and this file supplies **no state dynamics for threat either**: only a set of events that shift ties conditional on exposure, attribution and leadership. A leader-approval gate is new.
- **What I would not carry.** Any magnitude without its era and medium tag; S-C and S-E as laws; the Sumner covariation as a constraint (it is one run's pattern in Sherif and a null in the cross-group correlation Brewer quotes).

## 13. What exists versus what does not

- **Exists.** A 1906 verbal law and a 1956 set of structural qualifications (Sumner, Coser); a 1954 field experiment with the pattern and its defeat qualification (Sherif); a 2016 pooled analysis of 16 war-exposure studies with a small positive in-group effect and a null out-group effect (Bauer et al.); lab and field demonstrations of enforcement (Gneezy & Fessler), fusion (Whitehouse) and defence-over-attack (De Dreu); an animal review that finds the effect inconsistent (Radford et al.); a conceptual argument and cited data that the halves are separable (Brewer); three formal versions that each take the sign or payoff structure as given (Münster & Staal, Hammond & Axelrod, Axelrod & Bennett); a verbal and abstract-level record of conditions (Stein, Ross, Tyerman & Spencer, Baker & Oneal).
- **Does not exist in what I could access.** Stein's list of intervening variables; any equation that maps (threat, attribution, functional relation, leadership) to a tie sign; any test that varies a threat with and without an attributable source on the same population; any pre-competition sociometric baseline in a Robbers Cave-type design; any replication of Sherif with strangers; any test of the common-enemy move beyond Sherif's assertion and one fusion rate; any test of S-A or S-B at crowd scale with ties (not trust or sharing) as outcome; any measure of out-group hostility in the Twitter solidarity study; any test of whether the effects survive being known; any dose-response in threat intensity at the same scale.
- **"Does not exist" tested in validation.** The web-search budget was spent, so these were tested by re-reading the sources above and by checking specific known works at Crossref, not by open search. (a) *Pre-competition sociometric baseline:* false as a general claim. The 1949 and 1953 runs had a friendship stage whose choices reversed toward the experimental in-groups (Sherif ch. 2) [read], and Tyerman & Spencer's "no increase during camps" implies repeated measures [abs]; it holds for the 1954 run only. (b) *Common-enemy tests:* Feshbach & Singer (1957) and Burnstein & McRae (1962) test shared threat on cross-group attitudes (titles and metadata, unread). (c) *Crowd scale with ties as the outcome:* Phan & Airoldi (2015, *PNAS* 112(21): 6595-6600, doi:10.1073/pnas.1404770112) [abs]: 1.5 million college students on Facebook over four years around a hurricane; "affected individuals are more likely to strengthen interactions, while maintaining the same number of friends". That is S-A's form online after a disaster, in tie strength rather than new ties. (d) *Out-group hostility online after the Paris attacks:* Magdy, Darwish, Abokhodair, Rahimi & Baldwin (2016, ACM Web Science, doi:10.1145/2908131.2908150) exists (title and metadata only); the claim stays true of Garcia & Rimé alone. (e) *Dose-response:* the file's own sources hold three partial ones (Schraff, trust rising with cumulative cases, +0.154; Radford et al., woodhoopoe preening rising with encounter duration; Sumner's verbal clause), none on ties. (f) *An equation from threat to cohesion:* Turchin's asabiya (072) exists, for cohesion, not a tie sign, so the claim stands as written. (g) *Replication with strangers:* Diab (1970) is reported [sa] and unconfirmed. Not tested: varied attributability on one population; survival of the effects once known.

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

**What reduces.** (a) *The in-group half, in part.* A shared threat raising affiliation is a person-scale coupling, fear to belonging, which 49 and 51 already carry with the cautions recorded there (supported by Schachter's small samples, attachment and tend-and-befriend rather than by terror management, and conditional on the usefulness of affiliation, per 49). Brewer's inclusion need, held against a differentiation need, is a drive-like state with a set point, and it explains why identification with small distinctive groups is the one that engages. Sherif's boys appear to supply a competitive appetite on discovery of the other group, before any frustration. (b) *The out-group-negative half, in part:* anger or aggression toward an obstacle (frustration with an attributed source), which Sherif's general hypothesis names, plus fear. (c) *Status striving*: Sherif's leaders rose and fell with their stance on the intergroup trend, and low-status members were hypothesised to out-aggress for rank (inconclusive).

**What does NOT reduce.** (1) **The functional relation between the groups and the goal** (competitive, independent or shared). Sherif puts the cause there and "not primarily" in within-group patterns or individual frustration; 107 reaches the same place for rivalry (a negative tie needs an object structure, not only appetites). It is a property of the pair and the goal, and v0.1 has no sort for it. (2) **The attribution of the source**: a belief about who caused the frustration. The 1953 failure (the staff became the target) shows the sign following attribution, not exposure, and Duckitt & Mphuthing as reported by Brewer (hostility only toward the perceived source) point the same way at second hand. (3) **The boundary itself**: shared risk, not shared cause, set it in Libya (1% fusion with supporters who did not fight); culture and categorisation set it in the simulations; conflict re-draws it (Coser). Cross-cutting memberships moderate the coupling (Brewer, Coser) and, in Ross's sample [abs], the sign of the internal-external association; that is composition, not drive. (4) **The leader gate and coordination procedures**: raids needed the leader's approval; a first-mover raised aggressors' success from 20% to 35% and did nothing for defenders. These are roles and procedures. (5) **The formal results contain no drive state at all**: Münster & Staal derive internal peace from payoff trade-offs among identical maximisers (the "threat" is chosen by the group), Hammond & Axelrod derive in-group favouritism from reproduction dynamics, Axelrod & Bennett take signs and sizes as given. (6) **The mechanism of the in-group rise is undecided** even in the best synthesis: insurance and protection, a change in social preference, trauma and growth, expressive preferences (Bauer et al.). (7) **A group quantity** (norm-enforcement intensity κ_g) rises under threat in Gneezy & Fessler and Benard's abstract; whether it is the sum of persons' propensities or a group-level state is not separable from what I read.

**Verdict.** Partly, and unevenly. "Drives as primitives" is supported for the in-group half as a fear-to-affiliation coupling plus an inclusion need, and for the out-group half only as anger at an *attributed* obstacle. The sign of the cross-boundary tie is fixed by three non-drive primitives: a functional relation, an attribution, and an authority gate; the boundary is an output of conflict, not an input to it. If the working hypothesis is that persons' and groups' drives are the primitives of the algebra, this tradition needs those three added, and it gives a reason to treat κ_g as a candidate group-level quantity without yet giving it a set point or dynamics.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

- **Predicted outcome.** (i) Membership and group scale: solidarity, cooperation and norm enforcement inside a group rise under a shared threat (S-A). (ii) Group scale: hostility toward the out-group rises when that group is the perceived source or obstacle (S-B). (iii) Group scale: intolerance of dissent, centralised leadership and, at the failure point, splits (Coser, Benard). (iv) Crowd scale: rallies in leader approval and institutional trust. (v) Formal: substitution of internal by external conflict (Münster & Staal), and bloc formation from signs and sizes (Axelrod & Bennett).
- **Out-of-sample evidence.** S-A: at least five independent designs agree in sign (a camp, laboratory contests, surveys and games in seven countries plus one 35-country survey, as Bauer et al. describe their evidence (the first draft's "about ten countries" corrected in validation), a battalion survey, a natural experiment in one city), none preregistered and the pooled analysis observational with the authors' own identification caveats. S-B: one field experiment (Sherif, 22 boys, covert, with a failed predecessor and a partial failure to replicate [abs]); elsewhere near zero in the pooled analysis I read, in one cross-group correlation quoted by Brewer and in two closed summaries [abs]. Axelrod & Bennett is the only quantitative test of a formal version I read: 15 of 17 nations, chance probability 0.0047 by the authors' count, with the coding fixed after the outcome and a published critique I did not read; their own all-negative ("everyone fears everyone") version failed, with 209 stable configurations (read in validation). Münster & Staal and Hammond & Axelrod have no data. Ross's 90-society sample [abs] has the opposite sign for the substitution claim.
- **Effect sizes.** +0.07 to +0.08 SD pooled cooperation; +0.24 to +0.25 SD toward in-group partners and +0.04 SD toward out-group partners (wars 1991-2010, villages); 93.6% and 92.5% in-group friendship choices, 53.0% and 76.9% unfavourable out-group ratings (1954 camp, no baseline); 97% and 96% fusion with own and other battalions, 1% with supporters (2011); aggressors win 22.5% of 3-against-3 contests, 35% with a first mover; a rally of 51% to 90% in presidential approval (2001, Gallup; read in validation); +0.154 trust points per unit of case count (2020, Netherlands); out-group words multiply shares by about 1.67 (the years before the 2020 US election; read in validation). Group size dependence: exact in the formal models (section 6), absent from the human data.
- **Control.** Every steering case read is a **manipulation**: Sherif's staff engineered the frustrations and the outcomes and hid their hand (and in 1953 lost control when the staff became the target); diversion is a leader's choice of an external contest; the rally follows elite framing. The only intervention that reversed a sign, a shared-goal stage, is in 107. Nothing here steers a tie sign with the knowledge and consent of the people it acts on, and whether the effects survive being known is untested.
- **Failures and gaps.** Defeat, intra-community war, and acquainted groups remove or reverse the in-group rise; the animal evidence is inconsistent; the positive internal-external association in 90 societies [abs]; no Twitter measurement of the out-group half; the "threat" variable is almost never measured independently of the response (exposure is observational, "perceived threat" correlates with attitudes in the same respondents); Stein's conditions are unread; no replication of the common-enemy claim was read (two old shared-threat tests exist unread, section 15); no crowd-scale test of tie signs after 2016.
- **For the algebra.** Admissible: S-A and S-B as separate conditional laws with era tags, a default of zero across a boundary, a leader-approval gate, and a perceived-source variable as an input. Not admissible: any magnitude without its tag, S-C and S-E as laws, or the claim that threat alone generates a negative cross-boundary tie.
