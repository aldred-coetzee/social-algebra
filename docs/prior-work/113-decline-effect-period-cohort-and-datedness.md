## Headline (read this first)

1. **"Decay" names six different things and they do not have the same size, cause or remedy** (section 1). The two that matter most for the ledger are (E) *estimate shrinkage*, which is large, measured, and independent of era, and (P) *real drift*, which is smaller per decade in the series read, differs by construct, and changes sign within a decade or two. Mixing them is the main way to go wrong with an old magnitude.
2. **Shrinkage is the better-measured correction, and it applies to the 1950s-1990s numbers and to 2008 numbers alike.** Replication of 100 studies from 2008 journal volumes: mean r .403 down to .197, ratio 0.49; significant 97% down to 36% (social 25%, cognitive 50%) [read, OSC 2015]. Many Labs 2: 28 findings, 125 samples, 36 countries, 15,305 people: median d .60 down to .15, ratio 0.25 [read]. Social-science experiments from Nature and Science 2010-2015: replication effect about half of the original, and for the true positives alone 71% [read, Camerer 2018 preprint]. In the intelligence literature declines outnumber increases 2:1 and run at about r = 0.10 per decade from the first study [read, Pietschnig 2019]; in a sample of 3,042 meta-analyses (main analysis on the 1,910 with a common log-odds metric) the "decline" is mostly a first-year effect, not a line, and small-study effects are the largest bias [read, Fanelli 2017]. The largest and newest programme, 274 claims from 164 papers published 2009-2018 in 54 journals, replicated 55.1% of claims, with median r .25 down to .10, ratio 0.40 [abs, Tyner et al. 2026; added at validation; abstract only, so it feeds no equation]. A significance filter alone, at the 50% power Pietschnig measured, inflates a true effect by x1.41, that is a factor 0.71, the same figure as Camerer's [derived, section 2.6].
3. **Schooler 2011 is an opinion column with no data.** It lists eight or more candidate causes and asserts that the decline is "typically linear"; Fanelli's larger sample qualifies that. The one prospective test that was meant to show that rigour prevents declines, Protzko, ..., Schooler (2023), was **retracted on 24 September 2024** for misstated preregistration, outcomes chosen with knowledge of the data, and incomplete reporting [read, the retraction note]. So there is no accepted demonstration that shrinkage can be designed away, and its numbers must not be used. Equally, Many Labs 2 found very little heterogeneity by lab versus online or by WEIRD versus less WEIRD sample, which weakens "hidden moderators" as the explanation, but it varies space and setting at one time, not historical time.
4. **Trend claims are themselves unstable, which is the same lesson one level up.** Loneliness in emerging adults: +0.56 SD over 1976-2019, flat before 2000, rising after 2000, flat again after 2012 [read, Buecker 2021]; against a decline of d = -0.26 for US college students 1978-2009 [abs, Clark 2015]; against "no empirical support" for an increase in a 2019 explainer that does not cite Buecker [read, Ortiz-Ospina]. Narcissism: +0.33 SD [abs, 2008], no increase once new data are added [abs, 2010], -0.27 d after item non-equivalence is modelled [abs, 2017], then rise to 2008 and fall after, by the original authors [read, 2021]. A 2.94-to-2.08 fall in confidants is attributed by one analysis to interviewer effects [abs, Paik & Sanchagrin 2013]. In the Integrated Values Surveys file, generalized trust has no general decline across the 40 countries with at least five waves (21 up, 19 down), and across 64 countries with at least four waves 84% of the variance in levels is between countries [computed, section 4.2].
5. **"Generation" is not an identified variable here.** Age = period - cohort, so no linear effect of all three can be separated without strong outside assumptions [read, Hawkley 2019; Bell & Jones 2014]. Where age-period-cohort splits were attempted in what I read, period or age carried the effect (trust: "primarily a time-period effect" [abs]; older-adult loneliness: no cohort trend, OR 1.04 [0.94, 1.14] per decade of birth year in HRS [read]; narcissism: a reversal that tracks the economy, which looks like a period effect but is not identified [read]). The belief that kids are declining is a memory and self-comparison bias: false feedback about one's own reading lowered it through recalled childhood enjoyment, indirect beta = -0.009, small [read, Protzko & Schooler 2019].
6. **Re-runs of classic lab paradigms do not show a stable base rate: it moves with population and era, and specific levers fare worse.** (Corrected at validation, 2026-11-01; this item first said the re-runs "mostly reproduce the base rate".) Asch with five confederates, Bern, March-April 2021: 33% wrong answers among the 99 unincentivised participants (202 valid in all) against 36.8% in Asch's 1950s data as quoted by the authors [read]. But British students in a procedural replication yielded on 1 of 396 critical trials, while youths on probation facing probation-officer confederates conformed at Asch's level [abs, Perrin & Spencer 1981]; UK and US students erred less than Asch's subjects but more than zero [abs, Nicholson, Cole & Rocklin 1985]; and US studies declined after the 1950s [abs, Bond & Smith 1996]. Milgram-type obedience to 150 V in 2006: "only slightly lower"; in 2015: "similarly high" [abs]; Twenge reads the 2006 data as disobedience among men having "nearly doubled" since the 1960s [abs, Twenge 2009]. Burger's refusing-model condition did not lower obedience [abs]. The bystander effect depends on the outcome: lab individual helping g = -0.35 over 105 effect sizes [abs], but in 219 CCTV conflicts at least one person helped in 90.9% and each extra bystander raised the odds of help by x1.10 [read]. Three paradigms: Asch and Milgram are deception labs, the bystander laboratory work uses staged emergencies, and only the CCTV study is observational.
7. **Acceleration is documented for one thing: how fast collective attention turns over.** Top-50 Twitter hashtags stayed in the top 50 for 17.5 h in 2013 and 11.9 h in 2016; gradients steepen and peak heights stay stable in books, movies, Google queries and Reddit; citations and Wikipedia show negligible change; a one-parameter Lotka-Volterra model with equal production and consumption rates reproduces the distributions [read, Lorenz-Spreen 2019]. Time constants at crowd scale are therefore era-bound; nothing read says the same of coupling weights.
8. **No source here re-estimates a coupling weight, a threshold or gate, an inertia, or a tie sign across eras.** For those v0 parameters the literature gives only the shrinkage prior, not a drift rate; "unrefreshed" should be a flag, not a discount (section 9). Levels (set points, base rates) show drift in the series read; responses (conformity under unanimity, obedience under prods) are not shown to be steadier: the Asch rate has run from near zero to Asch's level across re-runs, with population confounded with era, and the Milgram re-runs are read both ways (section 5.4; corrected at validation, where this sentence first said responses "show little" drift).
9. **Proposed soft settings, for Aldred to decide (nothing adopted here):** a default x0.5 shrink with slack 0.25-0.75 for single-study social-psychology effects; an `unrefreshed` flag; an age-period-cohort status on every trend line; instrument-equivalence and composition flags; a time-indexed crowd-scale time constant; no cohort variable unless a source identifies it (section 9).

## 1. Six things "decay" can mean

A short taxonomy is needed because the sources use one word for all of them. The right-hand column is the question the strand cares about.

| Kind | What moves | Where it shows in what I read | Does it touch a 1950s-1990s magnitude? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **E** estimate shrinkage | the number we hold, not the world: selection on significance, low power, regression to the mean | OSC; Many Labs 2; Camerer; Pietschnig; Fanelli; Jennions & Møller | Yes, in every era; worst for small single studies. It is independent of how old the study is. |
| **P** period drift | the true parameter, for everyone at once | Buecker (loneliness after 2000); Twenge 2021 (narcissism, economy); Lorenz-Spreen (attention) | Only for parameters that are levels or time constants, and only where re-measured. |
| **C** cohort replacement | who is in the population: each birth cohort carries its own value and the mix shifts | Putnam's "generational change" [sf]; Twenge's "Generation Me" claims; the critiques | Only if identified. Mostly unidentified (section 3.1). |
| **M** yardstick or sample drift | the instrument, the interviewers, the campus mix | Wetzel (items not equivalent across cohorts) [abs]; Paik & Sanchagrin [abs]; Twenge 2008 (California mix) [abs]; Hawkley (response order) [read] | Yes: an old yardstick may not be the one in use now. |
| **O** observer illusion | the perceiver's belief, not the measured thing | Protzko & Schooler 2019; Pew's 71% who say trust is shrinking [read] | No effect on a magnitude; it contaminates claims about trends. |
| **R** reflexive change | the finding, once known, changes the behaviour it describes | Gergen 1973 [abs]; Schooler 2011 ("participants no longer being naive") [read]; 093 | Yes for widely known findings; no test in what I read. |

**Bookkeeping, not a result.** [derived] For a ledger magnitude reported by study j, write m_j = theta(tau_j, mu_j, pi_j) x (1 + b_sel,j) + b_meas,j + e_j, where tau is the era, mu the medium, pi the population and its composition, b_sel the selection inflation (E), b_meas the yardstick offset (M), and theta the quantity the algebra wants. P and C are the dependence of theta on tau and on the birth-cohort mix inside pi; O does not appear in m_j at all but in what people say about it; R appears as a dependence of theta on how widely the finding is known. The literature below supports estimating b_sel (section 2.6) and a few slopes of theta in tau for chosen constructs (sections 4, 6); it supports no slope of theta in mu except in the specific cases of section 5.

## 2. The decline effect: shrinkage of the estimate (kind E)

### 2.1 Schooler (2011) [read in full]

**Vocabulary.** The decline effect (effects "seem to diminish with time"), regression to the mean, publication bias, "negative results" that never reach the record, participants "no longer being naive". **Drives/operands.** None. Operands are published effects; the motive-like content is researcher behaviour (selective reporting, enthusiasm). **Equations.** None. **Content.** Dates the effect to 1930s parapsychology; says it has since been reported in individual labs (his own included) and in meta-analyses in biology and medicine; credits a December 2010 *New Yorker* article for the wider attention. Candidate causes, none tested: regression to the mean (which "would not account for the typically linear nature of the decline"); publication bias; unreported aspects of method; exclusive reporting of confirming findings; changing researcher enthusiasm; more rigorous later methods; experimenter-bias measurement error; the difficulty of publishing replication failures; and, in the social sciences, participants becoming familiar with the effect. He floats, as "less likely, but not inconceivable", an observer effect on science itself. **Proposal.** An open repository logging hypotheses and methods before, and results after, every experiment. **Standing.** No data, no effect size, no era, no medium; the linearity is asserted without a source. It is an agenda. Era n/a, medium n/a, scale n/a, no intervention. (Validation, 2026-11-01: Nature lists the piece as a "World View" column, published 23 February 2011, which confirms its standing; the wording quoted here could not be re-read, see section 14.)

### 2.2 Protzko and Schooler on the decline effect

The 2017 chapter is closed (section 0). Its typology reaches me through Pietschnig et al.: an *inflated* decline effect (a real but smaller effect exists) against a *false-positive* decline effect (the first effect was an artefact) [read, in Pietschnig]. The prospective test is Protzko et al. 2023: four coordinated labs, 16 new findings, preregistration and large samples; the abstract claims 86% of replication attempts significant and replication effects at 97% of the original when one lab replicated another's discovery [read, abstract]. **It was retracted on 24 September 2024** by the Editors after concerns raised by Bak-Coleman and Devezer: misstated hypotheses and predictions, no preregistration for the measures and analyses behind the titular claim (against statements asserting it), outcome measures and analyses chosen with knowledge of the data, and incomplete reporting; the authors agreed to the retraction on the preregistration statement and disagreed with the other concerns; the note adds that the authors "have been invited to submit a new manuscript for peer review" [read, note]. Whether a new version has since appeared was not checked at validation (no search was available). Schooler is a co-author. The claim is therefore unusable, and "rigour-enhancing practices remove the decline" has no accepted test in this file.

### 2.3 Open Science Collaboration (2015) [read, main text]

**Design.** Replications (of the last experiment in the article by default, 84 of 100) of sampled articles from the 2008 volumes of *Psychological Science*, *JPSP* and *JEP:LMC* (488 articles; 158 eligible during the project window November 2011 to December 2014; 111 claimed; 100 completed by the deadline), with original materials where available, protocols reviewed by original authors, pre-registered, one key effect per study, effects converted to r. **Results.** 97 of 100 originals counted as positive (four with p between .0508 and .0567 counted so); average replication power for the 97 was .92, implying about 89 successes if all were true and accurately estimated; 35 occurred (36.1%, 95% CI 26.6-46.2). Mean p fell from .028 to .302. Mean effect size r: .403 (SD .188) original, .197 (SD .257) replication; 82 of 99 pairs larger in the original; rank correlation between pairs .51. The original effect lay inside the replication's 95% CI for 47.4%. A fixed-effect combination of original and replication left 68% (51 of 75) with CIs excluding zero. Subjective team verdicts: 39 of 100. **By field:** social-personality 14 of 55 (25%) at p < .05, cognitive 21 of 42 (50%); the shrinkage of effect size was similar in all journals (chi-square(3) = 2.45, p = .48). **By design:** tests of main or simple effects replicated in 23 of 49 (47%), interactions in 8 of 37 (22%). **By strength of evidence:** original p < .02, 26 of 63 (41%); p between .02 and .04, 6 of 23 (26%); p above .04, 2 of 11 (18%); p < .001, 20 of 32 (63%). Replication power predicted success at p < .05 (r = .368) but not the size gap (r = -.053); a larger original effect predicted both success (r = .304) and a larger gap (r = .279); surprising effects and harder-to-run replications were less reproducible; team expertise, effect importance and self-rated quality did not account for meaningful variation. **What the authors say.** Low power plus publication bias "anticipates that replication effect sizes would be smaller... on a routine basis"; unanticipated factors in sample, setting or procedure could still matter; and, of the effects tested, "how many... have we established are true? Zero" (and false, zero). **Era, medium, scale.** Originals from 2008 volumes; replications 2011-2014; mixed lab and online, not coded in the text read; the units are studies, the underlying effects individual-level or small-group laboratory effects; no intervention. **What it cannot do for this strand:** it compares studies four to six years apart, so it cannot separate shrinkage from drift; it gave no moderator on age of the original (all are 2008); and it measures the 2008 literature, not the 1950s-1990s one.

### 2.4 Many Labs 2 and Camerer et al. [read in part]

**Many Labs 2.** 28 preregistered, peer-reviewed-in-advance replications of classic and contemporary findings; invitation to sites issued in early 2014; 125 samples (64 + 61, 15 sites ran both slates), 15,305 participants (mean 122, range 16-841), 79 in-person and 46 web samples, 36 countries and territories. 15 (54%) significant in the original direction at p < .05, 14 (50%) at p < .0001; 7 (25%) had effects larger than the original, 21 (75%) smaller; median d **0.60 original against 0.15 replication** in WEIRD samples; 16 (57%) replications with d below .20, 9 (32%) in the opposite direction. Heterogeneity: 11 (39%) significant by Q, mostly among the largest effects; one effect with tau above .20; "very little" attributable to task order, lab versus online, or WEIRD versus less WEIRD. The authors' reading: variation was "more attributable to the effect being studied than the sample or setting", and failures should not be dismissed by appeal to untested hidden moderators. **Camerer et al.** 21 social-science experiments published in *Nature* and *Science* 2010-2015, preregistered, sample sizes on average about five times the originals: 13 (62%) significant in the same direction; mean replication effect about 50% of the original (in the text: mean relative effect 46.2%, being 74.5% for the 13 that replicated and 0.3% for the 8 that did not; mean r .249 against .460 [read at validation]); estimated true-positive rate 67% and, for the true positives, a relative effect size of 71%, "suggesting that both false positives and inflated effect sizes of true positives contribute". Prediction markets (63.4%) and surveys (60.6%) of peers anticipated the observed 61.9%. **Era, medium, scale.** ML2 replications in 2014 (the text speaks of "2014 (i.e., when the replication study was conducted)"), lab and web; note that ML2 updated some stimuli to be "more appropriate for 2014", so a re-run of an old paradigm may already carry a changed yardstick (kind M) [read at validation]; Camerer's originals 2010-2015 (replication years not extracted); individual-level experiments; no intervention. **Caveat:** Many Labs 2 varies place and setting at one time; neither programme varies the calendar beyond the gap between original and replication.

### 2.5 Fanelli, Pietschnig, Jennions & Møller

**Fanelli et al. (2017)** [read]: second-order meta-regression over 3,042 usable meta-analyses from all disciplines (the main analysis used the 1,910 convertible to a common log-odds-ratio metric, n = 33,355 non-duplicated primary data points; the other 1,132 entered only robustness tests; corrected at validation, where this read "33,355 non-duplicated studies"); each bias is a regression coefficient (small-study effect: effect size on study standard error, predicted positive; decline effect: on year order within the meta-analysis, predicted negative; early-extreme, US effect, citation bias and others). Findings: small-study effects were "by far the largest", with gray-literature and citation bias next; the **decline effect, a linear association with year, was not significant in the main analysis and highly significant in all robustness tests**, and multilevel analyses suggest most or all of it is a **"first-year" effect**, "not linear", in which only the earliest studies overestimate; "early-extreme" is explained by the lower precision of early studies; the pressure-to-publish hypothesis was overall *not* supported (authors in high-incentive countries or publishing more were not more likely to overestimate); the social sciences showed biases of equal or larger size than the biological and physical sciences, and a possible worsening of small-study and decline effects over time that was small and not consistent across robustness analyses. Sizes: most biases accounted for 1.2% or less of the variance in reported effect sizes, small-study effects up to 27%; the authors add that small-study effects can also come from legitimate design choices, and that an apparent worsening over time may reflect changes in meta-analytic method rather than in research practice. **Pietschnig et al. (2019)** [read]: 22 meta-analyses in the journal *Intelligence*, 36 meta-analytic and 1,391 primary effect sizes, N above 697,000. Crude declines outnumber increases about 2:1 (20 against 10, plus 6 sign-reversing "Proteus" cases); mean absolute deviation of the first study from the summary effect r = .17 overall (.18 for declines, .08 for increases, .26 for Proteus); annual change about r = 0.01, "0.10 per decade" relative to the first study; average primary-study power 50.24% (median 48.18%, k = 29); among declines the first study's effect size explains most of its own misestimation (eta-squared .69) and its sample size is weakly negatively related to it (eta-squared .05). **Jennions & Møller (2002)** [abs]: 44 meta-analyses in ecology and evolution, a small but significant decline in effect size with publication year that survives control for sample size; publication bias offered as the most parsimonious cause. **Era, medium, scale.** Meta-research over decades of literature (years not extracted); psychometric, neuroimaging and field data; no social-scale outcome; no intervention.

**Where Schooler's linearity stands:** Schooler asserts "typically linear" [read]; Fanelli's larger sample says mostly a first-year drop [read]. That is a contradiction between two read sources (section 11, C1).

Ratios of replication to original effect size, from the numbers above:

A consistency check: 0.67 (true-positive rate) x 0.71 (relative size of true positives) = 0.48, close to Camerer's "about 50%" [derived]. A mechanical check: if a true effect is estimated with standard error s, only estimates beyond 1.96 s are published, and nothing else is going on (one fixed effect, no p-hacking, no heterogeneity), the expected published estimate overstates the truth as follows [derived; standard truncated-normal arithmetic, computed here, two-sided p < .05, wrong-sign tail included]:

| Power of the original design | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.5 | 0.8 | 0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expected published / true | 3.40 | 2.24 | 1.80 | 1.41 | 1.12 | 1.06 |

At 50% power, the average Pietschnig measured, the factor is 0.71, which is Camerer's figure for true positives. The remaining halving in OSC and ML2 then needs false positives, flexible analysis, or heterogeneity on top. This is arithmetic, not evidence about any one study.

### 2.7 Is the gap context? Two reads that point different ways

Van Bavel et al. (2016) [sf + abs]: three coders, blind to outcomes, rated 100 OSC studies on a five-point scale for how likely context ("time, e.g. pre- versus post-recession", culture, location, population) was to affect the result; the rating correlated negatively with replication success, r(98) = -.23, P = .024, and stayed predictive after adjusting for original effect size, power and surprisingness (odds ratio about 0.45 per unit); social-psychology studies were rated higher than cognitive (3.58 against 2.00); the paper does not analyse the age of the original or historical time, and says so ("Future work should test these (and other) factors separately"). The full abstract, read at validation, adds that the association "did not differ across psychological subdisciplines" [abs]; the figures above remain [sf]. Many Labs 2 (above) found little heterogeneity across 36 countries and between lab and web. The two are not in conflict if "context" acts through something other than place and procedure (the calendar, or correlated features such as how surprising or fragile an effect is); the one dimension neither tests is the calendar. **For the strand:** the hidden-moderator explanation of the OSC gap has a small judged correlate (r about -.23) and no measured one; the calendar is untested.

**Gergen (1973)** [abs] is the early statement of the opposite worry: theories of social behaviour "are primarily reflections of contemporary history"; disseminating psychological knowledge changes the behaviour it describes (through a prescriptive bias in theorising, the liberating effects of knowledge, and resistance on values of freedom); theoretical premises rest on "acquired dispositions" that change as the culture changes, "and the premises are often invalidated". Era 1973, no data, no equation; it is the source of kind R and, with 093, of the reflexivity caveat.

**Hagger et al. (2016)** [abs], a case of E on a *mechanism*: a resource-depletion account of self-control (a fill-and-discharge law) that an earlier meta-analysis had put at a medium effect gave d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15] in 23 laboratories, N = 2,141, one standardised protocol, preregistered. Era 2010s, computer tasks in labs, individual scale, a manipulation of fatigue by task; one paradigm only, so it refutes that paradigm, not fill-and-discharge as a form.

## 3. Period, cohort and age: identification, and the generational-shift debate (kinds P, C, O)

### 3.1 The identity and what it forbids [read]

Hawkley et al. (2019): "Due to the linear dependency among age, period and cohort (i.e., age = period - cohort), it is not possible to include linear terms for age, survey year and birth year in the same model" (their citation: Holford 2006). They fit two models, each dropping one linear term, "under the assumption that the linear trend for the third is negligible". Bell & Jones (2014): the three terms are "exactly mathematically dependent"; many in social science (they cite Glenn 1976, 2005, and Goldstein 1979) see separating them as a "futile quest" (Glenn 1976) (corrected at validation, where this read "the field's own senior view"); "there is no technical solution to the identification problem without the imposition of strong (and correct) a priori assumptions"; the hierarchical APC model works only if no period or cohort trend exists, or the cohort trend is controlled in the fixed part and no period trend is assumed, or cohort groupings match the unknown truth exactly, and otherwise "can arbitrarily reapportion APC effects". Buecker et al. (2021) and Twenge et al. (2021) both state that their design cannot tell cohort from period. **Consequence.** A trend in a single age band over calendar time (every cross-temporal meta-analysis of students; every high-school-senior series) is a mixture of period and cohort by construction. **Operand/operator translation:** age, period and cohort are three coordinates with one linear constraint; a ledger line asserting "generational" change needs at least two cohorts observed in at least two periods and a stated assumption.

### 3.2 The narcissism case: eight results, one construct

| Source | Window | Sample | Result | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Twenge et al. 2008 | 1979-2006 | 85 US college samples, n = 16,475, NPI | rise, beta .53 (sample-size weighted); +0.33 SD since 1982 | [abs] |
| Trzesniewski, Donnellan & Robins 2008 | | | convenience samples cannot support generational claims; no replication in a nationally representative sample; NPI interpretation | [abs] |
| Twenge et al. 2008 (reply) | 24 years | 27 campuses outside California | d = +0.41; no change among California students, attributed to a large shift in ethnic composition | [abs] |
| Trzesniewski & Donnellan 2010 | 1976-2006 | US high-school seniors, N = 477,380 | little evidence of meaningful change in egotism, self-enhancement, individualism, self-esteem, loneliness and ten more listed constructs; more cynical and less trusting; less fearful of social problems | [abs] |

Era US, medium questionnaire, scale crowd (a population mean, not a behaviour), no intervention in any row. **Reading.** The size of the early increase moved with the sample (0.33, 0.41, 0.24); a composition shift can erase or create a trend (California); an item-level equivalence test changed the sign; and the proponents' own update shows a reversal tied to the economy, which makes it look like a period effect, though the 2021 authors write that their time-lag design "cannot determine" cohort versus period [read]. The label "epidemic" did not survive its own data. For a ledger, a single "narcissism trend" line is not a usable magnitude.

### 3.3 Other generational claims

Empathy: a decline in Empathic Concern and Perspective Taking among US college students, 72 samples 1979-2009, N = 13,737, "most pronounced" after 2000 [abs, Konrath et al. 2011]. Work attitudes: a meta-analysis of 20 articles and 19,961 workers found generational differences small to near zero, plausibly age, tenure and shared circumstance [sa, Costanza et al. 2012, by search summaries]; the later review argues that the evidence for the existence of generations is "at best, scant" and that the idea persists as a social construction [abs, Rudolph et al. 2021]. That bears on the group definition itself: a birth-year bin is a modelling choice with no natural boundary (my reading; Hawkley et al. had to choose 1920-1947 versus 1948-1965). Putnam's civic-decline account assigns about half of the post-1965 decline in civic engagement to generational change, a quarter to electronic entertainment (television), a tenth each to work pressure and to suburbanisation, 15-20% unexplained [sf, Wikipedia on the 2000 book, CC BY-SA 4.0]; I could not read Putnam, so this is a pointer.

### 3.4 "Kids these days" [read, Protzko & Schooler 2019]

**Vocabulary.** The "kids these days effect": disparaging the present youth, stronger for people who excel on the trait. **Design and results.** Study 1: 1,824 US adults from an online panel (CriticalMix) matched to US demographics; believing children respect elders less than they used to: intercept b = 0.503 (SE 0.112), and higher authoritarianism predicted it (beta .265); older participants also believed it (beta .161). Study 2: 134 MTurk workers; no average belief of declining intelligence (P > .81), but more intelligent people believed it more (beta .173, P = .036), and this despite rising population intelligence. Study 3: 1,500 adults; belief that kids enjoy reading less (b = 1.189); the more well-read, the stronger (beta .227). Study 4: a replication with mediators; biased memory of one's own childhood mediated the effect, and the biased-sampling account (judging from a similar peer set) was not supported. Study 5: 1,500 adults, false feedback (told top 15% or bottom 15% on an author-recognition test); the manipulation changed subjective standing and recalled childhood reading, and the indirect effect on the kids-these-days belief was beta = -0.009, 95% CI [-0.02, -0.002], P = .04; no direct effect. The authors call the causal effect "small" and the mechanism partial. Participants did *not* believe that adults had come to enjoy reading less (study 4: intercept 4.115 where 4 means "the same amount"), though the well-read did; the test is for reading only (narrowed at validation from "adults generally had worsened"). The complaint is traced in text from 1624 and "since at least 624 BCE". **Era** before October 2018; **medium** online panels; **scale** opinion (a perception, crowd-level); **consent/manipulation** study 5 deceives about performance (a manipulation, in a lab-like online task). **Consequence.** Older-to-younger judgments of cohort decline are unreliable measurements of cohort change; the bias runs with the judge's own standing.

### 3.5 The digital-media attribution

Orben & Przybylski (2019) [abs]: a specification-curve analysis of three large datasets (n = 355,358) finds a negative but small association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being, "at most 0.4%" of its variation, "too small to warrant policy change". Twenge et al. (2018) [cited, not read] is the opposing report of rising adolescent depressive symptoms, suicide-related outcomes and suicide rates after 2010, linked to screen time. A time-series check on the smartphone story is in Buecker et al.: after 2012, the date the smartphone is said to have reached market saturation, emerging-adult loneliness was flat (beta = -0.061, 95% CI [-1.160, 1.030], k = 178 means) [read]. D606 owns the medium question.

## 4. Series: loneliness, trust, confidants

### 4.1 Loneliness

**Buecker et al. (2021)** [read]. *Vocabulary:* cross-temporal meta-analysis (CTMA); emerging adults (18-29); UCLA Loneliness Scale; percent of maximum possible (POMP) scores. *Drives/operands:* loneliness is defined as an aversive state from a perceived mismatch between desired and actual relationships (Perlman & Peplau, as cited), that is a deficit signal; the operand is the year-mean of that signal. *Equations:* POMP = (score - min) / (max - min) x 100 [read]; predicted score y = beta x year + c, change over 1976-2019 divided by the average reported SD gives the effect size [read]. *Data:* 449 means from 345 studies (437 independent samples), 124,855 emerging adults, UCLA scale, collected 1976-2019 (year of publication minus two when not reported); about 90% student samples; mean age 20.8; 60.6% female; k = 192 North American, 93 Asian, 64 European, 16 Oceanian, 4 African (as printed). *Results:* beta = 0.224 POMP points per calendar year, 95% CI [0.138, 0.309], I-squared 99.29%; equals **0.56 SD** over 43 years, about 0.14 SD per decade; before 2000 beta = -0.104 [-0.348, 0.139] (81 means, not significant), after 2000 beta = 0.430 [0.172, 0.687] (365 means), after 2012 beta = -0.061 [-1.160, 1.030] (178 means). The slope stays after adjustment for sample type, sex ratio, age, scale length; it is significant for American samples (0.301 [0.179, 0.422]) and positive but not significant for European (0.133 [-0.179, 0.445]) and Asian (0.233 [-0.014, 0.480]) samples, with no significant difference between continents; the time x percent-female interaction is +0.002 per point [<0.001, 0.004]; none of population growth, urbanisation or Gini moderated it. The authors write that "loneliness epidemic" "seems exaggerated", cannot separate cohort from period, and cannot say what drives it; unpublished studies and dissertations did not differ from journal articles. *Era 1976-2019; medium self-report scales (mode not coded); scale crowd (a population state); no intervention.*

**Clark, Loxton & Tobin (2015)** [abs]: 48 samples of American college students on the Revised UCLA scale (N = 13,041), loneliness *declined* 1978-2009, d = -0.26; Monitoring the Future high-school students (N = 385,153) declined 1991-2012; subjective isolation fell (d = -0.20) while social-network isolation rose (d = +0.06). **Hawkley et al. (2019)** [read]: National Social Life, Health and Aging Project (waves 2005-06, 2010-11, 2015-16) and the Health and Retirement Study (to 2016), preregistered; mean loneliness of US community-dwelling adults aged 57-85 went from 4.0 to 4.1 (95% CI of the change -0.002 to 0.2), the share above the cut from 0.28 to 0.31 (0.002 to 0.06); loneliness falls with age to the early 70s and rises after; the odds ratio for a ten-year-later birth year is 1.15 [0.92, 1.42] (NSHAP) and 1.04 [0.94, 1.14] (HRS), which the authors say lets them "rule out even small linear increases" with birth year in HRS; HRS prompts "often" first and NSHAP last, which shifts levels, a response-order effect. **Ortiz-Ospina (2019)** [read in full] argues from Hawkley, Trzesniewski & Donnellan, Clark et al. and a Swedish oldest-old series that "there is surprisingly no empirical support" for rising loneliness in rich countries, notes that young adults report *more* loneliness than the old in England, New Zealand, Japan and the US (which a cross-section cannot turn into a trend), and warns against generalising beyond rich countries. The snapshot does not cite Buecker et al.

**What the four add up to.** Not "no change" and not "an epidemic": among emerging adults, student-heavy UCLA-scale samples rise after 2000 (Buecker), flatten after 2012, and an earlier, smaller meta-analysis of the same scale showed a fall to 2009 (Clark); among older adults in 2005-2016 there is no trend and no cohort effect (Hawkley). Different windows, ages, scale versions and sample mixes; unexplained by any source. For the algebra the series is useful because its construct is a *deficit signal* (056), so its drift can be read as drift in either the desired level, the supplied level, or the mapping, and no source separates them.

### 4.2 Trust

**Putnam (1995, 2000)** [not read; sf]. The thesis (declining civic engagement and social capital in the US since the 1960s, league bowling falling while total bowling rose, generational replacement as the largest single cause) is named in D605 as a target. **Twenge, Campbell & Carter (2014)** [abs]: General Social Survey adults 1972-2012 (N = 37,493) and Monitoring the Future 12th graders 1976-2012 (N = 101,633); trust in others and confidence in institutions fell to all-time or near-all-time lows by 2012; hierarchical models separating period, generation and age show "this decline in social capital is primarily a time-period effect" (institutional confidence also has a generation effect, Baby Boomers lowest); trust was lowest when income inequality was high, confidence lowest when poverty was high. A search summary of the paper says it states that most researchers had favoured cohort explanations, citing Robinson & Jackson 2001 [sa]. **Pew (2019)** [read at validation; first sf]: 71% of 10,618 US adults (fielded 27 November to 10 December 2018) say interpersonal trust has shrunk over twenty years, and the page notes that comparable personal-trust items have been asked "less frequently or consistently".

**What an open multi-country file says** [computed from the OWID/IVS CSV, item "most people can be trusted" against "need to be very careful", wave-labelled years 1984, 1993, 1998, 2004, 2010, 2014, 2022; the OWID years are wave labels, country coverage differs by wave, modes differ; not a time series in the strict sense. Three points added at validation from the OWID metadata and the file: the share counts "don't know" and "no answer" as valid answers in the denominator, so it is not a two-way split against "need to be very careful"; the 2022 label covers surveys from 2017 to 2022; and one of the 118 entities, Northern Ireland, is sub-national. Every number in this paragraph was recomputed at validation and reproduces]. United States: 39.2, 50.0, 35.0, 35.5, 39.1, 34.8, 37.0 (per cent); a fall of 15 points between the 1993 and 1998 waves, then flat; the least-squares slope over all waves is -1.8 points per decade and from 1998 on +0.7. ### 4.3 Confidants: a series that was an artefact, or was not

McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Brashears (2006) [abs]: the General Social Survey's "important matters" network item in 1985 and 2004 gave mean network size 2.94 down to 2.08, the share naming nobody almost tripled, and the modal respondent went from three confidants to none; they note the data "may overestimate the number of social isolates". An erratum followed in 2008 [abs/sa: a 41-response coding error found by the survey organisation, per a search summary]. Fischer (2009) [abs]: the 2004 result contradicts other data and contains anomalies (no names given rose from about 10% to about 25%), so scholars "should draw no inference". McPherson et al. (2009) [abs] reply that any plausible modelling shows a downward trend. Paik & Sanchagrin (2013) [abs]: the 2004 and 2010 GSS network sizes carry significant **interviewer effects**, negatively correlated with mean network size, and some interviewers obtained "highly improbable" isolation; the shrinking-networks claim is an artefact. Era 1985 and 2004 (2010), face-to-face survey, scale membership (ties), no intervention. This is the cleanest case in the file of kind M (yardstick) being mistaken for kind P.

## 5. Re-runs of group-scale paradigms across eras

### 5.1 Asch

Franzen & Mader (2023) [read]: University of Bern campus recruits, 210 (202 valid; 61% female; mean age 22.6), **16 March to 30 April 2021**; five confederates and one naive participant (the participant answering fifth); ten line tasks, six critical. Average wrong answers on critical trials 33% among the 99 unincentivised participants (103 incentivised) against 36.8% in Asch's data, quoted by the authors (two-sample test t(16) = 0.59, p = .57); with correct answers rewarded, 8 points lower (1.97 down to 1.47 wrong answers of six, t(208) = 2.24, p = .03); on five political yes/no items the group lifted agreement by 38 points on average, all five differences significant. Their literature review: Asch reported 36.8% (123 participants); Bond & Smith's meta-analysis, **44 strict replications, an average error rate of 25%**, "the vast majority" with male US university students; Japanese male students with 6-9 confederates 25% (n = 40); Bosnian students with five confederates 35% (n = 95). Bond & Smith (1996) [abs]: 133 studies from 17 countries; "an analysis of US studies found that conformity has declined since the 1950s"; collectivist countries higher. **Reading.** Bond & Smith's replication average (25%, mostly US men) sits below Asch's 36.8%, in line with their finding of a US decline, while the Swiss 2021 rate is back near Asch's figure; the population changed (country, sex mix, no male-only restriction), so this is not a recovery. **Intermediate re-runs, added at validation** [abs]: Larsen (1974) is cited by Bond & Smith for the view that the 1950s levels partly reflected the McCarthy era; Perrin & Spencer (1981) found compliance on only 1 of 396 critical trials among British students in a procedural replication, but Asch-like levels among youths on probation whose confederates and experimenter were probation officers, and among alienated black youths with a white experimenter; Nicholson, Cole & Rocklin (1985) found British and US students alike, with errors significantly fewer than Asch reported but significantly above zero, and conclude the effect "has weakened over the past 30 years". So the Asch rate has run from near zero to Asch's level, and in every case the population moved with the date. Era 1950s to 2021, face-to-face laboratory, scale membership (conform) in a 6-person group; **manipulation by design** (confederates), an incentive lever that is disclosed (consent-compatible).

### 5.2 Milgram

Burger (2009) [abs]: 70 adults, a partial replication of Milgram's Experiment 5 stopping at 150 V (79% of Milgram's participants beyond that point had continued to the end); obedience in the 2006 replication "only slightly lower" than 45 years earlier; participants who saw a confederate refuse obeyed as often as those who saw no model; no sex difference; some effect of empathic concern and desire for control. Doliński et al. (2017) [abs]: the same procedure in 2015 with sex of participant and of learner varied; obedience "similarly high" to the original, sex and personality effects inconclusive. File 13 records Milgram's own peer-refusal condition as the largest effect in that cluster [sa]. **Added at validation** [abs]: Twenge (2009), commenting on Burger's data, writes that disobedience "has nearly doubled among male participants since the 1960s", that the ethnic composition of Burger's sample may have suppressed a larger change, and that stopping at 150 V may also have; Blass (1999) reviews 35 years of the paradigm and poses "Have obedience rates changed over time?", but the abstract does not give his answer and the text is closed. **Reading.** The overall rate to 150 V is similar across 45-55 years in two re-runs, but the same 2006 data are read as a near-doubling of male disobedience, so "stable" is one reading, not a finding; one specific lever did not reproduce in a modified design. Era 1961-62, 2006, 2015; face-to-face laboratory; scale membership (comply); manipulation (a staged learner; Burger's abstract says participants' well-being was protected by stopping at 150 V; the papers' debriefing procedures were not read).

### 5.3 The bystander effect: the answer depends on the outcome

Fischer et al. (2011) [abs]: a meta-analysis of literature from the 1960s to 2010, 105 independent effect sizes, over 7,700 participants, overall g = -0.35 in a fixed-effects model (more bystanders, less individual helping); attenuated when the situation was dangerous, the perpetrator present, or the cost of helping physical; weaker when bystanders were exclusively male, were naive rather than passive confederates or only virtually present, or were not strangers. Philpot et al. (2020) [read]: 219 public-space conflicts on actively monitored CCTV (Amsterdam n = 63, Cape Town n = 61, Lancaster n = 95), inclusive sampling of "the mildest animated disagreements" to violence, police absent at the start: at least one bystander intervened in **90.9%**, mean 3.76 interveners per video (SD 3.01); no difference by country (odds ratios 0.61 and 1.59, Bayes factor 17 in favour of no difference); the number of bystanders was positively related to the chance that *someone* helps, **OR = 1.10 per additional bystander** [1.03, 1.18] (about x2.6 for ten more, 1.34-5.23 [derived]). The two do not contradict: the lab measures one person's helping, the camera measures whether the group yields a helper. Era lab 1960s-2010 and CCTV (years not stated); medium laboratory and staged emergencies against in-situ observation; scale **membership** (the individual) against **group** (the aggregate); lab staged emergencies are manipulation, CCTV observation has no intervention.

### 5.4 What the re-runs say about levels and responses

Three paradigms, so only a pattern worth testing: **response rates under a fixed situation** (Asch unanimity at n = 5, obedience to 150 V) are not shown to be stable: the Asch rate has run from near zero (British students, Perrin & Spencer 1981) through "weakened" (1985) to Asch's level (Bern 2021), with population confounded with date each time, and the Milgram re-runs agree on the overall 150 V rate but are read both ways on male disobedience (corrected at validation; this sentence first said they "have shown little drift across 45-65 years in re-runs"); **levers and composition effects** (the refusing model, sex mix, culture) have not been stable or have not been re-tested; **levels of self-reported states** (loneliness, narcissism, trust) have drifted, with sign changes inside 10-15 years. The re-run samples are mostly students or from WEIRD countries (the CCTV study is three cities); nothing here is a coupling weight in the strand's sense.

## 6. Accelerated change: the pace of collective attention [read, Lorenz-Spreen et al. 2019]

**Vocabulary.** Collective attention, cultural items, bursts, relative gains and losses (change over level), "boringness", carrying capacity K, finite memory. **Drives/operands.** Topics i with volume L_i(t) (hashtag use, n-gram frequency, ticket sales, search queries, comments, citations, page views); "imitation" (growth proportional to current volume) and saturation by consumption are drive-like (a novelty-seeking, boredom-like term), but the paper proposes no drive. **Data.** Twitter: ~43 billion tweets, the global top-50 hashtags, 2013-2016, daily resolution; Google Books (100 years, 20-year bins), Hollywood box office (40 years, 5-year bins), Google Trends (2010-2018), Reddit (2010-2015), APS citations (about three decades) and Wikipedia traffic (2012-2017). **Findings.** A hashtag's average stay in the top 50 fell from 17.5 h (2013) to 11.9 h (2016), a factor 0.68 or about 12% shorter per year [derived]; average gradients up and down steepened while average peak popularity stayed constant; the same pattern, with distributions of relative change shifting and skewing, in books, movies, Google and Reddit; "for scientific citations and traffic on Wikipedia pages, there is negligible growth of relative gains and losses", which the authors read as knowledge systems being less governed by popularity dynamics. **Model** (their Eqs. 1-3, restated):

dL_i/dt = r_p L_i ( 1 - (r_c / K) Y_i - c sum_{j != i} L_j ),   with   dY_i/dt = L_i - alpha Y_i   [read]

r_p the production (imitation) rate, r_c the consumption rate, K the carrying capacity, c the topic overlap, alpha the memory decay, N topics. For one isolated topic (c = 0, alpha to 0) the solution is L(t) = (2K / r_c) r_p e^{-r_p (t - t_peak)} / (1 + e^{-r_p (t - t_peak)})^2 [read; I re-derived it as the derivative of a logistic with rate r_p and ceiling 2K / r_c, and it peaks at K r_p / (2 r_c) [derived]]. *Validation note (2026-11-01):* the paper's Methods print the peak as L(t_peak) = 2K r_p / r_c, which is four times what its own solution gives at t = t_peak (the solution's last factor is 1/4 there, and the maximum of r_p Y - (r_p r_c / 2K) Y^2 over Y is K r_p / (2 r_c)); the value above is the right one, and the proportionality to r_p / r_c, which is all the argument uses, is unaffected [derived]. So peak height depends on r_p / r_c and the width of a topic's life scales as 1 / r_p. Setting r_c = r_p = r keeps peak height fixed and shortens every topic; the model's parameters were fitted to the 2016 Twitter gain distribution (KS distance 0.01, p = .85) and varying the single rate r then reproduced the trajectories, losses and peak heights across years, and the other datasets "by the same procedure"; larger N (more topics) lowers slopes and peaks, and larger c lowers the whole curve. The authors conclude that acceleration is driven by rising content production and consumption. **Standing.** Descriptive plus an in-sample fit; no held-back year: the Methods, read at validation, say the parameters were chosen by scanning to minimise the KS distance to the 2016 Twitter gains, that "only the parameter r is varied to fit the distributions of the earlier years", and that the other datasets used "the same procedure" (SI not read); r is inferred from the dynamics, with growth in weekly tweets containing top-50 hashtags (two million in late 2013 to four million by the end of 2016) offered as consistent. **Era** Twitter 2013-2016, other datasets spanning about a century of books (the main text says "100 years"; the exact years are in the SI, not read) to 2018; **medium** online (Twitter, Reddit, Wikipedia, Google) and offline (books, cinema, citations); **scale** crowd (adoption and attention); no intervention.

**What it licenses for the strand.** The time constant of a crowd-scale process is a function of the information-throughput regime, so lags, memory lengths and cascade durations estimated in one regime (for example a petition or a phone tree of the 1970s-90s, as an illustration of mine) should carry an era or medium index; the shape (imitation, saturation, competition) is claimed constant; knowledge systems in the data did not accelerate. It says nothing about the person-scale gains of v0.

## 8. Group size and group composition: what the sources say

- **Statistical n, not group size.** Initial studies with small samples and large effects drive the decline: among Pietschnig's declines the first study's effect size accounts for eta-squared .69 of its misestimation and sample size is weakly negatively related to it (eta-squared .05); Fanelli finds small-study effects the largest bias; OSC finds replication power predicts replication success (r = .368); Jennions & Møller find a decline after controlling for sample size. Power is the lever (section 2.6).
- **Group size n.** Asch at five confederates reproduces 33% (Bern); Takano & Sogon with 6-9 confederates 25% (n = 40) and Usto et al. with five 35% (n = 95), both as quoted by Franzen & Mader; the saturation of conformity beyond about three opposing persons is reported in 13 section 16 and in Wikipedia's summary of Asch's variations [sa]; I did not read Asch and this file does not use it. Bystanders: lab individual helping falls with number, real-world aggregate help rises, OR 1.10 per added bystander (Philpot). Collective attention: more competing topics (N) lowers slopes and peaks in the model, and the acceleration is driven by rates, not by N.
- **Composition.** Convenience samples (US university students, mostly male in the Asch replications; 90% students in Buecker) are the evidence base; a campus mix can erase a trend (Twenge 2008, California); sex ratio moderates the loneliness slope (+0.002 per point female); continent differences were not significant but under-powered (Europe 64, Asia 93 samples); bystander effects depend on who the bystanders are (all-male, naive, known to the helper, physically present) [abs]; ML2 found little difference between WEIRD and less WEIRD samples at one time; generalized trust runs from under 10% to over 70% between countries (ICC .84), so a magnitude from one country is a statement about that country; "generation" is a birth-year bin with no natural boundary (Rudolph; the bins in Hawkley are the authors' choice).

## 10. Operand and operator translation

The object-level content of this tradition is small: the crowd-scale attention law above (operands: topic volumes; operators: imitate, saturate by consumption, compete for attention) and the loneliness series (operand: a population mean of a belonging-deficit signal). Everything else is **meta-level**: operands are *claim records* (magnitude, era, medium, population, design, instrument, APC status); operators are SHRINK(claim, power, design), REFRESH(claim, new data), INDEX(law, era), RESCALE(time constant, information regime), and DEBIAS(judgement, judge's standing) for perceived trends. These act on the ledger and on the parameter vector of v0, not on social state; they belong to the versioned-laws machinery in the fluidity note, not to the algebra of persons.

## 13. What exists versus what does not

**Exists.** (1) Three replication programmes whose shrinkage ratios run from 0.25 to 0.71, a fourth and largest at 0.40 on median r (Tyner et al. 2026 [abs], added at validation), and a mechanical account (power times selection) that reproduces the 0.71 for true positives. (2) A clear statement of why generation, period and age cannot be separated without assumptions, by authors who then declined to claim more. (3) A handful of series (loneliness, narcissism, trust, confidants) in which the same construct has been measured by several teams with disagreeing signs, which is itself data about how far to trust a trend. (4) Re-runs of two lab paradigms across 45-70 years (Asch, Milgram) whose base rates vary with population and era (corrected at validation from "with similar base rates"; section 5.4). (5) One measured acceleration, with a one-parameter model. (6) Evidence that perceived generational decline is a memory bias.

**Does not exist (in what I could access).** A generic decay rate for social-behaviour parameters that is not confounded with E. Any cross-era re-estimate of a coupling weight, threshold, inertia or tie sign. A calendar test of the hidden-moderator account. An accepted prospective demonstration that rigour prevents declines (retracted). A well-identified cohort effect in any series read. A re-test of the strand's own load-bearing sources (fraternity ties, couple time series) against a modern sample, which is D608's job. Independent out-of-sample tests of the attention-acceleration model.

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

Mostly no; the part that reduces is narrow and is about the *signals*, not the decay.

**What reduces to drives of persons or groups.**

- *Loneliness as a deficit signal.* Defined as an aversive state from a perceived mismatch between desired and actual relationships, it is the kind of deficit signal that 056's need-threat and sociometer loop models. A drift in its mean can be written as drift in the desired level (a set point), in the supply (what relationships are available), or in the mapping from mismatch to report; the drive reading gives the form of the question. No source separates the three, so nothing here estimates a drive parameter.
- *Imitation and saturation in the attention law.* Growth proportional to current volume (a copying coupling) and a boredom-like consumption term are drive-shaped (cf. 054, boredom; 064, contagion), and the competition for finite attention is a shared-resource constraint. The paper itself proposes no drive.
- *Researcher incentives as a cause of shrinkage.* Status and publication pressure are drive-like, but Fanelli tested the pressure-to-publish hypothesis directly and it was not supported; the dominant bias is small studies, which is power, not motive.

**What does NOT reduce.**

- *Estimate shrinkage (E)* is selection and statistics: low power, significance filtering, regression to the mean. It reduces to nobody's drive; it is a property of how magnitudes are produced, and it applies equally to a drive-based law's parameters.
- *Yardstick and composition drift (M)*: item non-equivalence, interviewer effects, response-order effects, campus mix are properties of instruments and samples.
- *Cohort replacement (C)* is arithmetic on a population mixture (the merge and split of groups, 083), not a drive.
- *Observer illusion (O)*: the belief that youth decline is a memory and self-comparison bias; the memory mechanism is cognitive.
- *Acceleration of attention*: the moving parameter is the communication rate r, a property of the technology and information regime, not of any drive.
- *Reflexive change (R)*: knowledge modifying behaviour (Gergen) is an operator on parameters.
- *The composition and group-definition issues* (bystanders who are known versus strangers; what a "generation" is) are structure.

**Verdict on "drives as primitives".** Untouched in the sense that nothing here tests it, and constrained in a useful way: whatever the primitives are, their *parameters* need to be records with era, medium, population and design, and their *forms* are the part that can be stated once. The decay literature gives the drive hypothesis no support and no refutation; it gives it a validity regime.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**What it predicts.** (1) *The direction and rough size of shrinkage on re-estimation* (individual and small-group lab effects): replication effects about half the original in three programmes (OSC 0.49, Camerer about 0.5, ML2 median d 0.25), and 0.71 for true positives; a mechanical account (power x selection) gives 0.71 at 50% power. (2) *Which findings will replicate*: peers in prediction markets and surveys predicted Camerer's 61.9% at 63.4% and 60.6%, and the abstract says peer beliefs were strongly related to which individual results replicated; in OSC the original p-value and effect size predicted success better than team characteristics (p < .02 gave 41%, p < .001 gave 63%). (3) *Period trends in self-report population means*: the narcissism "epidemic" reading implied a continuing rise, and the same authors' update shows a decline after 2008 (d = 0.24 up, 0.16 down); the loneliness "epidemic" reading is partly supported among emerging adults after 2000 and not among older adults; the claim of a post-2012 smartphone jump in loneliness was not borne out in Buecker's data; the US trust decline appears in the GSS and not in the IVS series after 1998. (4) *The pace of attention turnover* on Twitter (17.5 h to 11.9 h in three years) and its one-parameter model, fitted in sample.

**Out-of-sample evidence.** The shrinkage results are the only ones with real out-of-sample character (prospective replications, preregistered). In several of the series read (narcissism, confidants, loneliness) later data, another window or another instrument changed the sign or the size of the trend. The attention model had no held-back year. The one test of whether rigour removes shrinkage was retracted.

**Effect sizes.** Shrinkage ratios 0.25-0.71; Pietschnig about r = 0.10 per decade from the first study; loneliness +0.14 SD per decade (Buecker); narcissism +0.24 d then -0.16 d; Asch -3.8 points over 65 years (36.8% to 33%, different populations; an endpoint comparison, not a drift rate, since re-runs in between ran from near zero to Asch's level, section 5.1); attention turnover about -12% per year in residence time (2013-2016). Typical self-report drift of a tenth to a seventh of an SD per decade, against a shrinkage factor of about two for single-study effects (the two are not directly commensurable: one moves a population mean, the other an experimental effect size).

**Failures and gaps.** Trend claims disagree in sign across samples and instruments (sections 3.2, 4.1, 11); the confidant decline may be an interviewer artefact; the "decline is linear" assertion is not what the largest sample shows; no cross-era estimate exists for any coupling, threshold, inertia or tie sign; the hidden-moderator account is untested on the calendar; the best-known prospective remedy for decline is retracted.

**What it controls, if anything.** Nothing about social behaviour. The controllable object is the evidence base: preregistration, larger samples, open repositories (Schooler's proposal), and prediction-market triage of what to re-test. Those are institutional levers that operate by policy or choice rather than by manipulation. The only interventions on people in what I read are laboratory deceptions: confederates and false feedback (Asch, Protzko & Schooler 2019 study 5, Milgram-type procedures), which are manipulations by design, and an incentive lever disclosed to participants (Franzen & Mader) that lowers wrong answers by 8 points. Scale of every steering claim: membership (conform, comply) in labs; no group- or crowd-scale control claim is made or tested by any source in this file.
