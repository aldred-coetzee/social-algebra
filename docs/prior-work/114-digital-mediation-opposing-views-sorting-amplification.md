## Summary

1. **Bail et al. 2018 is a sign result in one design, not a result about the medium.** [read in full; supplement from the March 2018 preprint.] Republicans paid $11 to follow a bot that retweeted 24 messages a day from liberal elite accounts for a month moved +0.12 points (intent-to-treat; the paper puts the range of its estimates at 0.11 to 0.59 SD, which pairs 0.12 points with 0.11 SD [derived pairing]) more conservative on a 10-item, 7-point policy scale (t = 2.68, p = 0.008, n = 316 treated); the Democratic estimate is the opposite sign and indistinguishable from zero (-0.02, p = 0.45, n = 416). The sign of the point estimate is away from the source in both parties, so in the v0.1 difference form the coupling a_ij is negative for out-party elite streams (significantly so for Republicans, at a size of about a tenth of a standard deviation a month). Era 2017, online, crowd (opinion), consented and paid exposure to content the participants had not seen in advance, with the algorithmic timeline switched off at the experimenters' request. No replication of the bot design was found in one search.
2. **Face to face has the same sign problem, so the data do not show that the medium flips the sign.** The contact literature averages a reduction in prejudice (mean r = -0.21 over 515 studies, collected through 2000, restated by the same authors in 2008 [read; not re-opened at validation]; Δ = 0.39 SD over 27 randomised comparisons, with 20 of 27 intervals overlapping the pooled value and weaker effects for ethnic and racial prejudice [read, Paluck et al. 2019]), but a randomised face-to-face exposure raised exclusionary attitudes (Enos 2014 [abs]). Whether the sign is positive or negative may track conditions (identity relation, source, equal status, common goals) that Bail did not vary, but the contact literature has not shown which: Pettigrew and Tropp's abstract calls Allport's conditions "not essential for prejudice reduction" [abs], and Paluck et al. write that they "have not been systematically investigated" [read] (both checked at validation). "Online flips the sign" is not supported; "the sign of the exposure term is an input indexed by receiver identity, source type, dose and medium" is.
3. **Three field experiments, 2017 to 2020, three different answers on opposing-view exposure.** Bail et al. 2018 (data 2017): backfire on issue positions among Republicans. Levy 2021 (data 2018; Feb to Mar recruitment; two-month exposure; 37,494 at baseline, 17,635 at endline): subscribing to counter-attitudinal news lowered an affective-polarisation index by 0.03 SD (intent-to-treat) to 0.06 SD (treated) against pro-attitudinal news, and left political opinions unchanged [read: working paper; not re-opened at validation, directions confirmed by the AER abstract]. Nyhan et al. 2023 (data 2020, 24 Sep to 23 Dec; 23,377 consenting users): cutting like-minded sources in the Facebook feed from 53.7% to 36.2% moved none of eight pre-registered attitude measures by more than the bounds (±0.12 SD ruled out) [read]. The designs differ in source (elite accounts, news outlets, an algorithm re-rank), outcome (issue position, affect), incentive and feed state. Three more online designs, added at validation from the validator's knowledge, resolved by DOI and read at abstract level only, point away from backfire when the source is a peer or an outlet: cross-partisan video conversations (Santoro and Broockman 2022), anonymous cross-party chat (Combs et al. 2023, Bail's group) and paid reading of extreme out-party outlets (Casas et al. 2023) [abs; section 14].
4. **Törnberg's sorting mechanism is a model with one assumed medium parameter and no data.** [read in full.] Agents on a torus copy attributes from interlocutors chosen with weight (similarity)^h, partisanship counting c = 4 times an issue attribute; digital media are represented only by γ, the fraction of interlocutors drawn from the whole population. Sorting ψ rises sharply with γ at high influence homophily (h = 8), not at h = 1, and is bistable at h = 4. My independent re-implementation reproduces the three regimes qualitatively [repro], and shows that the stationary ψ is the same at N = 196 and N = 400 while the time to reach it grows faster than N. Its neighbour-replacement rule differs from the author's published code, found at validation; under the published rule the regimes also appear but the rise with γ is slower (section 2). The paper's premises are partly contradicted by evidence read here (Bail, which the paper itself cites, has negative point estimates; interaction homophily is platform-dependent in Cinelli et al. [read]). It moves the support of the relation matrix A and the similarity gating of |a_ij|, not a sign.
5. **Lorenz-Spreen et al. 2023 gives directions, not magnitudes.** [read in full.] 496 articles to 15 September 2021; 354 coded associations over ten variable groups; 24 articles with causal designs; no effect sizes were computed. Participation: mostly beneficial. Trust, polarisation, populism, hate, misinformation: detrimental in the clear majority. Established democracies detrimental, emerging ones beneficial. Only 13 of 354 associations were pre-registered and 79 had open data; the authors report a slight drift toward more detrimental and more null findings over time. Its causal list for polarisation rests partly on Allcott et al. 2020, whose index fell 0.16 SD but whose affective component was not significant.
6. **The feed is an operator on exposure with measured size, and exposure changes have not moved attitudes inside three months in 2020.** Twitter's personalised timeline amplifies the reach of party accounts by more than 200% in some cases and of single politicians by up to 400% against the reverse-chronological holdback (Huszár et al., 2020 data) [read]; Levy's accounting identity E = S × A × U (subscription, algorithmic share, usage) puts the algorithm first among the forces skewing exposure to pro-attitudinal outlets [read: working paper; not re-opened at validation]; engagement-ranked Twitter selected tweets 0.24 SD more partisan and 0.24 SD more out-group hostile than the chronological timeline, and readers felt 0.17 SD worse about the out-party (Feb 2023, n = 806, momentary self-report) [read]. Yet the Facebook and Instagram deactivation experiment of 2020 moved affective polarisation by -0.03 SD (not significant after correction) and issue polarisation by an amount whose interval excludes ±0.04 SD [read], where the 2018 deactivation had found a significant -0.10 SD on issues (as the 2024 paper restates it; the working-paper text I read gives the overall index, -0.16 SD, and calls the issue-polarisation effect highly significant) [read].
7. **Online crowd gates: the form is stable, the magnitudes are not.** Hazard of adoption rises with the second and third social signal and clustered networks beat random ones (Centola 2010, anonymous health forum, N = 98 to 144 per network) [read; not re-opened at validation]; the empirical threshold distribution of 87,569 Spanish Twitter users in May 2011 is roughly uniform with modes at 0 and 0.5, and most users joined after bursts of activation among followees [read]; a social message with friends' faces added 0.39 percentage points to validated voting in November 2010 and the real-world spillover ran only through 'close friends', those at or above the 80th percentile of interaction frequency among all friendships in the sample (about 10 of an average 149 friends, about 7% of friendships) [read]; the 2012 replication on the same platform found +0.24 points direct, +0.10 per close friend and a multiplier of about 2 against about 4.7 [read; derived], and its authors call the fall in the direct effect not statistically significant [read].
8. **Sharing and expression are operant, and the medium sets the reward schedule.** Across 2.7 million posts (retrieved 2020; mostly 2016 to 2020) each out-group word multiplied shares or retweets by about 1.67 on the log scale (a regression on log-transformed counts, estimator not named in the main text; the authors call exp(b) a change in odds), in-group words by a factor indistinguishable from 1 [read]; social feedback raises next-day outrage expression with coefficients of 0.02 to 0.03, and network norms weaken the dependence on feedback (7,331 users, 12.7 million tweets, plus two simulated-feed experiments of N = 120 each) [read]. v0.1's x_i is an expression; the authors themselves say they measure expression, not the emotion.
9. **Tie formation online is strongly identity-gated; opinion influence online is not shown to be negative in the lab.** Twitter users followed back a copartisan bot about three times as often as a counterpartisan one, equally in both parties (842 users, field, waiver of consent) [read]; in a computer-mediated lab with 100 students, opinion shifts rose linearly with distance and neither discrepancy nor induced disliking produced shifts away [read; the Study 2 results were read at validation, and its control arm also has a significant quadratic distance term].
10. **No source read measures a small team (n = 8 to 12) under digital mediation, and none compares one coupling across media within a design.** Every result here is dyad scale (the lab pairs, the follow-back) or crowd scale. The v0.1 team-scale and composition terms are therefore untouched by this file.

## 1. Bail et al. 2018: exposure to opposing views on Twitter [read in full]

**Citation.** Bail, C. A., Argyle, L. P., Brown, T. W., Bumpus, J. P., Chen, H., Hunzaker, M. B. F., Lee, J., Mann, M., Merhout, F., Volfovsky, A. (2018). Exposure to opposing views on social media can increase political polarization. *PNAS* 115(37), 9216-9221. doi 10.1073/pnas.1804840115 (Crossref-confirmed). Preprint: SocArXiv, 21 March 2018, osf.io/4ygux, file `Bail_et_al_2018_03_21.pdf`. A web search returned two pre-analysis plans at socialscienceregistry.org that turned out to belong to a different study (Belot and Briscese) and were discarded.

**Their vocabulary.** "Echo chambers", "selective exposure", "virtual contact", "intergroup contact effects", "backfire effects", "compliers", "intent-to-treat (ITT)" and "complier average causal effect (CACE)".

**Operands and operators.** Operand: a person with a party identity (Democrat or Republican, self-reported, with a "strength of attachment" blocking variable) and a position x_i, the mean of ten 7-point agree/disagree policy statements (five worded liberal, reverse-coded so that higher = more conservative; alpha = .91; a negative change = more liberal), measured before and after [read; items and scoring from the preprint SI]. Operator: a stream of 24 retweets a day for one month from accounts of the opposing ideology. No drive is measured.

**Design [read].** Survey firm recruited self-identified Republicans and Democrats who visit Twitter at least three times a week; pretreatment survey mid-October 2017 and a post survey about 1.5 months later; 1,652 pretreatment responses (901 Democrats, 751 Republicans); separate experiments by party, block-randomised on party attachment, interest in current events and frequency of Twitter use; one week after the survey, treated respondents were offered $11 to follow a bot that would retweet 24 messages a day for a month, without being told the content. Each experiment had a liberal and a conservative bot; the bots retweeted at random from 4,176 political accounts (elected officials, opinion leaders, media organisations, nonprofits). Of those invited, 64.9% of Democrats and 57.2% of Republicans accepted; about 62% of followers answered every weekly question about the bot's tweets and 50.2% identified a picture of an animal the bot tweeted and deleted. Analysed: Democrats n = 697, Republicans n = 542 (treated 416 and 316, so controls 281 and 226 [derived]). Controls: pre-score and twelve covariates. All respondents gave consent; Duke and NYU review boards approved.

**Results [read].** ITT (effect of assignment): Democrats -0.02 (t = -0.76, p = 0.45, n = 416); Republicans +0.12 (t = 2.68, p = 0.008, n = 316). CACE, by how strictly compliance is defined: minimal (followed the bot throughout) Democrats -0.04 (p = 0.45, n = 271), Republicans +0.19 (p < 0.007, n = 181); partial (answered some content questions) -0.05 (n = 211) and +0.31 (n = 121); full (answered all) -0.14 (p = 0.46, n = 66) and +0.60 (t = 2.53, p < 0.01, n = 53). The authors state that the estimates "correspond to an increase in conservatism between 0.11 and 0.59 standard deviations". The Democratic estimates are negative, which means more liberal after following a conservative bot, and none is significant. The preregistered hypotheses were: H1 contact lowers polarisation (not supported), H2 exposure creates backfire (supported for Republicans), H3 backfire is stronger among conservatives (supported). A first reading of the sign: in both parties the point estimate is away from the source.

**What the preprint supplement adds [read: preprint SI; numbers differ from PNAS where noted].** (a) Pretreatment survey fielded 10 to 19 October 2017 through YouGov (10,634 invited, 5,114 accepted, 2,539 eligible, 1,754 completed, 102 removed as too fast, leaving 1,652); compliance checks 27 to 29 October, 3 to 5 November and 10 to 13 November; post survey 23 to 27 November. The invitation to follow went out on 21 October, so the exposure window ran from late October to late November 2017 (corrected at validation from "November 2017"). (b) Respondents were asked to disable Twitter's algorithmic timeline "to ensure that they viewed tweets from their bot"; the bots followed no one, to blunt the recommender; the first days showed only landscape pictures before opposing tweets were phased in. The algorithm was therefore deliberately not part of this treatment. (c) The liberal bot retweeted at random, once an hour, from accounts in the three most liberal of seven quantiles of a correspondence-analysis ideology score of accounts followed by the presidential candidates and members of Congress as of 5 August 2017 (636,737 accounts reduced to those followed by at least 15 of these officials; centrist quantile dropped); the conservative bot likewise from the three most conservative. (d) Respondents were told the sponsor would monitor muting, which was not possible: an IRB-approved deception, debriefed afterwards; the consent text said "research sponsor" and not "academic". (e) A search for moderators by a LASSO showed, in the printed output, every moderator coefficient shrunk to zero at the chosen penalty. (f) The preprint's final sample was 1,220 (691 Democrats, 529 Republicans), its compliance percentages were 40.14% and 32.48% and its acceptance rates 64.8% and 57.6%, against 697/542, 62%/50.2% and 64.9%/57.2% in PNAS (the acceptance pair added at validation); the published numbers presumably follow a revised sample and denominator, which I could not check. (g) The preprint compares the full-compliance Republican estimate with the 1994 to 2014 drift of a Pew policy-consistency index (Dimock and Carroll 2014, as the preprint cites it) by a rough rescaling, and says the two are not directly comparable.

**Limits the authors state [read].** Not general to all Americans or to non-Twitter users; no independents or light users; participants were paid, so unpaid users might ignore opposing messages; the mechanism is unidentified; the bots exposed people only to high-profile elites, who are more polarised than the public; the effect might come from more exposure to politics in general (the preprint tests Twitter use and finds no significant change), or from exposure to women or minorities in the liberal bot's stream. They write that readers "should not interpret our findings as evidence that exposure to opposing political views will increase polarization in all settings".

**Empirical standing, bluntly.** One field experiment, one run, 2017, a self-selected, paid, frequent-user sample, a single party significant, no replication of the design found in a search on 2026-11-01. Levy (section 4) argues the difference between his result and Bail's lies in the source (news outlets against opinion leaders), the incentive, the disabled algorithm and compliance checks. Not independently resolved.

**Reading for the algebra [ours].** In v0.1 §3 the signed difference form is I_ij = a_ij(x_j - x_i); for a_ij < 0 the receiver moves away from the source. The point estimates fit a_ij < 0 for out-party elite streams in both parties, with |a_ij| of about 0.1 SD a month for Republicans and unmeasurably small for Democrats. v0.1 §2.2 makes sgn(a_ij) an input, and this result says the input depends at least on the receiver's identity, the source's identity and the dose. It does not say it depends on the medium: nothing in the design is face to face.

**Era, medium, scale, size, consent.** Era: October to November 2017. Medium: online (Twitter), chronological timeline by request. Scale: crowd, opinion. Size: n = 542 and 697 analysed; composition: party-identified frequent Twitter users, median age about 50 [read: preprint SI], with a Republican-only significant effect. Intervention: exposure to a stream the participant consented to receive, but with unknown content and a deception about monitoring; paid.

## 2. Törnberg 2022: digital media, partisan sorting and the nonlocal fraction [read in full]

**Citation.** Törnberg, P. (2022). How digital media drive affective polarization through partisan sorting. *PNAS* 119(42), e2207159119. doi 10.1073/pnas.2207159119 (Crossref-confirmed; CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). Precursor model, not read: Törnberg, Andersson, Lindgren and Banisch (2021), *PLoS ONE* 16(10), e0258259.

**Their vocabulary.** "Selective exposure" and "echo chambers" (the hypothesis opposed); "affective polarization"; "sorting" or "alignment of difference" (conflicts coming to coincide with one partisan line); "cross-cutting cleavages"; "influence homophily"; "nonlocal interaction"; "negative influence" (opinions moving away from a source).

**Claim in one sentence [read].** Digital media raise the share of interactions with people outside one's local network; where people are more influenced by those similar to themselves, that alone drives issue attributes to align with partisan identity at the system scale, with no negative influence, no interaction homophily and no selective exposure assumed.

**The model, transcribed from the text and the MathML [read].** Agents on a w x w torus (default 196 = 14 x 14; 625 for pictures), eight Moore neighbours. Agent i has n dynamic attributes D_{i,l} in {1..m} (opinions, tastes, behaviours; defaults n = 10, m = 10) and one fixed attribute S_i in {1..k}, k = 2, partisan affiliation, held fixed. All attributes start uniformly random. Each step a uniformly random agent is updated (asynchronous). Its interlocutor set I is its Moore neighbours "with a fraction γ (in [0,1]) replaced by nodes sampled randomly from the entire network". Define δ^S_ij = 1 if S_i = S_j else 0, and δ^D_{ij,l} = 1 if D_{i,l} = D_{j,l} else 0. Absolute similarity: δ_ij = (c δ^S_ij + Σ_{l=1..n} δ^D_{ij,l}) / (c + n), with c = 4 in the runs. Relative similarity: w_ij = δ_ij^h / Σ_{l in I} δ_il^h; "one of the interactors is chosen using an urn model" with these weights. "When influenced, the agent takes one of the dynamic attributes from the other agent for which the two have different values." Sorting: d_ij = (Σ_l δ^D_{ij,l}) / n; Q_same = {d_ij : i != j, S_i = S_j}, Q_diff = {d_ij : S_i != S_j}; ψ = mean(Q_same) - mean(Q_diff), 0 at random, 1 if groups are internally identical and share nothing. The run ends after ten full sweeps without an update or at 10^6 iterations. The paper says convergence is hard to prove analytically and offers no theorem.

**Results as stated [read].** At h = 8 the final ψ is near 0 at γ = 0 and rises sharply once γ passes a threshold; at h = 1 there is no sorting at any γ; at h = 4 the system is bistable for higher γ ("low-polarization or high-polarization states, depending on initial conditions and stochastic dynamics"). Parameters for the figures: |A| = 196, c = 4, m = 10, n = 10, 8,000 runs. (One sentence says the runs used h = 1, 2, 5; the figure caption and text use 1, 4, 8; I used 1, 4, 8.) The same relation holds on a random regular graph, a scale-free network with clustering, and a connected-caveman network, which needs a larger γ before it sorts. Mechanism: with γ = 0 the groups converge locally on separate attribute sets within each region, so local cultures cancel and global ψ stays low; with high γ the groups converge globally on two sets along S. With h = 1 the whole system converges on one culture. The paper reports no calibration to data and no estimate of γ; the Discussion links the model to the nationalisation of US politics as an analogy (a Mississippi Republican once resembled a Mississippi Democrat more than a Massachusetts Republican).

**Independent re-implementation [repro].** I wrote the rules above in C (own reading of two ambiguities: each neighbour is replaced with probability γ, and the chosen interlocutor always influences if any attribute differs). 24 runs per cell, 14 x 14 torus. h = 1: ψ = 0.000 at every γ from 0 to 1. h = 8: mean ψ 0.024 (γ = 0), 0.41 (0.1), 0.65 (0.2), 0.78 (0.3), 0.85 (0.4), 0.88 (0.5), 0.87 to 0.89 for γ from 0.6 to 1; the share of runs with ψ > 0.5 goes 0, 0.29, 0.79, 0.96, 1.00; at the larger γ most runs hit the 10^6 cap with ψ about 0.88. h = 4: mean ψ between 0.18 and 0.65 with standard deviation 0.33 to 0.44 for γ of 0.1 and above (0.20 at γ = 0), non-monotone in γ, i.e. bimodal runs. The three regimes the paper describes are reproduced; the rise is sharper than "a threshold" suggests, the plateau is 0.87 to 0.89 and not 1.0 (the paper also says ψ does not always reach 1), and the h = 4 behaviour is bistable but not a smooth LOWESS curve. Size check (h = 8): at γ = 0.1 the stationary ψ is 0.45 (N = 64, converged in about 0.9 thousand sweeps), 0.43 (N = 196, about 5.9 thousand sweeps) and 0.43 (N = 400, about 16 thousand sweeps); at γ = 0.2 it is 0.65, 0.61, 0.58; at N = 784 most runs had not converged after 25,000 sweeps (ψ 0.22 and 0.47). So in the range tested the endpoint does not depend on N but the time to reach it grows faster than N. The paper runs one size.

**Check against the published code (validation, 2026-11-01) [read: code; repro].** The repository the paper deposits (section 0) settles both ambiguities. The second reading matches: the chosen interlocutor copies one differing attribute whenever one differs, and a step counts as no update when the two already agree or every weight is zero. The first does not: the code replaces exactly int(γ × degree) neighbours, drawn without replacement from all nodes (the focal agent included), so on the eight-neighbour torus every γ below 0.125 is identical to γ = 0 and γ acts in steps of 1/8. The γ = 0.1 to 0.3 cells above and the size check therefore describe this re-implementation's binomial variant, not Törnberg's model. A short Python re-implementation of the published step (4 runs per cell, otherwise the same settings and stopping rule) gives, at h = 8, mean ψ 0.02 (γ = 0), 0.19 (γ = 0.125; range 0.09 to 0.36), 0.39 (0.25), 0.94 (0.5) and 0.95 (1.0, every run at 0.90 or 1.00); at h = 1, ψ = 0.000 at γ = 0 and 1; at h = 4, final ψ between 0 and 0.58 at γ = 0.25 and between 0 and 0.90 at γ = 1 (bimodal). So the three regimes hold under the published rule too, but the rise is slower than in the variant above (0.39 at γ = 0.25 against 0.65 to 0.78 at γ = 0.2 to 0.3), and "the rise is sharper than 'a threshold' suggests" does not carry over. Neither implementation matches the paper's own Fig. 1 text, in which a γ = 0.5 run "gradually increases to a ψ of 0.3" over 500,000 steps: all four published-rule runs at γ = 0.5 stopped at ψ of 0.90 or more within 408,319 steps. Not resolved; the deposited code may differ from what drew Fig. 1. Four runs per cell is a check, not an estimate; the code sits in the validator's scratchpad, not in the repo.

**Premise audit.** The model's claim about digital media enters only through γ. Three premises, checked against what was read:
- *Digital media raise interaction outside the local bubble.* Cinelli et al. [read] (platform data: Twitter June 2016, Facebook January 2010 onward, Reddit January 2017, Gab November 2017) find homophilic interaction clusters on Facebook and Twitter, a single community on Reddit and Gab, and higher news segregation on Facebook than on Reddit: the interaction graph is platform-dependent. Nyhan et al. [read] find the median US Facebook user in mid-2020 had 50.4% of feed exposures from like-minded sources and 14.7% from cross-cutting ones; only 32.2% of users had a quarter or more of exposures from cross-cutting sources. Cross-cutting exposure exists; γ itself, and its pre-digital value, are not measured by anyone I read. Mosleh et al. [read] show that new ties between strangers are about three times likelier with copartisans, so nonlocal does not mean random with respect to S; the paper assumes unbiased nonlocal choice and says bias "would likely intensify" the effect.
- *Negative influence is elusive.* The paper cites Wood and Porter 2019, Guess and Coppock 2020 and Takács et al. 2016 (the last read here: in a computer-mediated lab with 100 students and 850 interactions, opinion shifts rose linearly with distance and neither discrepancy nor induced disliking produced shifts away). Against that, Bail's month-long field design (section 1) is a negative-influence estimate, significant for Republicans, and the paper itself cites Bail for a different point. The honest reading: no negative influence in short single-exposure designs, a negative point estimate in the one long field design; mixed.
- *Reducing echo chambers does not reduce polarisation.* Supported by Nyhan et al. and by the 2023 chronological-feed abstract [abs], but those experiments remove exposure for three months and measure attitudes, not an alignment-across-issues quantity like ψ. No experiment I read measures a ψ-like outcome. Boxell et al. [read: working paper] find, across twelve OECD countries, that trends in partisan sorting are positively associated with trends in affective polarisation and that the share getting news online is negatively or weakly associated (internet penetration likewise); twelve countries is a small sample, and the association is between trends, not a mechanism.

**What moves in v0.1 [ours].** Three things, none a sign. (i) The support of A: γ is the fraction of non-local entries in each row. (ii) The magnitude of the coupling becomes similarity-gated and unsigned, |a_ij| proportional to δ_ij^h, a graded gate (h to infinity is a hard gate) of the same family as Leonard's dynamic a_jl in v0.1 §3; the identity label S_i enters the similarity with weight c = 4, so group identity sets the gate's width and not the sign. (iii) The outcome ψ is a between-block distance, the crowd-scale partition Π_T of v0.1 §5. v0.1's no-split certificate (§6.1) assumes fixed, sign-stable difference-form weights; Törnberg's weights depend on state, and his own results show multiple stable partitions (the bistable h = 4 regime) from positive-only influence. That is why the certificate is silent here [inf]: it is not violated, its hypothesis class does not contain this model.

**Empirical standing.** None as a model: no data fit, no held-out test, no independent replication located (one search; a 2025 arXiv paper, Komuro, arXiv:2503.19756, couples digital-media-driven affective polarisation to epidemic spread; its abstract, read at validation, does not say that it builds on Törnberg's model). It is an existence proof that a positive-only mechanism can produce sorting, not evidence that it does. Scale: crowd, opinion alignment; medium: abstract; era: none.

**Size and composition.** The paper's N is 196 (625 for figures), and "n" in the paper is the number of attributes, not the group size. Composition enters through k = 2 and c = 4; k > 2 is in the SI, not read. Network structure matters more than N: the caveman graph (14 cliques of 14) needs a larger γ.

## 3. Lorenz-Spreen, Oswald, Lewandowsky and Hertwig 2023: the review [read in full]

**Citation.** Lorenz-Spreen, P., Oswald, L., Lewandowsky, S., Hertwig, R. (2023). A systematic review of worldwide causal and correlational evidence on digital media and democracy. *Nature Human Behaviour* 7(1), 74-101. doi 10.1038/s41562-022-01460-1 (Crossref-confirmed; CC BY 4.0). Pre-registered protocol and coded table at osf.io/7ry4a (not fetched).

**Their vocabulary.** "Digital media" (internet access in general down to specific platforms and content types), "causal and correlational evidence", "political variables" (participation, trust, political knowledge, expression, polarization, populism, hate, network homophily, misinformation, exposure to diverse viewpoints), "beneficial" or "detrimental" to democracy.

**Method [read].** MOOSE guidelines; Scopus and Web of Science; articles up to 15 September 2021; original empirical work in English only; small laboratory experiments and student surveys under N = 100, simulations, preprints and evidence syntheses excluded. The query returned 3,509 articles; 1,349 survived title screening; 474 survived abstract rounds; 22 more were added from earlier reviews: N = 496. Abstract-round inter-coder reliability: Krippendorff's alpha 0.66 (87% agreement). For the overview "we mainly restricted ourselves to the evaluation effects reported in the abstracts". Ten variable groups give 354 coded associations; 24 articles have causal designs (voting-advice-application studies excluded). "No calculation of meta-analytic effect sizes was possible"; findings are coded beneficial or detrimental to democracy by the authors, with polarisation and populism counted as detrimental, a normative step they flag.

**Findings [read].** Participation: associations point mostly beneficial, including causal studies on turnout and protest. Trust in institutions: "overwhelmingly" detrimental, and mostly detrimental in the causal articles (a browser-default experiment found a long-term loss of trust in mainstream media; unrestricted internet access lowered political trust in several authoritarian settings, which the authors note may be a step toward liberalisation there). Political knowledge and diversity of news exposure: mostly beneficial, less clear, with a news-finds-me mechanism that lowers knowledge. Polarisation: "most articles found detrimental associations", more outgroup polarisation on social media, linked to exposure to opposing views on feeds; "among established democracies, both social media use and overall internet use increased political polarization" (their refs 63 and 70, the second being Allcott et al. 2020; ref. 63 has broadband and populism in its title and was not read); Bail's treatment is cited as also polarising and Levy's as running the other way. Populism and hate: detrimental in the clear majority, with causal studies on broadband and far-right voting and on social media and hate crime. Echo chambers: news diets diversify while social networks stay homophilous, a seeming paradox the authors say outcome definition resolves. Context: most beneficial effects in emerging democracies (South America, Africa, South Asia), detrimental in Europe, the US and parts of Russia; beneficial outcomes are moderated more by political interest and news use, detrimental ones by partisanship and political position. The authors "strongly caution" against generalising US-bound findings.

**Bias checks [read].** Clear conflict-of-interest statements on 143 of 354 measurements; open-data practice on 79; pre-registration on 13 (9 detrimental, 3 beneficial, 1 none). Without open data: 103 beneficial against 33 detrimental; with open data: 19 against 14. The beneficial share is 76% (103/136) without open data and 58% (19/33) with [derived]. A trend "toward an increasing number of both detrimental directions and null effects over time" is reported (Supplementary Fig. 4, not read). Authors contacted by mailing lists for unpublished work and received none that fitted.

**Their own baseline warning [read].** The reading of Levy's and Bail's results, they write, has findings that "depend on whether one assumes that social media have increased or decreased exposure to opposing views relative to some offline benchmark", a benchmark most articles lack; they note that in Levy's study Facebook's feed down-ranked counter-attitudinal exposure.

**Empirical standing.** A direction-of-association map, not a measurement. (Corrected at validation: an earlier line here said probability samples and surveys give more beneficial findings and behavioural data more detrimental ones. The review says the method tracks the outcome studied, behavioural data being "much more prevalent" for polarisation and echo chambers and probability or quota surveys "often used" for trust and participation; that "no coherent pattern emerges in terms of the reported directions"; and that "if anything, large probabilistic samples report relatively less beneficial associations" [read].) It cannot give a gain or a sign for a_ij; it gives which outcomes to model in which political setting. The review's headline on polarisation is stronger than its causal base: the causal studies it names for polarisation are Allcott et al. 2020 (an index that fell 0.16 SD under deactivation, the affective component not significant), a broadband-and-populism study (ref. 63, unread) and Bail (a Republican-only effect), against Levy (the other direction) and a browser-default experiment with no opinion shift; the most direct opposing-view experiments point in opposite directions.

**Era, medium, scale.** Studies through 2021, most in the last five years; online (internet in general, social media, online news); scale: crowd (participation = mobilisation, trust, polarisation = opinion, populism = vote); consent varies by study and is not summarised.

**Translation [ours].** A validity table keyed by outcome and political setting: for each of (participation, trust, polarisation, populism, knowledge) the sign of the association with digital media use and the settings in which it holds, with the quality grade. That is the review's usable content for a validity column, and it is a direction column only.

Four observations.
1. **The sign differs by design, and only Bail's is positive on issues.** On issue positions: Bail +0.12 points for Republicans; Levy 0; Nyhan 0 within ±0.12 SD; Allcott et al. 2020 (2018 data) a reduction when Facebook is removed (so Facebook use raises issue polarisation); Allcott et al. 2024 (2020 data) 0 within ±0.04 SD. On affect: Levy a small decrease from counter-attitudinal news; Allcott et al. 2020 a non-significant decrease from deactivation; Allcott et al. 2024 and Nyhan 0. (Labels corrected at validation: this paragraph had named the two deactivation studies by data year, so "Allcott 2020" pointed at the 2024 paper while the table's "Allcott et al. 2020" is the 2018 study.)
2. **The same method gave different answers in 2018 and 2020.** The deactivation study of 2018 reported a significant issue-polarisation reduction (-0.10 SD as the 2024 paper restates it) and the 2020 one a precise zero; the authors suggest a presidential election saturated with other political information, or the choice of issues. That is a replication failure on a magnitude, inside one platform, two years apart. Allcott et al. 2020 also report, as heterogeneity, larger polarisation reductions for older people, white people and men [read: WP], and Levy's effect is stronger for the less polarised at baseline (10% level).
3. **Large exposure changes, no attitude change.** Nyhan's treatment moved like-minded exposure by 0.77 SD (53.7% to 36.2%) and cross-cutting exposure by 0.43 SD (20.7% to 27.9%); a larger share of the freed feed went to sources that are neither (25.6% to 35.9%). Engagement per exposure to like-minded sources rose (+0.04 SD passive, +0.13 SD active). Attitudes did not move.
4. **Consent and manipulation.** Every Meta-platform study had informed consent to participate and payment or none; the feed change itself was not individually negotiated. The Twitter holdback of Huszár et al. and the Bond message were applied without individual consent (section 5, 6). Levy's design is the one closest to consent-based anchoring: a visible, ordinary, declinable offer, to which about half said yes. None of these works by appeal to a drive; each changes the environment.

## 5. The feed as an operator on exposure

**Huszár, Ktena, O'Brien, Belli, Schlaikjer and Hardt 2022** [read]. *Citation:* PNAS 119(1), e2025334119, doi 10.1073/pnas.2025334119 (Crossref-confirmed, published 21 Dec 2021). *Design:* since June 2016 Twitter has held back a random group of accounts (1% of all accounts, against 4% treated) from the personalised timeline; as of 5 June 2020 the experimental pool had 58,087,969 accounts, 5% of all accounts, 20% of them control (reverse-chronological) and 80% treated. *Measure:* the amplification ratio, the reach of a set of tweets T among an audience U in the treated group against its reach in the control group, normalised so 0% means equal proportional reach and 100% means double. *Results:* legislators in seven countries (3,634 accounts): every party is amplified, some above 200%; the mainstream right is amplified more than the mainstream left in six of seven countries (Canada: Liberals 43%, Conservatives 167%; United Kingdom: Labour 112%, Conservatives 176%; Germany not significant); single politicians range from below 0% to about 400%, and at the level of individuals party membership does not predict amplification, while at the level of whole parties it does; far-left and far-right parties are amplified less than centrist ones; news outlets (100,575,284 tweets pointing to 6,258,032 articles, 1 April to 15 August 2020 in the main text; the Methods give tweets "created between 1 March and 30 June 2020" for the same counts, an inconsistency in the source noted at validation): on the AllSides ratings partisan sources are amplified more than centre sources and the partisan right marginally more than the partisan left; on the Ad Fontes ratings the partisan left is amplified only 10.5% and the neutral category least, so the conclusion depends on the rating scheme. *Limits stated by the authors:* the control group is exposed to the treated group's content (no stable-unit assumption), so these are simple comparisons that probably understate the effect; the ranking changed over time and depended on the experiment itself; the holdback was a business measure reviewed by the company's legal and privacy teams "(and not an IRB)", which found no additional notice or consent mechanism necessary. *Scale:* crowd, exposure (it measures reach, not adoption, mobilisation or opinion). *Composition and definition:* the answer changes with the unit: aggregated party reach differs by party while individual reach does not, because audiences overlap.

**Levy's accounting identity** [read: WP; not re-opened at validation, so the numbers in this paragraph rest on the author's reading]. E_ij = S_ij A_ij U_i: exposure of person i to posts of outlet j is the subscription indicator, times the share of i's feed that comes from j (the algorithm's allocation), times the total number of posts i sees. The pro- against counter-attitudinal gap in exposure is decomposed into seven terms (subscription gap, algorithm gap, usage gap and four interactions); the text says the algorithm is "the strongest force" behind the skew toward pro-attitudinal exposure, that participants also prefer to subscribe to pro-attitudinal outlets, and that usage falls when counter-attitudinal outlets are offered (10% level); the algorithmic effect grows slightly over time, which Levy reads as engagement entering the ranking. The magnitudes sit in a figure I did not read. Instrumented effect of exposure on affect (exclusion restriction assumed): a one-standard-deviation rise in the counter-attitudinal share of the feed lowers affective polarisation by 0.13 SD; the effects are 26% to 34% of the cross-sectional coefficients in the control group (so the correlation is partly selection). If the feed were balanced, the thermometer gap would fall by 3.94 degrees; to the share seen off-platform, by 0.25 to 0.62 degrees.

**Milli, Carroll, Wang, Pandey, Zhao and Dragan 2025** [read]. *Design:* 11 to 27 February 2023, 806 US Twitter users recruited on a crowd-work platform, a Chrome extension collected the first ten tweets of the engagement-ranked timeline and, through the API, the ten most recent from followed accounts; the same users rated each tweet; informed consent; preregistered. *Results against the chronological timeline:* tweets 0.24 SD more partisan and 0.24 SD more out-group hostile (reader judgements); readers feel 0.17 SD worse about their out-group and 0.08 SD better about their in-group; anger expressed +0.47 SD, anger felt +0.27 SD (political tweets 0.75 and 0.37 SD); stated preference for tweets +0.06 SD overall but -0.18 SD for political tweets. A timeline re-ranked by stated preference lowers anger and animosity but does so mainly by removing out-group tweets and animosity toward the in-group, not animosity toward the out-group, and the effects on readers' perceptions of in-group and out-group do not differ significantly between the stated-preference and the engagement timelines. *Scale:* crowd, exposure plus a momentary reaction, not an attitude.

**Other platform audits at abstract level [abs].** Bakshy, Messing and Adamic 2015 (10.1 million US Facebook users; the dates July 2014 to January 2015 are not in the abstract, the only text read, and are unverified): individuals' own choices limited cross-cutting exposure more than the algorithm. González-Bailón et al. 2023 (208 million US users, 2020): segregation of political news rises from potential exposure to exposure to engagement, with a corner of the news ecosystem consumed only by conservatives and holding most flagged misinformation. Cinelli et al. 2021 [read] (section 2) is the comparative platform study. Taken with Levy's decomposition these say that the allocation is shared between user choice and ranking, and that the shares differ by platform and year.

**Reading for the algebra [ours].** The medium acts on the exposure weights, not on the response law: if w_ij is the probability that i sees j's content, the feed replaces it by w_ij times a ratio (Huszár's amplification, Nyhan's down-ranking), and the response x_i per unit exposure is a separate parameter. The only identified estimate of the latter in what I read is Levy's instrumented 0.13 SD per SD of counter-attitudinal share (affective), which is itself conditional on an exclusion restriction. This is the form direction D611 will need; I do not fix it here. The data say the exposure operator is large and measurable and the response coefficient is small, platform-bound and unstable across years.

## 6. The online crowd gate: mobilisation, adoption, sharing

### 6.1 Bond et al. 2012 and the 2012 replication [read]

**Citations.** Bond, R. M., Fariss, C. J., Jones, J. J., Kramer, A. D. I., Marlow, C., Settle, J. E., Fowler, J. H. (2012). A 61-million-person experiment in social influence and political mobilization. *Nature* 489, 295-298, doi 10.1038/nature11421. Jones, J. J., Bond, R. M., Bakshy, E., Eckles, D., Fowler, J. H. (2017). Social influence and political mobilization: further evidence from a randomized experiment in the 2012 U.S. presidential election. *PLoS ONE* 12(4), e0173851, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0173851.

**Design (2010) [read].** All US Facebook users aged 18 or over who loaded Facebook on 2 November 2010, the congressional election day. Social message group n = 60,055,176 (an election message, a polling-place link, an "I Voted" button, a counter, and up to six faces of friends who had clicked); informational message group n = 611,044 (the same without faces); control n = 613,096. Outcomes: clicking "I Voted" (expression), clicking the polling-place link (information seeking), validated voting for 6.3 million users matched to public records. Users had 149 friends on average; "close friends" are those at or above the 80th percentile of interaction frequency among all friendships in the sample (about 10 close friends and 139 others per user).

**Results (2010) [read].** Social against informational message: "I Voted" +2.08 percentage points (20.04% against 17.96%); polling-place link +0.26 points; validated voting +0.39 points (s.e.m. 0.17, p = 0.02), the same difference as social against no message; informational against control 0.00 (s.e.m. 0.28, p = 0.98), so information without social cues did nothing. Spillover per close friend who got the social message: expressed vote +0.099 points (null interval -0.042 to +0.048), validated vote +0.224 points (null -0.181 to +0.174), polling search +0.012 points; ordinary friends had no detectable effect on validated vote or polling search but did on "I Voted" (+0.011 points, null -0.009 to +0.010). Totals: friends generated 886,000 extra expressed votes, close friends 559,000 more, close friends of close friends another 1 million; close friends generated 282,000 validated votes and 74,000 polling searches, "close friendships accounted for all of the significant contagion of these behaviours, in spite of" being 7% of friendships. Direct plus indirect: about 340,000 extra votes, 0.14% of the voting-age population [checked: 60,000 + 280,000]. The authors say all effects are intent-to-treat and close friends gave "about four times more influence on the total number of validated voters mobilized than the message itself".

**Replication, 2012 [read].** A 2 x 2 design with a banner and a News Feed story; 14,458,236 users received both and 150,139 neither; validated turnout 73.91% against 74.08%. Direct effect +0.17 points unadjusted (95% interval -0.05 to +0.40, not significant) and +0.24 points with prior-turnout covariates (+0.03 to +0.44, p = 0.0266); instrumented for log-in, 0.35 points. Spillover per close friend (90th percentile and above, about five per person): banner +0.10 points (a null distribution of -0.09 to +0.07 contains 99%); the feed story +0.01 (no spillover). The multiplier the authors compute is 5 x 0.10 / 0.24, about 2 friends moved per person moved. Totals: about 90,000 direct and 180,000 indirect, 270,000 [checked].

**Comparison of the two runs [derived].** Same platform, two years apart, same lab: direct effect 0.39 against 0.24 points (the 2012 interval just excludes zero); spillover per close friend 0.224 against 0.10 points; indirect-to-direct ratio 280,000/60,000 = 4.7 against 180,000/90,000 = 2.0. The sign is stable and the magnitudes fell by roughly 40% to 55%, with a changed message system and a different definition of "close". The 2012 authors expected smaller effects in a saturated presidential campaign. They also call the drop in the direct effect "statistically insignificant", and write that "the differences between the 2010 estimates, ITT estimates from 2012 (0.24%), and LATE estimates (0.34%) from 2012 estimates are not statistically significant" [read; added at validation; the same paper prints its Wald estimate as 0.35% in the Results]. The per-close-friend and ratio comparisons are untested and cross a change of definition (80th against 90th percentile), so this is a fall in point estimates, not a demonstrated decline.

**Scale, medium, consent.** Crowd, mobilisation; online (Facebook) but acting on offline voting; era November 2010 and November 2012; manipulation without individual consent (the main text states no consent procedure; the SI is unread). Offline benchmark from the paper's own introduction: most methods of contacting voters, face to face included, move turnout by 1% to 10%, and a meta-analysis of email experiments suggested online appeals were ineffective.

### 6.2 Centola 2010: adoption against the number of signals [read in full]

**Citation.** Centola, D. (2010). The spread of behavior in an online social network experiment. *Science* 329(5996), 1194-1197, doi 10.1126/science.1185231.

**Design [read].** An Internet health community of 1,528 participants recruited from health-interest websites, anonymous profiles, assigned at random to one node of either a clustered-lattice or a rewired random network with the same degree; "health buddies" were the neighbours; the behaviour was registering for a health forum known to nobody else; adoption by a neighbour triggered a message to the others; each diffusion ran three weeks. Six trials (N = 98, Z = 6; three of N = 128, Z = 6; two of N = 144, Z = 8), 12 diffusion processes. The main text does not give the years of data collection. (Validation note [derived]: one network size of 98, three of 128 and two of 144, with a clustered and a random network in each trial, is 2 × 770 = 1,540 places, not the 1,528 participants stated; the text was not re-opened, so this is unresolved.)

**Results [read].** Behaviour reached 53.77% of clustered networks and 38.26% of random ones; speed 0.2820 x 10^-3 nodes per second against 0.0643 x 10^-3 (more than four times); both differences P < 0.01 (rank-sum). Degree Z = 8 did better than Z = 6; no effect of population size over the range tested. Hazard of adoption: a second signal significantly raised the likelihood against one signal (P < 0.001, Cox model), a third raised it with a smaller marginal effect (P < 0.05), later signals had no significant effect (attributed to shrinking samples). Return visits: under 15% of adopters who had received one signal came back, over 30% of those with two, about 40% with three.

**Scale and relevance.** Crowd, adoption, in small anonymous networks (N about 100 to 144; no communication, no identity). The measured adoption function is a hazard that rises over the first two or three signals and flattens: the shape of a threshold gate with a threshold of about two, for a low-cost act. The paper expects higher thresholds for costly real-world behaviours. No upper gate appears.

### 6.3 González-Bailón et al. 2011: a measured threshold distribution [read]

**Citation.** González-Bailón, S., Borge-Holthoefer, J., Rivero, A., Moreno, Y. (2011). The dynamics of protest recruitment through an online network. *Scientific Reports* 1, 197, doi 10.1038/srep00197.

**Data [read].** Twitter around the Spanish protests of May 2011: 25 April to 25 May 2011, 87,569 users, 581,750 protest messages, with follower networks (also the reciprocated sub-network). A user counts as recruited at the first protest message. About 10% had posted before 15 May; about 98% by the end of the window.

**Results [read].** The threshold of a user, the share of followed accounts already active at recruitment (k_a / k_in), has an empirical distribution that is "roughly uniform for almost the full threshold interval" with two local maxima, at 0 (seeds) and at 0.5; early participants had lower thresholds. Susceptibility to recruitment bursts (a rising rate of activation among followees) precedes joining for the vast majority of users but not for the early low-threshold ones; moderate-threshold users sensitive to bursts are the critical mass. Cascades of messages are rare even in this exceptional episode; message spreaders sit in the high k-cores, but early participants have no characteristic network position.

**Scale, medium, relevance.** Crowd, mobilisation, measured by posting rather than street attendance (an assumption the authors state); online; era spring 2011. This is the nearest thing in the set to an empirical Granovetter threshold distribution for an online crowd, and it adds a variable v0.1's gate does not have: the rate of exposure, not only its count.

### 6.4 Sharing and expression as operant behaviour [read]

**Rathje, Van Bavel and van der Linden 2021** (PNAS 118(26), doi 10.1073/pnas.2024292118). 2,730,215 posts (stated total): Facebook and Twitter posts of liberal and conservative news outlets and of Democratic and Republican members of Congress; data retrieved during 2020, outlets mostly 2018 to 2020 and Congress 2016 to 2020. A regression on log-transformed share and retweet counts (the main text says "regression models" and names no estimator; "ordinary least squares" corrected at validation), on dictionary counts of in-group words, out-group words, negative, positive and moral-emotional words, mean-centred, with controls for retweet status, URL or media, and audience size. The coefficients are reported as exp(b), which on a log-transformed outcome is a multiplicative factor on the geometric mean of the counts per word rather than on their expectation [inf], and which the authors call an increase in the "odds". Out-group words (names of opposing politicians, identity terms): factors 1.29 to 1.57 per word in media accounts and 1.58 to 2.80 in Congress accounts; internal random-effects meta-analysis over eight datasets 1.67 per word (interval printed as 1.43 to 1.69, which is asymmetric on the log scale; a footnote's alternative estimator gives 1.40 to 2.00, so the 1.69 is probably a misprint [inf]; the paper prints other impossible or lopsided intervals, 2.32 [1.57, 2.47] for posts with any out-group against any in-group word and 1.18 [0.99, 0.42] for in-group words and "angry" reactions, noted at validation), in-group 1.05 (0.90 to 1.22, not significant), negative affect 1.14, moral-emotional 1.10, positive affect 0.95; the out-group effect was not moderated by political orientation or platform and was larger for Congress than for media (printed as P < 0.018, beside an interval of 0.91 to 1.58 that includes 1 and repeats the platform row's interval exactly, so the moderator statistics are inconsistent as printed; noted at validation). Out-group words predicted "angry" reactions (3.33 for liberal and 1.83 for conservative media accounts) and in-group words "love". The authors read this as identity expression: "motivated tweeting". *Standing:* observational, so subject to the placebo-dictionary critique recorded in 064 (Burton, Cruz and Hahn 2021 on the neighbouring moral-contagion result); the 4.8 times ratio to negative-affect language is correct as a ratio of excess odds, the figure caption's 4.7 is a rounding difference [checked]. *Scale:* crowd, adoption (sharing); online; no consent question (public posts).

**Brady, McLoughlin, Doan and Crockett 2021** (Sci. Adv. 7(33), doi 10.1126/sciadv.abe5641). Studies 1 and 2: 3,669 users who tweeted about the Kavanaugh hearings (October 2018) and 3,669 who tweeted about the United Airlines incident (April 2017), whose full tweet histories (6.1 and 6.6 million tweets, 12.7 million in all) were analysed, so the observational era runs back before the two seed events by an amount the main text does not give; the paper's total of 7,331 users is not 3,669 + 3,669 = 7,338 (a discrepancy in the source; both noted at validation); a classifier for moral outrage. Next-day outrage expression rises with the social feedback received for the previous day's outrage (b = 0.03 and 0.02; "a 2 to 3% increase" in outrage expression for a 100% increase in feedback), less so for users with long histories, and more with positive than negative prediction errors; users in more ideologically extreme networks express more outrage (b = 0.13 and 0.31) and depend less on feedback (b = -0.02 and -0.05). Studies 3 and 4 (N = 120 each, recruited on Prolific, all liberal because the stimuli were left-leaning) in a simulated feed: norm information drove the first choice (odds ratio 4.94 and 5.38 for outrage in an outrage-norm network) and feedback drove learning (odds ratio 1.10 per trial), with reinforcement weaker under an outrage norm. The authors say they measure the expression of outrage in text, not the emotion, and that platform design may decouple them. *Scale:* membership and crowd (the unit is a user's expressive habit); online; observational plus a simulated-feed experiment with consenting, paid participants.

## 7. Signs and tie formation: online fields, a computer-mediated lab, and face-to-face baselines

**Mosleh, Martel, Eckles and Rand 2021** [read]. PNAS 118(7), e2022761118, doi 10.1073/pnas.2022761118. Eight human-looking bot accounts varying party (Democrat or Republican) and strength of identification; 842 Twitter users who had retweeted MSNBC or Fox News content were randomly followed by one; planned 6,000 over 14 days but Twitter blocked the accounts after two days. Users were nearly three times more likely to follow back a copartisan bot (b = 0.093, interval 0.051 to 0.135; t(840) = 4.38); no interaction with bot strength; no partisan asymmetry (b = 0.012, interval -0.071 to 0.096); an exploratory three-way interaction suggests more partisan users follow back strong copartisan bots (p = 0.051). Waiver of informed consent (MIT board); preregistered. Era not given in the main text. *Scale:* membership (join, at the dyad level: tie formation); online.

**Takács, Flache and Mäs 2016** [read in part; Study 2 results read at validation]. PLoS ONE 11(6), e0157948, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0157948. Two experiments testing whether discrepancy with, or disliking of, a source produces opinion shifts away from it. Study 2 (read for design): Groningen, 100 students in sessions of ten, nine real-time computer-mediated interactions each, 850 interactions in all, paid 8 euros with a lottery; disliking induced by a study-subject mismatch, a Prisoner's Dilemma defection or a stigmatising message (manipulation check: attraction 56.57 against 60.22 in the control, t = 2.47). The abstract: similarity induces attraction; "a robust positive linear relationship between opinion distance and opinion shifts" and no negative influence from discrepancy or disliking. Study 1 showed "a small differentiation effect" at very small distances, combined with a positive linear effect of distance toward the source. Study 2 results (read at validation): negative shifts after the first stimulus were 7.37% of 380 control cases and 8.72% of 470 disliking cases, "no discernible difference" (t = −0.67); the linear distance coefficient is 0.64 (control) and 0.44 (disliking), both significant; in the control arm a quadratic distance term is also significant (−1.53 per 100 squared points), which the authors still read as "well approximated by the linear positive influence hypothesis"; in the disliking arm initial attraction has a negative main effect and a positive interaction with distance, and average shifts are positive in every attraction-by-distance cell. *Scale:* dyad; medium: real-time computer-mediated exchange in a lab, partners really present in the laboratory and never identified (corrected at validation from "believed present in the building").

**Face-to-face baselines.**
- *Pettigrew and Tropp 2006* [abs; headline numbers read in the same authors' 2008 restatement, Eur. J. Soc. Psychol. 38: 922-934]: 515 studies "involving a quarter of a million participants in 38 nations", studies through 2000; mean effect between contact and prejudice a correlation of .21 in the direction of less prejudice; 94% of studies inverse; more rigorous experimental studies yield a stronger mean r of .33; the mean effect rose every decade after 1970. Contact in the meta-analysis is mostly face to face and mostly survey-measured; the 2006 text is closed. The 2006 abstract, re-read at validation, adds: 713 independent samples; "the more rigorous studies yield larger mean effects"; contact under Allport's optimal conditions "typically leads to even greater reduction in prejudice", the conditions act "as an interrelated bundle", and they "are not essential for prejudice reduction" [abs]. The 2008 restatement was not re-opened at validation.
- *Paluck, Green and Green 2019* [read]: 27 randomised comparisons with delayed outcome measures, nearly two thirds published after the 2006 review; pooled Δ = 0.39 SD (95% interval 0.231 to 0.554); 24 of 27 positive; only 20 of 27 intervals overlap the pooled value; effects for ethnic and racial prejudice are "substantially weaker"; no study of ethnic or racial contact includes participants over 25; and a significant association between standard error and effect size (coefficient 2.086, p = 0.049) is reported in the funnel regression.
- *Enos 2014* [abs]: Spanish-speaking confederates were randomly inserted into the daily routines of unknowing Anglo-white commuters in homogeneous communities for days; exclusionary attitudes rose significantly. A face-to-face randomised exposure with a backfire sign.

**What these say about the sign-generator [inf].** (a) Whether a new tie forms is strongly identity-gated online, by a factor of about three, symmetrically across parties. (b) Whether opinion moves toward or away from a source is not a constant of the medium: toward the source in the computer-mediated lab (no negative influence), away in the Republican arm of Bail's month-long field design, and in both directions in face-to-face contact designs (average reduction of prejudice, but an RCT with a backfire sign and weak effects for racial and ethnic targets). (c) Allport's conditions (equal status, common goals, cooperation, institutional support; Allport 1954, as Paluck et al. 2019 cite him) are the obvious candidate for what separates the cases, and none of the online designs manipulated them. But the contact literature does not show that they separate the signs: in Pettigrew and Tropp's meta-analysis they enlarge the average reduction yet "are not essential" [abs], and Paluck et al. report that they "received relatively little empirical support" there and "have not been systematically investigated" in the randomised literature they review [read]. (Corrected at validation from "the condition that separates the cases".) So the data do not support "the medium flips the affiliation sign"; they support "the sign is a function of the relation between receiver and source and of the conditions of exposure, and the medium changes which conditions are usual".

## 9. What changes with group size and composition (collected)

- **Törnberg.** Defaults N = 196 (625 for pictures); "n" is the number of attributes. My checks: stationary ψ equal at N = 196 and N = 400 at γ = 0.1 and 0.2, convergence time growing faster than N (about 0.9, 5.9 and 16 thousand sweeps at N = 64, 196, 400) [repro], under the re-implementation's binomial replacement rule; under the published rule γ = 0.1 is local-only on the torus (section 2). Network structure matters more than N: a clique-dominated graph needs a larger γ. Composition: two groups, partisanship weighted four times an issue attribute.
- **Centola.** Networks of 98 to 144 anonymous participants, degree 6 or 8: higher degree helped; no effect of population size across the range; clustering beat random rewiring for a complex contagion with a threshold of about two signals.
- **Bond and Jones.** Of an average 149 friends, about 10 (80th percentile of interaction) or about 5 (90th percentile) are "close"; only these carry real-world voting; changing the definition changes the per-friend effect and the multiplier (4.7 against 2.0), so "per close friend" is not comparable across the two runs.
- **Huszár.** Definition of the unit changes the answer: party-level amplification differs by party, individual-level amplification does not, because audiences overlap.
- **Nyhan.** The median user has 50.4% like-minded and 14.7% cross-cutting exposures; 20.6% of users have over 75% like-minded; heavy users look similar. The intervention effects did not vary by ideology, sophistication, digital literacy or prior exposure (272 subgroup estimates, none significant).
- **Allcott et al. 2020 (2018 data).** Larger polarisation reductions for older people, white people and men; the sample is the 61% willing to be paid under $102. **Allcott et al. 2024 (2020 data).** Sample over-represents liberal and civically engaged users; weights applied.
- **Bail.** Republican and Democratic responses differ; the preprint's moderator search found none. **Mosleh.** No partisan asymmetry in follow-back. **Rathje.** The out-group effect is not moderated by orientation or platform, but is larger for Congress than for outlets (as printed; the moderator statistics are internally inconsistent, section 6.4). **Milli.** The in-group effect was not significant for left-leaning users.
- **Brady 2021.** Network ideological extremity moderates the response to feedback (b = -0.02 and -0.05); ego extremity does not. The two simulated-feed experiments recruited liberals only.
- **Lorenz-Spreen.** Context (established against emerging democracy) reverses the sign of some associations; US studies often do not state their conditionality.
- **Team scale.** Nothing read concerns n = 8 to 12 under digital mediation; v0.1's K1/N and enemy-row-sum terms are untouched.

## 11. Datedness and the fluid reading

- **A magnitude moved, in point estimate, within one platform in two years.** Bond to Jones: the direct effect on validated voting fell from 0.39 to 0.24 points and the indirect-to-direct ratio from 4.7 to 2.0 [derived], with a changed message system. The 2012 authors cite the Google Flu Trends failure as the reason such replications are needed. They also call the fall in the direct effect not statistically significant (section 6.1), so this bullet records point estimates, not a demonstrated decline.
- **A magnitude vanished in two years.** Allcott et al. 2020 (2018 data) to Allcott et al. 2024 (2020 data): a significant issue-polarisation effect (-0.10 SD) against a zero with an interval inside ±0.04 SD, same method, same platform family, different election.
- **The platform state is a hidden parameter that the studies set differently.** Bail asked participants to switch off the algorithmic timeline; Levy left it on and found the algorithm to be the largest force toward pro-attitudinal exposure, growing over the study; Nyhan and Guess et al. changed it by design in 2020; Huszár's control group is a 2016 holdback whose treatment "has not remained the same over time"; Milli audits Twitter's ranking as of February 2023, described in the paper by its April 2023 form. Anything in this file about "the algorithm" is therefore dated to a platform build.
- **The review itself drifts.** Lorenz-Spreen and colleagues report a slight trend toward more detrimental and more null findings over time (not quantified in the text I read) and a more beneficial picture in studies without open data or pre-registration (76% against 58% beneficial; 3 of 13 pre-registered associations beneficial). Part of the older beneficial picture may be a publication-design effect and not a period effect; the two cannot be separated from the text.
- **The opposite claim, stability, has support only for the form.** Complex contagion (Centola 2010, González-Bailón 2011) and the identity-gating of ties (Mosleh) are measured on platforms of 2008 to 2020 with the same qualitative shape (neither Centola's nor Mosleh's main text gives its data years, so both ends of that range are approximate; noted at validation).
- **Proposed validity record for a digital-mediation module (for v0.2).** Carry per fit: era; platform and build; feed state (chronological, engagement-ranked, stated-preference, none); source type (elite broadcast, outlet, peer, algorithm); dose and duration; compliance definition; population and selection (paid, volunteer, platform sample); outcome type (issue position, affect, behaviour, expression, exposure); consent or manipulation; whether re-tested and when. Treat the response coefficient as an editable input with slack and the exposure operator as a separate, dated input, in the soft-setting spirit of `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md`.
- **Reflexivity.** The platform changes the treatment in response to its own experiments (Huszár: changes to the ranking "depend on the experiment itself"), which is the reflexivity of 093 in a concrete form; no ε was measured.

## 13. What exists versus what does not

- **Exists.** A pre-registered field experiment with a backfire sign in one party (Bail); two further field designs on opposing-view exposure with a small affective gain (Levy) and a null (Nyhan); a family of platform deactivation and feed experiments with large exposure changes and attitude effects between zero and -0.16 SD, one of which failed to replicate its issue-polarisation result two years on; a direct audit of one platform's ranking (Huszár) and an identity of exposure into choice, ranking and usage (Levy); randomised mobilisation effects with a replication whose point estimates fell, the multiplier from about 4.7 to 2 (Bond, Jones; the authors call the fall in the direct effect not significant); a controlled test of the shape of the adoption gate (Centola) and a measured threshold distribution for an online crowd (González-Bailón); observational measures of the sharing gate and of operant expression (Rathje, Brady); an identified tie-formation sign online (Mosleh); a computer-mediated lab test finding no negative influence (Takács); a minimal model of sorting without negative influence or selective exposure (Törnberg), reproduced qualitatively here and, at validation, under its deposited code; a direction-coded review (Lorenz-Spreen).
- **Does not exist in what I could read.** A replication of Bail's bot design (not re-searched at validation: the shared search budget was spent); a within-design comparison of the same coupling face to face and online; any experiment varying Allport's conditions online (near miss added at validation and resolved by DOI: Santoro and Broockman 2022 randomised the topic of online cross-partisan conversations, a condition of contact but not one of Allport's four [abs]; section 14); any measurement of Törnberg's γ or of a ψ-like cross-issue alignment as an outcome; any data fit or held-out test of Törnberg's model; effect sizes in the review; any small-team (n = 8 to 12) cohesion, decision or split outcome under digital mediation; any estimate of the response coefficient that is stable across years; any consent-based version of the large exposure manipulations other than Levy's nudge; and any measure of a drive (as opposed to an expressed attitude, a behaviour or a rating).

## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

**Only in part, and only at the level of the individual's motive to express and to tie; the medium's own operator and the sign of the exposure effect do not reduce to drives on anything read here. "Drives as primitives" is the hypothesis under test; digital mediation is the clearest case so far of an environment-side operator that has to be a primitive in its own right.**

**What reduces to a person-level quantity.**
- **Identity-congruent expression and tie choice.** Sharing posts about the out-group at a factor of about 1.67 per word (Rathje) and following back a copartisan about three times as often (Mosleh) are readable as one affiliation-with-the-in-group motive acting through identity-congruent signals, a preference per person measured only by its behavioural consequence.
- **Approval-seeking.** Outrage expression rises with social feedback with small coefficients (Brady), a reward-learning term on expression that fits a social-approval motive; the authors insist they measure expression and not the emotion.
- **Influence homophily and threshold propensity.** Törnberg's h (how much more one is moved by the similar) and the thresholds of Centola and González-Bailón (how many signals before acting) are per-person parameters; the second has a measured distribution online, roughly uniform with modes at 0 and 0.5. Neither is derived from a drive; both are fitted or assumed.

**What does not reduce.**
- **The exposure operator.** Who sees what is set by subscription, ranking and usage (Levy's product), and the ranking optimises engagement, a revealed behaviour; Milli shows the engagement ranking surfaces political tweets users value less (-0.18 SD stated value). The thing the system acts on is a behavioural proxy, not a drive of persons or groups.
- **The sign of the opinion response.** Away in Bail's Republicans, toward in the computer-mediated lab, toward on average and away in some face-to-face designs. It follows the relation between receiver and source and the conditions of exposure, which no drive in the algebra currently supplies.
- **Sorting.** Törnberg's ψ is a property of the pair (influence homophily, nonlocal fraction) and the graph; no drive appears and none is needed for the mechanism to run. A group-level quantity is generated with no group drive and no person-level drive beyond a similarity weighting.
- **Magnitudes.** Every effect size is bound to a platform build, a year and a source type.
- **Group-level drives.** No cohesion, threat or morale quantity is measured in any source read.

**What would count as a test.** An online exposure experiment that holds the feed state fixed, varies the receiver-source identity relation and the Allport conditions (status, common goal, cooperation), and shows that a person-level identity-strength measure, obtained independently of the outcome, predicts the sign and size of the shift; and a controlled community in which the nonlocal fraction γ is varied and a cross-issue alignment index is the outcome. Neither exists in what I read.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**Predicts.** Directions of exposure change from a ranking change, and the form of crowd response to social signals; not attitude change from exposure change, and not magnitudes across years.

- **Exposure.** Reliable and by construction: the Nyhan re-rank moved like-minded exposure by 0.77 SD; Huszár's amplification ratios are measured, not predicted.
- **Attitudes from exposure.** Poor out of sample. The echo-chamber logic (cut like-minded exposure, reduce polarisation) was tested in 2020 by Nyhan (±0.12 SD ruled out) and, at abstract level, by the chronological-feed experiment, and failed. The contact logic (cross-party exposure depolarises) was Bail's preregistered H1 and failed there; the backfire logic (H2, H3) held in one party. Levy's small affective gain (-0.03 SD ITT, counter against pro-attitudinal) is the only positive result; each arm against the control group has Lee bounds that include zero because of differential attrition. Deactivation gave -0.16 SD on an index in 2018 and about zero in 2020.
- **Mobilisation and adoption.** The sign of social-cue effects replicated (Bond to Jones: both positive, 2012 interval just excluding zero); magnitudes fell in point estimate (direct 0.39 to 0.24 points, a fall the authors call not significant; multiplier 4.7 to 2.0, untested and across a change in the definition of a close friend). The shape of the adoption gate is stable across a controlled online forum (published 2010), a 2011 protest and platform experiments (2010 to 2012). Thresholds are measured (González-Bailón), not predicted.
- **Sorting.** The model's prediction of a phase transition in the nonlocal fraction is untested against data; it is reproduced only in simulation. The cross-country trend data do not show that online news share tracks affective polarisation (twelve countries).
- **The review.** Predicts the direction of association for a given outcome and political setting; no calibration, no effect sizes.

**Controls.** Only the environment and, weakly, the crowd.

- **What steers.** Ranking rules steer exposure strongly (0.77 SD; up to 400% amplification); social cues in a message steer a costly real-world act by 0.24 to 0.39 points and spread through close friends; feedback structure steers expressive habits in a simulated feed (N = 240); a visible, declinable offer to follow counter-attitudinal outlets steers affect by 0.03 to 0.06 SD.
- **What did not steer.** Attitudes in 2020 across four feed and access manipulations, with intervals excluding anything above 0.04 to 0.12 SD over three months.
- **Consent against manipulation.** Levy's nudge is the consent-based lever (about half accepted). The Meta designs had consent to take part but not to the details of the feed change; the Twitter holdback, the Bond message and the Mosleh bots had no individual consent. None works by appeal to a drive; each changes what people encounter.
- **Harm.** The largest attitudinal estimate among the field designs read here is Bail's, and it runs against the depolarising hypothesis that motivated the design (+0.12 points ITT, +0.60 for full compliers, Republicans); engagement ranking selects political content that users rate lower than they would choose (Milli).

**Failures and limits.** Replication drift inside one platform (Bond to Jones, in point estimates only; Allcott et al. 2020 to 2024, 2018 to 2020 data). The platform build is an unrecorded treatment arm in most designs. Compliance and selection are heavy (paid frequent users in Bail; the 61% willing to be paid under $102 in Allcott; a self-selecting 28,296 eligible in Nyhan). The Twitter holdback violates no-interference and is read only as a comparison. Observational diffusion regressions (Rathje; Brady's first two studies) are confounded and the neighbouring moral-contagion result failed a placebo test (064). The review cannot give magnitudes, and its beneficial findings come disproportionately from studies without open data, which the authors attribute to survey-based participation studies. No source tests anything at team scale, and no replication of the strongest sign result (Bail) was found.
