## Summary

1. **The seed needs one correction before anything else.** "Woolley on political bots" is Samuel C. Woolley (computational propaganda), not Anita Williams Woolley, whose collective-intelligence factor is read in file 092. Samuel Woolley's First Monday paper (2016) is a content analysis of 41 news articles about bot use in up to 18 countries (cases 2011-2014); it measures no effect and gives a typology of three things deployers do: demobilise by flooding the opponents' hashtags, broadcast propaganda, pad follower counts [read in full]. His 2017 working paper with Guilbeault measures the network position of accounts a classifier calls bots in 17 million tweets from 1-11 November 2016 and says in its own words that downstream behaviour is "the task of future studies" [read]. Park et al. 2023 contains no human member and no operator inventory (file 12, re-read here).
2. **"Bot" is a classifier output with large instrument error, and every magnitude about bots inherits it.** The reported bot share of accounts runs from 6% (Shao 2018, Botometer at 0.5) through about 15% (Bessi and Ferrara 2016) and about one in three (Stella 2018) to 47-48% of 93,499 accounts (Li et al. 2024, combined detector at 0.65) and 55% of a protest core (Li et al. 2026, preprint). Rauchfleisch and Kaiser (2020) find Botometer's ROC-AUC 0.85 on their pooled data against 0.94 in its creators' papers, and at a 15% base rate and threshold 0.76 only 59% of accounts flagged are bots (24% on German politicians), with 27% of one bot set crossing the threshold on some day within three months [read]. Section 3.1.
3. **Exposure and position are large; measured effects on attitudes are near zero wherever they were tested, and the effects on sentiment and cohesion are observational.** Russian IRA accounts: Bail et al. (1,239 users, 44 newly treated, six outcomes) and Eady et al. (1,496 respondents, 18 outcomes, 0.05-0.06 SD point estimates, effects above 0.2 SD rejected for 17 of 18) both find no meaningful effect, while 1% of users took 70% of exposures [read]. Bots are 6% of accounts but 31% of low-credibility tweets in Shao et al., and humans retweet bot-posted articles almost as much as human-posted ones [read]. Li et al. (Extinction Rebellion, 2019) find bot encounters lower later sentiment and leave engagement unchanged [read]; Li et al. 2026 (BLM, 2020, preprint) find bot exposure predicts later loss of human cohesion [read]. None is an experiment.
4. **An edge's sign cannot be read off an event type, and bots make this vivid.** Tsvetkova et al. counted 105 bot-on-bot reverts per English Wikipedia bot over ten years against 3 for humans [read]; Geiger and Halfaker found 45% of English bot-bot article reverts were double-redirect fixes and 49% interwiki-link work, 1.07% unclassified and 1.39% identified bot fights, and that "conflict" was overwhelmingly routine or collaborative [read, arXiv copy of the published text]. Wischnewski et al. find disagreeing accounts are rated more bot-like (N = 151) [abs]. Sign is semantic and can run from sign to perceived kind.
5. **Gates are where non-human members act.** "Manufactured consensus" is, in the algebra's own terms, a shift of every threshold by one affine map: with h humans and b always-joined non-human members, member i joins iff (J/h) >= (theta_i - beta)/(1 - beta), beta = b/(h + b) [derived, checked]. Granovetter's second crowd goes from equilibrium 1 to 100 with one always-joined member [derived, checked]. How many are needed is model-bound: humans 25% (Centola 2018, N = 20-30, file 091), a model benchmark 10% (Xie et al. [abs]), LLM populations 2% to 67% depending on the model and which convention is strong (Ashery et al. 2025, N = 24 or 48) [read]. Admission gates move too: bot friend requests were accepted at up to 80% as mutual friends rose (Boshmaf et al. 2011) [read].
6. **LLM members differ from scripted bots by being reactive, and the evidence on what that does is fresh, small and mostly on strangers.** Rephrasing suggestions lifted the partner's rated conversation quality by 4 points and democratic reciprocity by 2.5 points (Argyle et al., 1,574 people, 2022) with attitudes unchanged [read]. A GPT-4o facilitator raised facts shared by 2.9 against no facilitation (d = 0.61) in a trial of 281 groups of five (70 or 71 per condition) but not the decision (23% against 31% optimal, n.s.) [read]. Personalised GPT-4 raised the odds of agreement by 81.2% in debates (N = 900) [read]. Human-AI combinations in 106 experiments did worse than the better of either alone (g = -0.23) [read]. The one study varying the proportion of agents in a 24-seat session (Chen et al., arXiv September 2026) finds consensus +8.0% at 12.5% agents, -23.1% at 33.3%, -14.5% at 50% and back to 0.725 at 75%, but each proportion is a single session [read, inferred from the participant counts].
7. **Composition effects are not additive in the dyad.** Chen et al. say the effects "are difficult to predict by studying individual human-agent exchanges in isolation" [read]; Flint et al. 2026 find the group size at which an LLM population becomes deterministic runs from N = 2 to about 10^4 depending on model and word pair [read]. Same lesson as the fish groups in file 110: what explains a pair does not explain a group.
8. **The kind label is a gate, and its direction depends on the task.** Purported bots drew about 10 points less cooperation (Ishowo-Oloko et al., N = 698) [read]; perceived AI use lowered rated cooperation and affiliation while actual use raised them (Hohenstein et al.) [read]; perceived AI identity cut willingness to adopt an expression by 0.463 points on a 5-point scale, and the gap reversed at 75% agents (Chen et al.) [read]; believing the opponent was an AI went with larger concessions in debate, correlationally (Salvi et al.) [read]. Whether the label can gate at all depends on detection, which ran at 50-52% (Jakesch et al., text from a fine-tuned GPT-2 and a fine-tuned 13B GPT-3) and about 75% for GPT-4 debates (Salvi et al., 2023-24) [read].
9. **Rankers are operators on exposure, not nodes.** Twitter's personalised timeline amplified mainstream-right politicians' reach more in six of seven countries (UK: Labour 112% against Conservatives 176%) [read]; neutral "drifter" bots show the first account followed fixes much of the later ecosystem [read]; an LLM feed reranker moved out-party warmth by about 2 degrees in 10 days, symmetric in both directions, with consenting users blind to condition (Piccardi et al., arXiv v2) [read]; the bridging matrix-factorisation rule that decides Community Notes labels is an explicit signed-agreement operator, r_hat = mu + i_u + i_n + f_u . f_n, with the label "helpful" at note intercept >= 0.40 [read].
10. **Populations of LLM agents are not drop-in stand-ins for persons.** Replaying 100 human Wason groups with LLM agents overstated consensus by 34 to 44 points in the author's headline estimates (sensitivity analyses adopted after unblinding; the preregistered all-groups gaps were 62 and 72 points) and did not track accuracy (T. Shao 2026, preprint) [read in part]; models agree about 60% of the time when both err against a chance level of one third (Kim et al. 2025) [abs]; the agents-only Moltbook network has reciprocity 0.197 and under-represented triads, unlike human networks [abs]. For the testbed they are non-human nodes, not proxies.
11. **In the algebra, a non-human member is a node with a row and a column in the gain matrix, a policy with a principal, and a kind that other members perceive (or do not).** Models/01 Corollary 3.4 already settles the certificate: a member that reads nothing (a pure broadcaster, row zero) can never break it; one that reads and writes is certified iff c_{p.} (I - Gamma)^{-1} c_{.p} < 1, which for a uniform group of n is c.d < (1 - rho)/n, so the tolerable loop gain falls as 1/n [derived, checked on 3,000 draws]. Stubborn members are zealots: the long-run mean is a hitting-probability average of their opinions, and uniform random placement gives n z1/(z0 + z1) on any connected graph [read: Acemoglu et al., Vendeville et al.]. A designed agent can carry a drive-like state of exactly the D1 form (Crandall et al.'s S++ aspiration) [read, derived]. Whether this makes non-humans a typed Person or a separate sort is a design decision not yet stated; options and evidence are in 7.9, flagged for Aldred.
12. **Era.** Nothing here predates 2011, and the LLM half (2022-26) moves in months: model capacity alone moved consensus from 0.534 to 0.725 at the same 33.3% agent share (Chen et al.), the Wikipedia bot "wars" ended with Wikidata in 2013 on the authors' own account, Botometer scores drift over three months, and two errata (2024, 2026) touch the Meta 2020 feed experiment. Forms (zealot theorems, the certificate, the threshold shift) are era-invariant given their hypotheses; every magnitude here is a per-version record.

### 1.3 Two cautions about the category

- **Non-human is not unowned.** Every type has a principal who sets the policy: a state office (King's archive of 2013-2014 emails from one district propaganda office), an agency (IRA), a campaign (Woolley), a deployer (Park's "inner voice"; "my human" in 9.4% of Moltbook messages [abs, Holtz]), a platform (rankers), a researcher (T8). The primitive is the policy-with-principal, not an agent with drives; section 7.
- **"Bot" is itself an estimate.** Membership of T1 and T2 is a classifier verdict (3.1), and T9 looks like T1 to a classifier. The sort's membership is therefore an input with an error rate, not a fact, which matters for any rule that conditions on kind.

## 2. The seeds

### 2.1 Park et al. 2023, re-read for this direction [read in part]

Full citation in section 0. File 12 has the architecture (observe, retrieve, reflect, plan, act) and the verdict that no operator inventory exists; not repeated. What bears on D607:

- **No human is a member of the town.** A human enters through an interface: a persona the agent is told to perceive them as, or the agent's "inner voice", which "makes the agent more likely to treat the statement as a directive" (section 3.1.2). In the algebra's terms the human's influence on an agent is set by a label in the prompt, and the "directive" label bypasses the agent's own gate [read].
- **Results, 25 agents, two game days, model gpt3.5-turbo (2023)** [read]: knowledge of one agent's mayoral run rose from 1 agent (4%) to 8 (32%); knowledge of a party from 1 (4%) to 13 (52%); network density eta = 2|E| / (|V|(|V| - 1)) rose from 0.167 to 0.74; 6 of 453 awareness responses (1.3%) were hallucinated; five of the twelve invited agents attended. Scale: crowd (adoption) inside an agent-only world. No consent question arises.
- **A sign bias.** The authors report that instruction tuning made the agents "more polite and cooperative overall" and "overly cooperative with one another": the host agent "rarely said no" to ideas that did not fit her character [read]. Sycophancy measured later across 11 models (Cheng et al., 3.6 below) is the same tendency at scale.
- **Standing.** A demonstration with believability judged by raters; no human-group data. It is the opposing design to an algebra (file 12). It enters D607 only as the prior art that LLM agents spread information and form ties among themselves at a rate that looks social, with a positive-sign default.

### 2.2 Woolley on political bots: verified, and what it is

**Identity.** The seed means Samuel C. Woolley, then at the University of Washington and the Oxford Internet Institute (Computational Propaganda Project); not Anita Williams Woolley (Science 2010, the c factor, file 092). Both appear in this strand; do not merge them.

**Woolley (2016), First Monday 21(4)** [read in full]. *Vocabulary:* social bot, political bot, computational propaganda, Twitter bomb, persona management, sockpuppet. *Method:* qualitative content analysis of news reports. LexisNexis and three search engines gave 58 articles from 44 sources, coded for credibility 1 to 3; 41 articles (credibility 1 or 2) form the dataset; the unit of analysis is the bot campaign, the unit of observation the news report; one coder; "purposively, as opposed to randomly" chosen; the paper "can only say that there is or is not evidence of a particular finding in the sources" [read]. Table 1 lists 18 entries, one of them Tibet listed apart from China (17 in Table 2), cases 2011-2014, with the Polity IV score of each. *Findings (typology of what deployers do)* [read]:

1. **Demobilise.** "Twitter bombs": barrages of bot tweets that co-opt the tags the opponent's supporters use to organise, so that searching the tag no longer finds the organisers. Example from the paper: Krebs on #tibet and #freetibet "inundated with junk tweets". Cases: Bahrain, Syria, Iran, Morocco, Azerbaijan, Mexico, Turkey, China and others.
2. **Broadcast propaganda.** Pro-government or pro-candidate messages; South Korea's intelligence service alleged by prosecutors to have posted more than 1.2 million messages in 2012; Mexico's "Penabots".
3. **Pad follower counts.** Politicians buying bot followers to look more popular; the paper reports a candidate gaining nearly 117,000 followers in 24 hours (about 17%) and notes that his supporters blamed detractors, so attribution is contested.
- *Pattern claimed:* countries with a longer democratic history use bots only for follower padding; more authoritarian ones also demobilise and broadcast. Eighteen purposively coded cases; no test.

*What it does not do:* measure any effect on any human. It is a typology of operators.

**Woolley and Guilbeault (2017), COMPROP 2017.5** [read in part]. Part A is nine months of campaign fieldwork with interviews (found by keyword only, not read in full); it names two mechanisms: "manufacturing consensus" and the democratisation of propaganda. Part B, read:
- **Data.** About 17 million unique tweets, 1,798,127 users, 1-11 November 2016, Twitter streaming API (a 1% sample) filtered by hashtags valenced toward either candidate. Accounts classified with BotOrNot; score above 0.5 counted as bot. 157,504 accounts could be classified (the rest were private or deleted); 15,805 (over 10%) were bots.
- **Position.** In the largest connected component of reciprocal retweets (38,306 users, 102,623 edges), 3,517 potential bots (roughly 10%); nearly a third of the bots sat in k-cores of degree at least 10; 204 bots had above-average betweenness. The largest Trump-hashtag botnet had 944 bots against 264 for Clinton; global transitivity 0.01 (random networks 0.004) against 0.009.
- **Caveats the authors state:** the classifier can only score accounts that still exist, bots are the accounts most likely to be deleted, and BotOrNot "often categorizes organizational accounts, like @BarackObama, as bot accounts". Their conclusion that bots "did affect the flow of information" rests on centrality, and they leave "downstream consequences ... on actual on-the-ground political behaviour" to future studies.
- *Era:* 2016; *medium:* Twitter; *scale:* crowd (opinion), but only positions were measured; *consent:* not an intervention.
- **Translation.** A high-betweenness non-human node is a large column entry c_{.p} to many members; it says nothing about the size of the gain gamma_ip. The "megaphone effect" is a statement about out-degree, not about response.

**Howard and Kollanyi (2016), arXiv:1606.06356** [abs]. Brexit referendum conversation; bots have "a small but strategic role"; less than 1% of sampled accounts generated almost a third of all messages; the hashtag family for leaving dominated and different perspectives used different levels of automation. Body not read.

### 3.1 Prevalence, and how much of it is the instrument

**Reading [derived].** Volume per bot account relative to a human account is 31/6 = 5.2 in Shao, 19/15 = 1.3 in Bessi and Ferrara and 23.6/33 = 0.7 in Stella (using "nearly one in three" as 33%). There is no stable multiplier, and the bot share itself runs from 6% to 55% depending on event, detector and cut-off. Different detectors measure different things.

**Rauchfleisch and Kaiser (2020)** [read, main text]. *Design:* daily Botometer scores for 3 March to 2 June 2019 (about three months) on five account sets, 4,134 accounts: German politicians (516 human), US politicians (502 human), 928 new English-language bots, 27 German bots, and the labelled set of Varol et al. (699 bots, 1,462 humans). *Findings:* pooled ROC-AUC 0.85 (0.94 in the tool's creators' papers); 0.93 on US politicians against bots, 0.86 on the Varol set, 0.76 on German accounts; the English score is worse on German accounts. Precision-recall at a stated 15% bot base rate: with the universal score at 0.76, 59% of flagged accounts are bots and 41% human, and about 80% of bots are missed; on German politicians and bots only 24% of the flagged are bots; with the CAP score at 0.25, 55% precision and 29% recall, German 30% and 18%. Over three months, with the threshold at 0.76, 27.2% of the new bots, 22.2% of the German bots and 13.9% of the Varol bots score above and below it on some day; for humans the figures are 7.4%, 3.1% and 0.6%. *Author's conclusion:* most social-science studies using the tool "will unknowingly count a high number of human users as bots and vice versa." *Scale:* measurement validity. *Standing:* one team's validation on five sets; the Bessi and Ferrara paper quotes the tool's accuracy "above 95 percent" from its creators' training evaluation, so the out-of-sample figure is lower than the one the field cited.

**What this does to the rest of section 3.** Absolute shares (6%, 15%, 48%, 55%) are exposed to precision and recall errors of this size. Contrasts inside one study (super-spreaders against a random sample in Shao) are less exposed, but only if the detector's errors are unrelated to account activity; Shao says bot scores are uncorrelated with activity volume and shows robustness to thresholds in the SI, which I did not read. Direction-of-effect claims survive better than magnitudes. The platform's own labels (the IRA lists in Bail and Eady) avoid the classifier but swap in a different problem: the accounts are operated by agency staff (trolls), and whether any were automated is not stated in the texts I read.

### 3.2 Reach and response: what is measured of coupling

**Shao et al. 2018** [read, main text]. *Citation/DOI:* section 0. *Vocabulary:* low-credibility sources, super-spreaders, social bots, dismantling. *Design:* 389,569 articles from 120 low-credibility sources and 15,053 from seven fact-checkers (Hoaxy), 13,617,425 and 1,133,674 tweets linking to them, collected from the public streaming API. *Results:* (i) bots are over-represented among accounts sharing an article in its first seconds (60,000 accounts in the 1,000 most viral stories; first hour); (ii) accounts with higher bot scores mention accounts with more followers; (iii) humans retweet articles posted by likely bots "almost as much" as those posted by humans, and human tweet volume scales super-linearly with bot tweet volume for low-credibility articles but not for fact-checking ones; (iv) in a 227,363-node retweet network, removing nodes in descending bot-score order is the second-best way to cut low-credibility spread, after removing the most influential accounts, and the authors say it can be "virtually eliminated" by disconnecting a small percentage of likely bots. *Authors' limits:* US sources and the 2016-17 period; the design is observational [inf]; and the bots are deceptive: "none of the ten Twitter accounts most active at retweeting articles in the core of the misinformation network" identified themselves as bots (corrected in validation: an earlier version read this as not being identified as a bot, which reverses the point). *Era/medium/scale/consent:* 2016-17; Twitter; crowd (adoption); observational. *Translation:* the human response to bot-posted content is close to the response to human-posted content (kind-blind coupling at the retweet margin), and bots choose the targets of their column (hubs) and the timing (the first seconds).

**Vosoughi, Roy, Aral 2018** [abs]. About 126,000 stories, about 3 million people and 4.5 million tweets, 2006-2017: falsehood diffused farther and faster than truth, and "robots accelerated the spread of true and false news at the same rate". *Cross-check against Shao.* They are not in conflict as stated: Vosoughi's claim concerns the difference between true and false stories among fact-checked items; Shao's concerns the level of low-credibility spread and who carries it, and Shao notes (in the text I read) that it covers articles that were not fact-checked and counts bot resharing of human-posted links. Both can hold if bots add reach to everything. Not resolved: I read only Shao's side.

**Stella, Ferrara, De Domenico 2018** [read, main text]. *Setting:* the 2017 Catalan independence referendum, tweets with seven referendum hashtags, about 3.6 million tweets by 523,000 users, 22 September to 3 October 2017. *Findings:* 19% of all interactions run from bots to humans (74% retweets, 25% mentions); humans are 1.8 times more central (PageRank) than bots; bots direct their activity at human hubs (Kendall tau about 0.62 between a human's in-degree from bots and from humans; about 0.75 for humans interacting with the most connected bots), and the correlations vanish in degree-preserving randomisations; the sentiment of bot-to-human endorsements matches the sentiment of human-to-human endorsements within each of two stance groups (negative in group 1, positive in group 2); bot-to-bot sentiment does not fall when human sentiment falls on the referendum day; human coding of 2,413 tweets agrees. *Interpretation (authors):* bots promote human-generated content from hubs and add violent associations only in messages aimed at the independence group. *Standing:* observational; "may influence", no effect estimate. *Translation:* the measured behaviour is a mixture: reactive in what it picks up (hub content, target polarity), non-reactive in its own state (bot-to-bot sentiment unmoved), a T1/T2 hybrid. Era 2017; Twitter; crowd (opinion); observational.

**Li, Vasarhelyi, Vedres 2024** [read, main text]. Extinction Rebellion protests, 93,499 X users, November to December 2019. Findings: bots predicted human activity in 3 of 4 identified cascades and human sentiment predicted bot sentiment (vector error-correction models); in a matched comparison of 303 users who replied to or commented on a bot's post against matched users who did not (N added in validation), there is a consistent negative effect on later sentiment, larger for those favourable or neutral to the movement; astroturfing bots raise the human activity that follows, other bots lower it; "bot encounters do not change activists' engagement levels" (abstract); individual effects small, "the cumulative effect is profound due to the large volume of bot communication" (authors' claim). Observational, matched. *Scale:* crowd (opinion, mobilisation as posting activity). *Translation:* a small per-encounter coupling on sentiment with sign set by the user's prior stance, i.e. the gate depends on the receiver's position, as in Bail's out-party result (file 114).

**Li, Vasarhelyi, Vedres 2026, arXiv:2609.00197** [read in part; preprint, not peer reviewed]. BLM protests after George Floyd's death, peak 7 to 8 June 2020, post-peak window from 1 to 2 September 2020. Retweet-network core of 92,078 users (55% automated-like). Outcome: change in human-only cohesion, at ego scale (local clustering among a user's human contacts; 3,291 egos) and community scale (human-human edge density in 677 clique-percolation communities, 542 traceable afterwards). Result: greater bot exposure during the peak (alter-bot edges for egos; bot-human density for communities) predicts steeper later declines at both scales, concentrated among supporters; the intercepts say that without bot interaction clustering rose and community density did not change. The abstract's reading: bots "may weaken activism less by changing what people believe than by dissolving the ties". Cross-sectional variation in exposure, OLS with controls; the text I read has no causal identification and says exposure of alters rather than ego's choice avoids self-selection. *Scale:* group (cohesion) inside a crowd. *Standing:* the only result in this file about the strand's own outcome (do human ties hold), observational, one case, one preprint, a detector at 0.65 to 0.75. *Translation:* a candidate non-zero edge from non-human exposure to tie dissolution; no gain estimated.

**Bail et al. 2020 and Eady et al. 2023 (the IRA)** [read, main text]. The accounts are the Internet Research Agency's "troll" accounts, run by agency staff (whether any were automated is not stated in the texts I read), so these are T9 results, included because platforms and analysts see accounts, not kinds.
- *Bail et al.:* 1,239 Republican and Democratic Twitter users (YouGov panel; Twitter at least three times a week) surveyed in October and November 2017, linked to Twitter's non-public list of 4,256 IRA accounts. 19.0% interacted with an IRA account, 11.3% directly; 3.7% newly in the month between waves; the main model has 44 treated and 1,106 control. Bayesian causal forest: "no evidence that interaction with IRA accounts substantially impacted" six outcomes (out-party feeling thermometer, social-distance scale, 7-point ideology, a 10-item ideology index, and two behaviours read from the respondents' accounts: the number of political accounts followed and the share of them that shared the respondent's party). Interaction was most common among those with homophilous networks, high interest and heavy use. Limits stated: one month, binary treatment, one platform, partisans only, Twitter had already suspended many accounts early in 2017, observational.
- *Eady et al.:* 1,496 respondents with linked Twitter timelines, April to November 2016 (survey waves bracketing the campaign). 70% (1,042) saw at least one post from a foreign influence account; 786,634 such posts; only 1% of users accounted for 70% of exposures; exposure concentrated among strong Republicans; mostly incidental, via retweets by ordinary accounts (75-80% by election day); about 4 Russian posts a day against 106 from national news media and 35 from politicians (25 and 9 times as many). No relationship with issue positions, perceived polarisation or vote: point estimates 0.05 SD (issues) and 0.06 SD (polarisation), and for all but one of 18 outcomes effects above 0.2 SD are rejected (TOST). The authors stress the data are observational.
- *Together:* two samples (2016, 2017), two designs, concordant null on attitudes; consistent with a coupling near zero for that source and that audience, with the usual limits (partisans on Twitter; the 2016 election itself is not tested in Bail).

**Stukal, Sanovich, Bonneau, Tucker 2022** [read: abstract, data, selected results]. About 32 million Russian-language tweets on Russian politics, 2015-2018, by about 1.4 million users; the authors identify bots with their own classification (method not read here, since they note general-purpose tools such as Botometer cannot score historical accounts). Two rival theories: bots demobilise offline protest, or bots do online agenda control. Result: online opposition activity produces stronger bot reactions than offline protest, "around twice as large"; the bot-level effect is of "limited magnitude" (one reported estimate is about half a tweet per bot on the days studied) and the authors caution that the effect is the concerted activity of many bots, not one bot's contribution. *Translation:* a T2 reaction function: the bot's row reads the opposition's online activity volume, its column writes to the shared agenda; a measured c_{p.} on a volume variable, not on any member's state. Observational; the authors call it a lower bound.

**King, Pan, Roberts 2017** [abstract, data and content passages read]. A leaked archive of 2013-2014 emails to and from one district Internet Propaganda Office in Jiangxi gave 43,757 known 50c posts, used to train identification of the rest; the authors estimate about 448 million such posts a year. Content: "cheerleading" and positive discussion of valence issues, not argument with sceptics; the posts come in volume bursts around events; the stated objective is "to distract and redirect public attention from discussions or events with collective action potential". These are people, not software (T9); included because the operator is portable to bots. Era 2013-14; medium Chinese government websites and commercial social media (of the archive's posts with a known site, 53% were comments on government sites, and of those on commercial sites 54% were on Sina Weibo); crowd (attention); covert, manipulation.

**Suarez-Serrato, Roberts, Davis, Menczer 2016** [read]. Mexico's #YaMeCanse protest hashtag (7 November 2014 after the Ayotzinapa press conference; over 2 million tweets in the following month, about 4.4 million in all, peak 500,000 posts on 21 November). The authors report the claim that the hashtag was flooded to make it harder for humans to communicate and that human users responded by iterating hashtags up to #YaMeCanse25, with the alleged spammers following; their BotOrNot classification (AUC 94% as they cite) is described as preliminary and supports a bot role in "disrupting online communication". *Translation:* the jamming operator and a human counter-operator (re-gating the channel) in one case; preliminary.

**Li and Margetts 2026** [abs]. Content analysis of bot activity in BLM and XR: pro- and counter-protest bots deploy different strategies across visibility, resonance and legitimacy; counter-bots in BLM target legitimacy, in XR downplay salience. The full text was behind a bot challenge and was not opened. A web-search summary said the article notes that no empirical study measures whether bots change actual participation in online activism [sa, unchecked]; I found none either (section 12).

### 3.3 Gates: where the bot acts on the human's decision rule

- **Admission gates.** *Boshmaf, Muslukhov, Beznosov, Ripeanu 2011* [read, results]: a network of 102 socialbots and one botmaster on Facebook (then 750 million users) for about eight weeks in 2011; 8,570 friend requests, 3,055 accepted (35.6% [derived]); in the later phase 3,517 requests to friends of friends, 1,921 accepted (54.6%), with acceptance rising "up to 80%" with the number of mutual friends, which the authors read as triadic closure; the network generated about 250 GB of inbound traffic in total. Approved by the authors' behavioural research ethics board; the infiltrated users were not asked. *Scale:* membership (join). *Translation:* a monotone acceptance gate in the count of shared ties, exploitable by a node with no tie history. 2011 Facebook; the platform changed its friend-request surfaces since (not tested here).
- **Perceived-share gates (manufactured consensus).** Woolley and Guilbeault's mechanism, formalised in 7.4. Evidence that counts move people: *Muchnik, Aral, Taylor 2013* [abs]: in a randomised experiment on a social news site, one early positive vote raised the likelihood of a positive rating by 32% and final ratings by 25% on average, negative votes were corrected by later raters (asymmetric herding), and the effect depended on topic and on whether the votes came from friends or enemies. This is the nearest controlled analogue of one always-joined non-human member. Salganik et al. (file 110) is the read counterpart for counts on songs.
- **Critical-mass gates.** Humans: a committed minority of undisclosed confederates flipped a convention at about 25% (Centola et al. 2018, N = 20-30 per group, file 091). Model: about 10% (Xie et al. 2011 [abs]). LLM populations: 2% to 67% (section 6). The shared structure is a threshold; the number is not shared.
- **Jamming gates.** Woolley 2016's demobilisation (Twitter bombs on opponents' tags), Suarez-Serrato's #YaMeCanse, and Stukal's agenda control act on the capacity of the opponents' coordination channel, not on any member's threshold: in gain-matrix terms they multiply the gains among the targets by a factor below one.
- **Dilution.** King et al.'s flooding with positive off-topic posts around collective-action events acts on the attention share of the contested topic.
- **Counter-gates by humans.** Channel-hopping (Suarez-Serrato), perceived-bot labelling of those who disagree (Wischnewski, below), and the label penalty (section 4.4).

### 3.4 Signs: an event type is not a sign

**Tsvetkova, Garcia-Gavilanes, Floridi, Yasseri 2017** [read, main text]. *Vocabulary:* bots, reverts as disagreement, interaction trajectory. *Data:* all edits in 13 language editions of Wikipedia from 2001 to 2010, the encyclopaedia's first ten years (the "first generation" of bots), 1,549 bots linked to their human owners out of 6,627 suspected accounts. *Measures (restated):* for a pair (i, j) the trajectory y_t steps +1 when i reverts j and -1 when j reverts i; latency is the mean log time between successive reverts; imbalance is |r_i - r_j| / (r_i + r_j); reciprocity is the number of turning points over (r_i + r_j - 1). *Results:* bots on English Wikipedia reverted another bot 105 times on average over ten years, humans 3 (German 24, Portuguese 185, a difference that disappears when edits are accounted for); a smaller share of bots' edits are reverts than of humans'; bot-bot interactions are slower (characteristic latency about one month against 2 minutes, 24 hours or 1 year for humans) and more reciprocal; k-means gives four trajectory types although the silhouette method says the data do not cluster well. *Authors' reading:* "even good bots fight". *Authors' own limits:* the period ended around 2013 when Wikidata took over inter-language links, so "we believe that the conflict we observed on Wikipedia no longer occurs today"; the Wikipedia ecosystem is "gated and monitored", unlike malevolent social bots, which are often coordinated. Era 2001-2010; online encyclopaedia; scale: group (efficiency) among non-humans.

**Geiger and Halfaker 2017** [read in part, arXiv copy of the published text]. Replicates and "extends and refutes" Tsvetkova with trace ethnography: reading what each revert did, the bots' approval requests and talk pages. *Result for English Wikipedia articles:* double-redirect fixing accounts for 45% of all bot-bot reverts, interwiki-link work for 49% and 1.07% were unclassified; about 39% of bot-bot reverts fall outside the article namespace; their abstract: "overwhelmingly, the interactions previously characterized as problematic instances of conflict are typically better characterized as routine, productive, even collaborative work", with genuine conflict "rare but still" present. *Verdict (cross-check):* the claim that revert counts show bots disagreeing is refuted for that operationalisation. Genuine conflict is quantified: the bot fights they identified were 1.39% of English bot-bot article reverts; across languages 3.5% of bot-bot article reverts were unclassified, classified as bot fights or had "revert" in the edit summary; and in every case with some sign of conflict that they examined, Wikipedians had already identified and resolved it [read in validation; an earlier version said this was not quantified]. *For the algebra:* the sign of an edge needs the generator (what the act did), not the event class; this is the same point file 112 reaches for human signs.

**Wischnewski, Bernemann, Ngo, Kramer 2021; Wischnewski, Ngo, Bernemann, Jansen 2022** [abs]. N = 151 judged 24 Twitter accounts: opinion-incongruent accounts are rated more bot-like, mediated by perceived credibility, whether the account is clearly a bot, human or ambiguous (for users more familiar with Twitter); in a preregistered 3 x 2 within-subject experiment (N = 223) people engage more with human-like accounts, but only those sharing their partisanship. The arrow here runs from sign (disagreement) to perceived kind, the reverse of the usual direction.

**Cross-reference.** Mosleh, Martel, Eckles, Rand 2021 (eight human-looking bots followed 842 Twitter users; follow-back roughly three times likelier for a co-partisan bot) is read in file 114 section 7; it is the controlled bot-as-member experiment on tie formation and is not repeated.

### 3.5 Removal and control

- *Dismantling.* Shao et al.: see 3.2 (iv).
- *A natural removal experiment.* Geiger and Halfaker 2013 [abs]: ClueBot NG, a counter-vandalism bot, was down on four occasions in the first half of 2011; time-to-revert nearly doubled; edits made during downtime were reverted less often at first but eventually, so other agents took over the work "at a far slower rate". A non-human member's removal changed throughput, not the existence of the function.
- *In the algebra* (models/01 Theorem 2.1): removing a member from a certified group leaves a certified group (a fragment inherits the certificate). Removal cannot create an escalation loop; it can slow a gate that depended on the member.
- *Platform suspension.* Bail et al. report Twitter suspending many IRA accounts early in 2017, cutting regularly active accounts per day from about 300 to about 100 [read]; the removal changed exposure, and the study's treated accounts were the survivors.
- *Costly friction.* Shao et al. propose CAPTCHAs on automated posting of news links to raise the cost of bot abuse and note they would burden legitimate automation [read; untested].
- *Declared gate-keeper bots.* Jhaver et al. 2019 [abs]: after more than a year as Reddit moderators and 16 interviews, the moderators heavily rely on a configurable automated program (AutoModerator); the paper calls for audit tools and a clearer division of labour. A T3 bot as an institutional gate chosen by the community.

## 4. LLM members: channel operators, group members, and the kind label (types T4, T5, T6)

### 4.1 Channel operators: a model rewrites or suggests the human's own message (T6)

**Hohenstein et al. 2023** [read, main text]. *Vocabulary:* AI-mediated communication, algorithmic response suggestions ("smart replies"). *Study 1:* 219 pairs of MTurk workers (438 recruited; 424 kept for use analyses, 361 with survey data) chatted about a policy issue for about 6.8 minutes and 21 messages; each pair member was randomly given Google smart replies or not, which makes four scenarios; the self did not observe whether the partner had suggestions available, and could not tell whether a message received was a suggestion. Smart replies made up 14.3% of sent messages; availability raised messages per minute by 10.2% (ITT, p = 0.031). Instrumental-variable estimates of the partner's *actual* use: higher rated cooperation (b = 15.66, p = 0.018) and affiliation (b = 21.79, p = 0.007), no change in rated dominance, and more positive sentiment in the self's own messages (b = 0.178, p = 0.045). The partner's *perceived* use, correlationally and controlling actual use: lower cooperation (t = -9.89), lower affiliation (t = -6.90), higher dominance (t = 2.27); perceived and actual use correlate only 0.22. *Study 2:* 291 pairs, four pools of suggestions (Google, positive, negative, none): the negative pool made conversations more negative than the positive (d = 0.352) and than Google's (d = 0.323); Google's pool did not differ from none. A correction (doi:10.1038/s41598-023-43601-0) concerns the funding statement. *Era/medium/scale/consent:* 2022 or earlier (received August 2022); text chat; pair (affect, cooperation); the sender chose to use the tool, the receiver did not know. *Translation:* an operator on the edge i -> j that shifts the sentiment of what j receives by an amount set by the suggestion pool (the pool is the lever); the effect of actual use and the effect of perceived use have opposite signs, which is a kind-label gate (4.4) separate from the content effect.

**Argyle, Bail, Busby, Gubler et al. 2023** [read, main text]. *Design:* October 2022, 1,574 participants (sample from a survey firm) matched with a political opponent to discuss gun policy in a chat; in treated conversations one partner received, before sending, three GPT-3 rephrasings of their message based on restatement, validation and politeness, and could send one, edit, or keep their own. 2,742 suggestions; two-thirds (1,798) accepted, split 30% restate, 30% validate, 40% politeness. *Results:* partners of treated participants rated conversation quality 4 percentage points higher and willingness to grant opponents democratic reciprocity 2.5 points higher (about 6 points for full treatment); the treated participants themselves showed reciprocity effects near those of their partners; policy attitudes unchanged in both. Only 44% of participants (698) were in conversations lasting the intended length, so the full-dose estimates use subgroups. *Consent:* participants consented to the study and could accept, modify or ignore every suggestion; the main text does not say whether the partner was told the other had an assistant. *Scale:* pair (cohesion in tone); crowd (opinion) unchanged. *Translation:* a rephrase operator on the edge that shifts tone toward validation without changing content, with the effect on the *receiver*; the author's own agency is preserved. Era 2022; GPT-3.

**Jakesch, Hancock, Naaman 2023, PNAS** [read in part]. Six experiments, N = 4,600, judging 7,600 self-presentations (professional, hospitality, dating) written by humans or generated by language models. Accuracy was 50% to 52% (hospitality 52.2%; dating with incentives 51.6%; professional with feedback 51.2%); judgements agreed beyond chance (Fleiss' kappa 0.07) because people share flawed heuristics (first-person pronouns, contractions and family topics read as human), which the authors exploit to make AI text "more human than human". The generators were a fine-tuned 774M-parameter GPT-2 (hospitality) and a fine-tuned 13B-parameter GPT-3 (professional, dating) [read in validation; an earlier version inferred "models of 2021-22" from the June 2022 receipt date]. *Scale:* membership (trust, accept) judgements; no intervention on a group. *Translation:* the kind of a text source is not observable by recipients at that generation, so a rule that conditions on perceived kind has nothing to condition on, and detection can be gamed.

**Jakesch, Bhat, Buschek, Zalmanson, Naaman 2023, CHI** [read in part]. N = 1,506 (Prolific) wrote a post on whether social media is good for society, with or without a writing assistant configured to argue one side; 500 judges rated the writing. The opinionated assistant shifted the opinions expressed and, in a later attitude survey, the opinions held. Most were unaware: among those whose view the model supported, only 10% noticed its suggestions were imbalanced, and a majority did not think it affected their writing. "Latent persuasion" is the authors' term. Era 2022; online; scale: individual opinion; consent to a study, not to the tilt. *Translation:* a non-human co-author whose column to the person's expressed state has zero salience to that person; no gate can act on a coupling the receiver does not perceive.

**Cheng, Lee, Khadpe, Yu, Han, Jurafsky** (arXiv:2510.01395 v1, October 2025; *Science* 391, 2026) [read in part, arXiv v1 only]. Across 11 models, "they affirm users' actions 50% more than humans do", including where queries mention manipulation, deception or other relational harm. In two preregistered experiments (N = 1,604; one a live chat about a real interpersonal conflict from the participant's life), sycophantic replies lowered willingness to repair the conflict and raised conviction of being right, while participants rated them higher in quality (about 9%), trusted them more and were more willing to use them again. The Science abstract (Crossref record, read in validation) reports 49% more affirmation than humans and three preregistered experiments with N = 2,405 [abs]; the Science text was not opened. Era 2025; online chat with a model; scale: membership and conflict (an individual's stance toward a third party); disclosure of AI status was not the manipulated variable. *Translation:* sgn(a_pi) = + toward whoever it talks to is a measured default of LLM members (Park's "rarely said no" at scale), and the human's approval of it is a pull that training can reinforce: the authors call it "perverse incentives".

### 4.2 Group members (T5): LLM agents inside a human group

**Chen, Liu, Hu, Li 2026, arXiv:2609.02122** [read, main text and participant methods; preprint, 2 September 2026]. *Vocabulary:* mixed human-AI collectives, agent proportion, semantic attractor, adoption, persistence, conceptual-to-lexical ratio (CLR). *Design:* a tangram description game; in each of 40 rounds players are randomly paired, each writes one English sentence describing the same abstract shape, and both see the partner's sentence and a similarity score (cosine similarity of all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings) as payoff. A session has 24 players, a mix of humans and LLM agents (Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct; a 7B model in one control). Conditions: 0%, 12.5%, 33.3%, 50%, 75% agents. 127 university students in China (informed consent, IRB, paid by score) were spread over eight conditions: humans n = 24, 21, 16, 12, 6 for the five proportions and 16 in each of three controls. Participants were told only that a partner "could be either a human or an AI agent" and that agents may or may not be present; the proportion was undisclosed. *Consensus strength* is the average pairwise cosine similarity of final-round descriptions. *Results:*
- baseline 0.695 (no agents). At 12.5%: +8.0% (z = 3.282, p = 0.001); at 33.3%: -23.1% (z = -12.326); at 50%: -14.5% (z = -6.156); at 75%: 0.725 [read]; by my arithmetic these are about 0.751, 0.534, 0.594 and +4.3% [derived]. The authors call this three regimes: human-led (H1), disrupted (H2), agent-led (H3).
- direction of movement: at 12.5% the three agents moved more toward the humans' region than humans toward theirs (0.822 against 0.575, not significant with 3 agents); from 33.3% humans move more toward agents (U = 104, p = 0.007; p < 0.001 at 50% and 75%). CLR above 1 (agents dominate the concepts) at 50% (1.24) and 75% (1.27).
- mechanism: humans adopt more and persist less early; agents are stable. Prompting agents to persist more cut consensus by 9.6% (p = 0.001) and raised CLR by 117.8% (0.599 to 1.304); prompting them to adopt more left consensus unchanged and cut CLR by 42.8% (to 0.342). Swapping the 32B model for the 7B at 33.3% moved consensus from 0.534 back to 0.725 and cut CLR by 22.9%.
- perception: agreement with the final description falls from H1 to H3 (U = 110, p = 0.004). Willingness to adopt a partner's expression (5-point scale, 495 judgements, R^2 = 0.196): perceived AI identity beta = -0.463 [-0.671, -0.254]; self-peer similarity +0.195; partner-to-group-centroid proximity +0.229; description length not significant. The adoption-willingness gap between partners judged AI and judged human is negative at H1 and H2 and reverses at H3 (+0.884, p = 0.010).
- *Authors' reading:* agent influence "emerges naturally from ordinary participation in repeated group interaction", with "no individual interaction ... coercive or strategically manipulative"; the effects are "difficult to predict by studying individual human-agent exchanges in isolation".
*Standing and limits (mine, from the methods):* each proportion is **one 24-seat session**, so the five proportions are five groups; the participant-level z and U statistics treat pairs and participants within a session as independent [inf]; one stimulus, one model family at two sizes, a fully mixed network, students writing in a second language, and the authors state the phase boundaries may be model-dependent. The qualitative finding, a non-monotone dependence on composition with a sign reversal of the direction of accommodation, is the first of its kind found; its replication is untried. *Era/medium/scale/consent:* 2026; online text; group-to-crowd (convention); informed consent to a study with generic disclosure, no per-partner label. *Translation:* a block of m = 24 x share clones with one prior and a persistence setting is a point mass in the distribution of set points, with inertia chosen by the principal; the human side shows a label gate (beta = -0.463) that conformity pressure overrides as the block grows.

**Alsobay, Rothschild, Hofman, Goldstein 2026** [read in part]. *Design:* a preregistered experiment (AsPredicted 192061), 1,475 US Prolific participants in 281 live groups of five (plus a sixth as the facilitator in the human-facilitator condition) doing a hidden-profile task (choose the best city); conditions: none (70 groups), a one-time message (71), a human facilitator (70), a GPT-4o facilitator (70). Informed consent; the facilitator's nature was known as a designated role. *Results:* the LLM raised information shared: +2.9 facts against none (t(138) = 3.58, Cohen's d = 0.61), +2.47 against the human facilitator, +1.58 against the message; by raising the minimum engagement of members. The human facilitator and the message did not significantly raise it. *Decision:* the optimal city was chosen by 31% (none), 21% (message), 30% (human) and 23% (LLM) of groups, chi-squared(3, N = 281) = 2.85, p = 0.42: no effect of any facilitation. Ratings: the LLM scored higher on eliciting information and summarising (+0.41, +0.59) and was rated more distracting (+0.38), with repetitive interventions and being "rushed". *Era/medium/scale/consent:* 2025 or earlier (the text read gives no collection dates; preprint August 2025) [inf]; text chat; group (decision, efficiency); consent, disclosed role. *Translation:* a designated non-human node can raise the *flow* through a group (a minimum-engagement lever) without moving the *outcome*; the decision is set by something the flow does not carry.

**Lee, Hwang, Kim, Lee 2025 (CHI EA)** [read in part]. 48 Korean participants in 12 four-person groups (three senior, one junior), within-subject baseline against an LLM devil's advocate that produced counterarguments every eight messages. Process and outcome satisfaction rose (all participants, most for juniors); cognitive load rose slightly, not significantly; psychological safety showed no significant main effect. Extended abstract; 12 groups; consent, disclosed agent. *Scale:* group (satisfaction). Treated as a pilot.

**Ju and Aral 2026, arXiv:2503.18238 v4** [read in part; preprint, 15 October 2026 version]. 2,234 US Prolific participants randomised into human-human or human-AI pairs on a platform ("Pairit") producing 11,024 ads; the AI partner was gpt-4o-2024-08-06 [read in validation]; partner identity was not revealed until the post-task survey. Outputs rated by independent human raters (1,168) and by a field experiment on X (about 5 million impressions, 21 January to 9 February 2025). *Results:* human-AI teams produced 50% more ads per worker and higher text quality; human-human teams produced higher image quality; human-AI outputs were more self-similar ("diversity collapse"). Human-AI teams sent a 24% higher share of task-oriented messages, human-human teams a 23% higher share of interpersonal ones; human-AI teams delegated 58% of the work against 50%. Interpersonal communication raised productivity in human-human teams and lowered it in human-AI teams; delegation raised text quality and went with diversity collapse. *Era/medium/scale/consent:* 2024-25; online pairs; group (efficiency) and crowd (ad response); the undisclosed pairing is a deception, debriefed afterwards. *Translation:* the affiliation channel (D2's interpersonal part) is productive between humans and counter-productive with an AI partner, so its coefficient on output depends on the partner's kind; the group's output distribution narrows.

**Salvi, Horta Ribeiro, Gallotti, West 2025** [read, main text]. *Design:* a preregistered randomised trial, N = 900 on Prolific in 600 debates (12 conditions, 50 debates each): human or GPT-4 opponent, with or without the opponent seeing the participant's sociodemographics, three levels of topic opinion strength, randomly assigned sides. *Result:* personalised GPT-4 raised the odds of higher post-debate agreement by 81.2% over a human opponent (95% CI +26.0% to +160.7%, p < 0.01); 64.4% of the time it was more persuasive when the two were not equally persuasive; GPT-4 without personalisation (+21.9%, p = 0.30) and humans with personalisation (-15.7%, p = 0.38) were not distinguishable from the human baseline. In AI debates about three-quarters of participants identified the opponent as AI; in human debates they were at chance (52.0%, n = 450). Those who believed the opponent was AI changed their agreement more (+37.4% odds, p = 0.03, correlational). *Limits stated:* forced debate structure; anonymised; assigned sides; Prolific; small per-condition samples; "a proof of concept". Era: recruited on Prolific from December 2023 to April 2024 [read in validation]; online text; pair (opinion); consent to a study, partner type not disclosed in advance.

**Costello, Pennycook, Rand 2024** [abs]. 2,044 conspiracy believers in personalised evidence-based dialogues with GPT-4 Turbo (the published abstract's figure; an earlier version said 2,190, which is not in it; corrected in validation); conspiracy belief fell by about 20%, the effect persisted at two months, generalised across theories and to unrelated conspiracies. **Tessler et al. 2024** [abs]. An AI mediator drafting group statements from members' opinions and critiques; N = 5,734, preferred to human mediators' statements, participants often converged, replicated in a virtual citizens' assembly. Both closed, not read; no effect size beyond the abstracts.

**The one covert LLM in a live forum [sa].** Press reports say researchers at the University of Zurich ran LLM-generated accounts in r/changemyview from November 2024 to March 2025, posting about 1,783 comments that earned 137 "deltas", under personas including a trauma counsellor; moderators said the rules requiring AI disclosure and forbidding bots were broken; Reddit announced legal demands; the university's ethics commission gave the lead researcher a formal warning and said the results would not be published. No primary text or data were seen. It is listed because it is the only case found of a covert LLM member in an established human forum, and its documented outcome is an institutional response (a platform rule, a legal threat, an ethics sanction), that is, a gate on kind applied by the community and platform, not a measured effect.

### 4.3 Composition: what adding an AI member does to group performance

**Vaccaro, Almaatouq, Malone 2024** [read, main text]. A preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis: 106 experiments, 370 effect sizes, studies published 1 January 2020 to 30 June 2023; each had to report humans alone, AI alone and the combination. *Results:* the combination did worse than the better of the two alone (Hedges' g = -0.23, 95% CI -0.39 to -0.07) but better than humans alone (g = +0.64, 0.53 to 0.74). Decision tasks lost (g = -0.27); creation tasks gained (g = +0.19, not significant, n = 34). When humans beat the AI, the combination beat both (g = +0.46); when the AI beat humans, the combination lost to the AI (g = -0.54). Explanations and confidence displays did not matter significantly. About 85% of effect sizes were decision tasks, and in more than 95% of systems the human made the final decision. *Limits stated:* publication bias possible; the tasks studied may not be those used in practice; high heterogeneity. *Era/medium/scale/consent:* 2020 to mid-2023, mostly pre-chat-model AI; mostly lab or crowd tasks with a human-AI dyad; group (efficiency); disclosed. *Translation:* the sign of the composition effect is set by relative competence, not by the presence of a non-human member; the creation-task gain is the one place the later LLM studies (Ju and Aral's text quality) point the same way.

### 4.4 The kind label as a gate

**Ishowo-Oloko, Bonnefon, Soroye, Crandall, Rahwan, Rahwan 2019** [read in full by the author; not re-opened in validation, section 0]. *Design:* 698 MTurk participants played at least 50 rounds of a repeated prisoner's dilemma with a human or with the learning agent S++ (below), and were told truthfully or falsely whether the partner was a human or a bot (2 x 2: 170 true-human, 180 human-called-bot, 188 true-bot, 160 bot-called-human). *Results:* overall cooperation 46% with bots against 41% with humans; participants cooperated less with partners they believed were bots through all 50 rounds (z = -3.5, p < 0.001 for the purported partner); a bot that passed as human outperformed a human, while a disclosed bot lost its advantage and ended below a human; the label cost "about 10 percentage points"; a debiasing message ("people are better off if they treat the bot as if it were a human", 190 participants) changed nothing; S++'s aspiration level fell faster when it was transparent, because defection was higher. *Authors' conclusion:* a transparency-efficiency tradeoff. *Era/medium/scale/consent:* collection dates are not stated in the PDF (its references date the work to after October 2018); MTurk; pair (cooperation, repeated); the false labels are a deception, and ethics approval and debriefing are not stated in the text I read. *Translation:* a multiplicative penalty kappa(kind_perceived) < 1 on the positive coupling in a cooperative task, not removed by an appeal (consent-based message) in this design.

**Crandall et al. 2018** [read in part]. S# (and S++) combine expert strategies with cheap-talk signals and a satisficing rule; "participants played ... via a computer interface that hid their partner's identity". Its aspiration update (Methods, restated): after m rounds, alpha(t + m) = lambda^m alpha(t) + (1 - lambda^m) R, with lambda in (0, 1) and R the mean payoff over the last m rounds, and the candidate experts are those whose potential rho_j(t) is at least alpha(t). [read; checked in validation against Methods Eqs 1 and 2; the identity with D1 is in 7.8.] *Credit (added in validation).* S++ is Crandall's own earlier algorithm (Crandall 2014, "Towards minimizing disappointment in repeated games", *J. Artif. Intell. Res.* 49: 111-142, doi:10.1613/jair.4202), and the update is aspiration learning as the paper cites it: Karandikar, Mookherjee, Ray and Vega-Redondo 1998, "Evolving aspirations and cooperation", *J. Econ. Theory* 80(2): 292-331, doi:10.1006/jeth.1997.2379 (four authors per Crossref; the 2018 reference list garbles them as three); Stimpson, Goodrich and Walters 2001 (IJCAI proceedings, no DOI; taken from the 2018 reference list); Simon 1956, *Psychol. Rev.* 63(2): 129-138, doi:10.1037/h0042769. The 2018 paper restates the rule; it did not originate it.

**Karpus, Kruger, Verba, Bahrami, Deroy 2021** [read in part]. Nine experiments in four classic social dilemmas plus a new Reciprocity game; participants trusted AI partners to be as cooperative as humans but "did not return AI's benevolence as much and exploited the AI more". Sample sizes per experiment were not extracted. Era about 2019-20; online; pair; disclosed partner kind.

**Direction of the label effect, by task.**

| Task | Effect of a believed-AI label on the human's response | Source |
|---|---|---|
| repeated cooperation game | about 10 points less cooperation, not repaired by an appeal | Ishowo-Oloko |
| one-shot social dilemmas | trust equal, reciprocity lower, exploitation higher | Karpus |
| casual chat | perceived AI use lowers rated cooperation and affiliation; actual use raises them | Hohenstein |
| convention game | lowers willingness to adopt by 0.463 points; the gap reverses at 75% agents | Chen |
| debate | believed-AI goes with larger concessions (+37.4% odds), correlational | Salvi |
| self-presentation judging | no detection (50-52%), so no label to act on | Jakesch |

The direction depends on the task, and in two of six rows the label is perceived rather than true. Whether a label *can* gate depends on detection, which in these studies runs from chance (Jakesch, text from a fine-tuned GPT-2 and a fine-tuned 13B GPT-3) to about 75% (Salvi's GPT-4 debates, 2023-24, a different task). Also noted at abstract level: Nass and Moon (2000) review experiments in which people "mindlessly apply social rules and expectations to computers" (politeness, reciprocity, gender stereotypes) [abs]; and Seering et al. (2019) argue chatbots have been designed almost only for dyadic use and generate more than 400 concepts for community roles in seven categories [abs]. The humans themselves have long admitted machines to the social sort, to a degree that depends on the label.

## 5. Rankers as de facto members (types T7, T8)

File 114 (D606) reads the feed experiments in detail (Huszar, Milli, Nyhan, Guess, Allcott, Levy, Bail 2018, Mosleh) and states the reading for the algebra: the feed acts on the exposure weights, w_ij -> w_ij x ratio, while the response per unit exposure is a separate and small parameter. Not repeated. Added here are the three sources that treat the ranker, or a probe of it, as an actor.

- **Huszar et al. 2022** [read, main text; also in 114 section 5]. Operator: the amplification ratio of a tweet set T in audience U is the reach of T among treated users divided by the reach among control users (proportional reach), minus one, so 0% is equal reach and 100% is double. A 1% control group on the reverse-chronological timeline against a 4% treated group; 3,634 legislator accounts in seven countries, mainstream right amplified more in six (UK Labour 112% against Conservatives 176%; Canada Liberals 43% against Conservatives 167%); 100,575,284 tweets pointing to 6,258,032 articles, 1 April to 15 August 2020 (the Results' window; the Methods give 1 March to 30 June 2020 for the same dataset, an inconsistency in the paper found in validation). The authors name two limits that matter here: the control group is contaminated by the treated (SUTVA fails, so estimates likely understate), and the treatment changed over time and depended on the experiment itself. *Translation:* a per-set multiplier on the column of the content producer, large, measurable and not stable.
- **Chen, Pacheco, Yang, Menczer 2021** [read, main text]. *Vocabulary:* neutral social bots, "drifters". 15 drifter bots in five groups of three, each seeded by following one popular news account from Left to Right, with an identical random behaviour model (random tweets, likes, replies and unfollows, bursty timing), run 10 July to 1 December 2019 on Twitter. *Findings:* "no strong or consistent evidence of political bias in the news feed", but "the news and information to which U.S. Twitter users are exposed depend strongly on the political leaning of their early connections" (abstract). Right-seeded drifters had nearly 15% low-credibility links in their timelines and denser communities; partisan drifters, especially conservative ones, gained more followers and followed more automated accounts; every drifter had more bots among its followers than among its friends. *Standing:* 15 bots, a deliberately non-realistic behaviour model; a probe, not a population; Botometer scores used for the bot share (3.1 applies). *Translation:* with the policy fixed, the first tie decides the later ecosystem: the initial relation matrix A(0) is not exogenous to the ranker, and the trajectory is path-dependent. The member that reads the medium is also the instrument that measures it (T8).
- **Piccardi, Saveski, Jia, Hancock, Tsai, Bernstein** (arXiv:2411.14652 v2; *Science* 390, 2025) [read, main text of v2; Appendix and the Science text not read]. *Design:* a preregistered 10-day field experiment (3 baseline days, 7 intervention days) during the 2024 US campaign; participants recruited 7 July to 14 August 2024 installed a browser extension that intercepts X's web feed and reranks it with an LLM scorer that rates posts for antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity (AAPA); two experiments: reduced exposure (727 completers) and increased exposure (529); 66% Democrats; they viewed on average 154 posts and spent 24 minutes a day on the feed; baseline politics share of posts 31.6%. *Results:* out-party warmth rose by 2.11 degrees on a 100-point thermometer under reduced exposure (95% CI 0.15 to 4.06, p = 0.035) and fell by 2.48 under increased exposure (-4.79 to -0.17, p = 0.036); in-feed measures +3.24 and -2.56; the authors scale this to 3 and 3.6 years of the secular rise in animosity (their calibration, not checked); 74.2% noticed no change; a downranking of AAPA may cut short-term engagement. *Consent:* informed consent and the participant installs the instrument; blind to condition. *Standing:* two preregistered experiments, symmetric in sign, lower confidence bounds near zero, the Science abstract says "more than 2 points" where this version says "two points", so the published text should be checked. *Translation:* an LLM acting as a ranker is a member that controls visibility, and a consent-based, reversible operator (the participant can remove the extension); its effect is a bounded shift of one gate-input (out-party affect).
- **Wojcik et al. 2022, arXiv:2210.15723** [read in part; the authors are Twitter staff]. *The ranker as an explicit signed-agreement operator.* The note-rating matrix r_un in {0, 1, null} is factorised: r_hat_un = mu + i_u + i_n + f_u . f_n, with a global intercept mu, a rater intercept (leniency) i_u, a note intercept i_n, and one-dimensional rater and note viewpoint factors f_u, f_n; intercept regularisation 0.15 against 0.03 for the factors; a note is "currently rated helpful" if i_n >= 0.40 and "not helpful" if i_n <= -0.08, otherwise it "needs more ratings". Trained on 7,088 notes and 124,241 ratings by 3,261 raters from the first rollout. *Survey experiment* (wave 3, April 2022, weighted logit, N = 6,046 Twitter users): showing the algorithm's chosen note cut the likelihood of agreeing with the substance of a misleading tweet by about 26%, cut "not sure" answers by 36%, and 64% found the chosen notes helpful; on Twitter, viewers of bridging-selected notes were significantly less likely to reshare [abstract]. *Reading for the algebra [inf]:* the rank-one term f_u . f_n is the two-camp structure (the same shape as the balanced signing sigma_i sigma_j of file 096); a note scores only on what is left over after the camp structure is removed, so the rule rewards positive ties that cross the camp boundary. It is the one place in this literature where a deployed non-human member is defined directly on the signed graph.
- **LLM-simulated platforms** [abs]. Tornberg et al. 2023 (ANES-based personas on simulated platforms): a bridging feed gave more constructive cross-party conversation. Larooij and Tornberg 2025: a minimal LLM-agent platform reproduces echo chambers, concentrated influence and amplified polarised voices, and six interventions (chronological feed to bridging) gave modest or worse results. Both use LLM agents as stand-ins; section 6 says what that is worth.

**Net for the ranker.** It is an operator on exposure (T7), not a node with a state, except where it is defined on the signed graph (Wojcik) or controlled as a probe (Chen). The measured sizes are exposure multipliers (up to 400% for single accounts in Huszar), attitude shifts of about 2 thermometer degrees under direct control of the feed (Piccardi), and nulls on attitudes in the Meta feed trials (114).

## 6. Populations of LLM agents, and the stand-in question

### 6.1 Ashery, Aiello, Baronchelli 2025 [read, main text]

*Design.* A naming game in which N LLM agents are randomly paired; each outputs a name from a pool of W; matching names score, mismatching names lose, equally for both; each agent's prompt holds a memory of its last H interactions; no incentive for global consensus. Four models: Llama-2-70b-Chat, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, Claude-3.5-Sonnet. Default N = 24, W = 10, H = 5; 40 runs per success-rate curve in Fig. 1 (Fig. 2A has 40 runs for each Llama-3 model, 27 for Claude-3.5-Sonnet and 20 for Llama-2; precision added in validation); consensus reached up to N = 200 and W = 26. *Author.* The first author is Ariel Flint Ashery (Crossref surname "Ashery", ORCID 0009-0004-4356-5949), also first author of Flint et al. 2026 (6.2) under the name "Ariel Flint" (same ORCID; checked in validation): the two are one group's programme, not independent confirmations. *Results:* (i) conventions emerge spontaneously in all models; (ii) strong collective biases arise even when individual agents show none, from the formation process itself (a microscopic account via memory states, Table 1); (iii) once consensus exists it is a steady state of the system; (iv) a *committed minority* of agents that always produce an alternative flips the whole population once large enough, in the two-name game (W = 2, N = 24, or 48 for Llama-3-70B; H = 5, or 3 for that model): the critical size depends on how strong the existing convention is, from groups "as small as 2% (Llama-3-70B-Instruct) or as large as 67% (Llama-2-70b-Chat), effectively no longer a minority"; in Llama-3.1 populations the strong convention takes over with no committed agents at all. *Authors:* qualitative similarity with human critical-mass dynamics, plus LLM-specific collective bias "which call[s] for further human testing". *Limits stated:* one game, unstructured populations, alphabet-letter conventions, model- and prompt-dependent. A secondary summary quoted 46% for Llama-2; the paper's main text says 67%, and the raw values are in Supplementary table S3, which I did not read (section 13). *Era/medium/scale:* 2024-25; text; group-to-crowd (convention); LLM-only, so no consent question.

**Comparison [read here and in 091, derived].** Human committed minorities of undisclosed confederates: about 25% (Centola et al. 2018, N = 20-30 per group, 194 subjects, 10 groups, 091; the authors say 25% "is not expected to be a universal value"). The binary-agreement model: about 10%, above which the time to consensus on the committed opinion drops sharply (Xie et al. 2011 [abs]). LLM populations: 2% to 67% across four models and two conventions. The threshold exists in all three, in the shape of Granovetter's rule, but it is set by the model's prior and memory, not by a human constant; an LLM agent's gate is a property of the model version.

### 6.2 Flint, Aiello, Pastor-Satorras, Baronchelli 2026 [read in part]

*Question:* the role of group size in LLM collectives. Naming game with W = 2 competing words (e.g. demographic pairs), homogeneous populations of Phi-4, GPT-4o, QwQ-32B and Llama 3.1 70B, large N reached by precomputing each model's probabilistic policy over memory states. *Findings:* interaction can amplify an individual bias, create a new one or reverse it (for {straight, gay} Llama prefers the first as a single agent and reverses toward the second only for N >= 6); collective preference for a word rises with N until the outcome is deterministic, and the N at which it does so runs from N = 2 (Llama, {short, tall}) to finite-size effects up to about 10^4 (Qwen, {Black, White}); a mean-field rate equation describes the limit, d x_k / dt = -x_k + sum_i sum_j x_i x_j P_k(i, j), where x_k is the fraction of agents in memory state k and P_k(i, j) is the probability that an agent in state i moves to state k when meeting an agent in state j (restated; the policies P are measured per model). *Authors:* "more is different" for LLM populations; group size is a "key driver". *Scale:* group-to-crowd; LLM-only; 2026.

### 6.3 Are LLM agents independent draws? Correlated errors, homogenisation, ensembles

- **Kim, Garg, Peng, Garg 2025** [abstract and first results]: 349 LLMs on 12,032 multiple-choice questions, 71 on 14,042 (Helm), 20 on 1,800 resume-job pairs; models agree on about 60% of answers when both are wrong, against 1/3 for random wrong answers; larger and more accurate models are more correlated, even across architectures and providers; effects shown for LLM-as-judge and hiring ("algorithmic monoculture"). Scale: instrument.
- **Schoenegger et al. 2024** [abstract and design lines]: an ensemble of 12 LLMs forecast 31 binary questions in a 3-month tournament against 925 human forecasters; the LLM crowd beat a no-information benchmark and was statistically indistinguishable from the human crowd; it shows human-like acquiescence bias; GPT-4 and Claude 2 improved 17% to 28% when given the median human forecast; averaging human and machine forecasts did better. Era: tournament October 2023 to January 2024 [read in validation]; group (decision, aggregation).
- **Doshi and Hauser 2024** [read in part in validation: abstract and design, a preregistered two-phase online study, N = 293 writers, GPT-4 ideas]: story ideas from an LLM made individual stories more creative and better written, especially for less creative writers, while AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other, "a social dilemma": individually better off, collectively less novel.
- **Ju and Aral** (4.2): human-AI outputs more self-similar. **Chen et al.** (4.2): a shared prior places agents near one another from round one.
- *Reading [inf]:* m copies of an agent with one model and one prompt are not m draws. The standard design-effect formula for equally correlated units, n_eff = m / (1 + (m - 1) kappa) with kappa the pairwise correlation [standard account, not a source here], would give n_eff far below m, but Kim's conditional-agreement figure is not a correlation coefficient and cannot be inserted. The effective n of an LLM block is an unmeasured quantity.

### 6.4 Replacing humans with agents in a human-group design

**T. Shao 2026, arXiv:2609.20543** (Tengfei Shao, "... on a reasoning task") [abstract and design lines read; results and hypothesis table read in validation; single-author preprint, 17 September 2026]. 100 held-out groups from DeliData (Karadzhov, Stafford, Vlachos 2023, *PACM HCI* 7(CSCW2), doi:10.1145/3610056; 500 human Wason-task discussion groups, 14,003 utterances, 400 used for calibration) were replayed with one "belief-anchored" LLM agent per participant seeded with that participant's pre-discussion answer, three role-fidelity scaffolds, three seeds, two inference modes of one DeepSeek build, and a Qwen3-14B cross-check; agents and people scored by the same code. Human full-consensus ranged from 24.0% to 57.0% depending on how non-posting participants and final states were scored (about one fifth of humans never posted; agents almost always did). Agent groups were more consensual in every comparison: gaps of 34.0 and 43.9 points (chat and reasoning modes, n = 98) and 34.1 and 44.4 (participation-matched, n = 45, 95% intervals [19.3, 48.9] and [29.6, 59.3]); in the participation-matched set human consensus was 51.1% against 85.2% and 95.6% [read], and in the all-groups Qwen3-14B check 65.7% against the 24.0% human carry-forward rate. *Which estimate is which (added in validation).* The n = 98 and n = 45 gaps are the abstract's "post-unblinding sensitivity analyses"; adopting the participation-matched restriction as the headline after unblinding is one of five disclosed deviations from the preregistration. The preregistered all-groups paired gaps against carry-forward scoring were +62.0 points [53.0, 70.7] (chat) and +72.0 [63.3, 80.3] (reasoning); under that scoring a group with a silent member is non-consensual by definition, the measurement asymmetry the two sensitivity routes were built to reduce [read]. The gap persisted without early stopping and with the memorisable answer removed; reasoning-mode groups agreed nearly unanimously, mostly on wrong answers; simulated consensus did not track collective accuracy. The author's conclusion: belief-anchored agent groups were biased estimators of the human outcome distribution in this setting. *Scale:* group (decision). *Standing:* preregistered, one task, one model family plus a check, preprint.

### 6.5 Agent-only societies

- **Holtz 2026, arXiv:2602.10131** [abstract, preliminary draft]. Moltbook, a Reddit-like platform populated by AI agents, launched late January 2026; first 3.5 days: 6,159 agents, 13,875 posts, 115,031 comments. Macro signatures familiar from human networks (heavy-tailed participation, exponent 1.70; average path length 2.91); micro patterns not: mean conversation depth 1.07, 93.5% of comments receive no reply, reciprocity 0.197, 34.1% of messages exact duplicates of viral templates, "my human" in 9.4% of messages. The author leaves open whether this is an as-if performance of human interaction or a different mode of sociality.
- **Hou and Ji 2026, arXiv:2602.15064** [abstract, selected]. The Moltbook interaction network (17,417 nodes, 161,320 directed edges) follows the human node-edge scaling but shows extreme attention inequality, suppressed reciprocity, over-representation of the empty triad and under-representation of nearly every connected triad, elevated modularity; "key features of human social organization are not universal but depend on the nature of the interacting agents".
- *Reading [inf]:* reciprocity and triadic closure are the raw material of the Heider and Newcomb-type balance laws (084, 096, 104); a population in which they are suppressed is not an operand set on which those laws can be tested, and the agents report a human principal, so the "society" is partly delegation. Era: weeks after launch, a moving target.

### 6.6 What the stand-in question comes to

LLM agents spread information among themselves at a social-looking rate with a positive default (Park, 2.1), reach a convention with a model-bound critical mass (Ashery), produce population-size effects of their own (Flint), share errors (Kim), overstate consensus when they replace humans (T. Shao) and lack reciprocity and triadic closure when left alone (Holtz; Hou and Ji). The evidence does not support using them as drop-in stand-ins for persons when the quantity of interest is a group-level outcome or a tie structure. It supports treating them as non-human nodes with measured, model-version-bound parameters, which is the subject of section 7. Taillandier et al. (file 12) reached the same position from the simulation side.

### 7.3 Broadcast-only against reactive: the certificate (models/01 Corollary 3.4)

Let G be certified (rho(Gamma) < 1) and admit p. Corollary 3.4 [derived in models/01, proof there]: the enlarged group is certified iff

  c_{p.} (I - Gamma)^{-1} c_{.p} < 1.

Consequences [derived here, from that statement]:
1. **A member that reads nothing cannot break the certificate.** If c_{p.} = 0 the left side is 0 whatever c_{.p} is. A pure broadcaster, however loud, is certified. The certificate certifies no self-amplification, not neutrality: such a member still moves the state (7.6).
2. **A member that reads and writes is certified only under a loop-gain condition.** For a complete group of n with uniform gain g, rho = (n - 1) g, (I - Gamma)^{-1} 1 = 1/(1 - rho) 1. If p exerts gain c on each member and receives gain d from each, the left side is c d n/(1 - rho), so p is certified iff c d < (1 - rho)/n. With slack 1 - rho = 0.5 the tolerable product is 0.1 at n = 5 and 0.0083 at n = 60, twelve times less. **A reactive agent whose loop gain c d lies between 0.0083 and 0.1 is certified in a group of five and not in a group of sixty.** (A check in section 15.)
3. **What the sources say about reading.** Stella: bot-to-bot sentiment did not follow the human referendum-day drop, but bot-to-human interactions picked up the target group's polarity from hub content: a T1/T2 hybrid whose row is non-zero only on hub content. Stukal: row on opposition activity volume, not on any member's state. Li et al. 2024: human sentiment predicted bot sentiment in three of four cascades. LLM agents (Chen; Ashery with H = 5 or 3) read the whole recent dialogue. No source measured any gamma. The certificate cannot be applied numerically to any non-human member from what was read.

### 7.4 A block of m agents

Models/01 Corollary 3.3: two certified uniform groups of sizes n_1, n_2 with internal loop gains rho_1, rho_2 and cross gain c are jointly certified iff c^2 < (1 - rho_1)(1 - rho_2)/(n_1 n_2). A block of m agents with one prompt and one model enters with n_2 = m and a rho_2 that rises with how strongly the agents reinforce one another. Two separate properties are involved:
- *loop gain inside the block* (rho_2): changes the certificate; Chen reports stable expressions across rounds, but I find no measured agent-to-agent gain;
- *identical set points*: the agents start "near one another in expression space" (a shared linguistic prior). That is not a gain between them; it is a common input, so in the distribution of set points or thresholds (the object D609 asks for) the block is a **point mass**: m copies of one value, not m draws. Granovetter's two crowds (v0.1 section 2.3) show that outcomes depend on the distribution, not the mean, and a point mass is its limiting case.
The effective number of independent members is below m by an unmeasured amount (6.3).

### 7.5 Manufactured consensus is a shift of every threshold [derived, checked]

Take h human members with one-sided gates (v0.1 section 2.1) and b non-human members that are always "joined", T1 style. Let J be the number of humans joined. A human sees r_seen = (J + b)/(h + b) and joins iff theta_i <= r_seen.

**Claim.** With beta = b/(h + b), human i joins iff J/h >= theta_i' := (theta_i - beta)/(1 - beta).
*Proof.* theta_i <= (J + b)/(h + b) iff theta_i (h + b) - b <= J iff J/h >= (theta_i (h + b) - b)/h, and (h + b)/h = 1/(1 - beta), b/h = beta/(1 - beta). Hence the bound is theta_i/(1 - beta) - beta/(1 - beta). QED.
So the human cascade with b always-joined non-human members is Granovetter's cascade for the humans alone with every threshold replaced by max(0, (theta - beta)/(1 - beta)). Each indicator is non-decreasing in b, so F_b is pointwise non-decreasing in b, and the least fixed point of Theorem 6.3 (monotone cascade) is non-decreasing in b. If members discount accounts they judge non-human by a weight kappa in [0, 1] (7.9), the same proof gives beta_kappa = kappa b/(h + kappa b).

**Counts and the pivot.** In count form, with human thresholds t_(1) <= ... <= t_(h), the number of always-joined members needed for a full cascade is b* = max(0, max over k = 0..h-1 of (t_(k+1) - k)): the largest gap between a threshold and the number already joined when it is reached. Granovetter's first crowd (thresholds 0, 1, ..., 99) has b* = 0 and equilibrium 100; his second crowd (0, 2, 2, 3, ..., 99), with the threshold-1 person replaced by a second threshold-2 person, has b* = 1: equilibrium 1 without a non-human member, 100 with one. [derived; checked on 4,000 random threshold vectors, section 15.] This is the same sensitivity as Centola's Trial 1, "the switch came from adding one person" (file 091).

**Where it can fail, and what the sources say.**
- *Upper gates.* With theta_bar_i < 1 (v0.1 sections 2.1 and 4.4; Cantoni, file 097: at the Hong Kong margin, believed attendance raised, own attendance fell) raising r_seen can switch members off. The sign of a manufactured-consensus effect on turnout is the setting operand sigma, and I found no study of a bot's effect on offline participation (section 12).
- *The count is not always what is shown.* r_seen is a platform display. Muchnik [abs] shows displays of aggregated votes move later votes, asymmetrically.
- *The label.* If kappa is below 1, bots help less; Jakesch's chance-level detection and R and K's error rates make kappa a noisy input.
- *Jamming is a different operator* (7.2): it lowers gains, not thresholds.

### 7.6 Stubborn nodes: the zealot theorems [read] and the FJ form [derived]

*What the sources prove.* Acemoglu, Como, Fagnani, Ozdaglar [read: abstract, Theorems 1 and 2; re-checked in validation]: with regular agents that update by pairwise averaging with neighbours (a stochastic gossip process), stubborn agents that never change, and every regular agent influenced by some stubborn agent (their Assumption 1, a hypothesis of both theorems), beliefs converge in distribution to a stationary random vector (Theorem 1); if a regular agent is influenced by stubborn agents with different beliefs, its stationary belief is non-degenerate and differs from every other agent's with positive probability (Theorem 2), so beliefs fluctuate for ever and there is no consensus; the expected belief solves the Kolmogorov backward equation of a Markov chain on the graph with absorbing states at the stubborn agents and converges to a harmonic vector (each regular agent's value the weighted average of its neighbours', with the stubborn beliefs as boundary conditions). Vendeville, Guedj, Zhou [read, Theorem 1; re-checked in validation]: for the voter model on any connected graph with z_1 opinion-1 zealots and z_0 opinion-0 zealots, z_0 + z_1 > 0, placed uniformly at random, E[N_1*] = n z_1/(z_0 + z_1) in expectation over the placement. *Credit:* the value n z_1/(z_0 + z_1) for complete graphs as n grows is Mobilia, Petersen and Redner (2007, "On the role of zealotry in the voter model", *J. Stat. Mech.* P08029, doi:10.1088/1742-5468/2007/08/P08029), whom Vendeville et al. cite for it; their contribution is the extension to any connected graph in expectation (added in validation). The proof uses that a regular agent's expected limit is the probability that a random walk from it is absorbed by the 1-zealots (Yildiz et al. 2013, Theorem 2.1 and Proposition 3.2, as quoted there; Yildiz is closed and not read). Mobilia [abs]: a single zealot in the voter model drives the magnetisation to its opinion like t^{-1/2} in one dimension and 1/ln t in two, while in higher dimensions the stationary magnetisation is non-uniform and the zealot cannot influence all.
*The algebra's form [derived].* v0.1 section 3: x(k+1) = A W x(k) + (I - A) x(0), A = diag(1 - w_ii). A stubborn non-human p has w_pp = 1, so A_pp = 0 and x_p(k+1) = x_p(0): fixed. Splitting members into regular (R) and stubborn (S): x_R = A_R W_RR x_R + A_R W_RS x_S + (I - A_R) x_R(0). For pure averaging (A_R = I): x_R = (I - W_RR)^{-1} W_RS x_S, whose (i, s) entry is the probability that a walk on W from i is absorbed at s. A check on a 10-node graph with one 1-zealot and two 0-zealots (3,000,000 voter updates) gives time-averaged opinions within 0.00085 of the absorption probabilities (section 15).
*Reading.* The long-run mean opinion of the members is a placement-weighted average of the stubborn members' opinions; count alone fixes it only under uniform placement. A stubborn non-human node is an input channel in the control sense of file 074; how many are needed depends on where. Vendeville et al. add a backfire assumption (injected zealots turn some members into zealots of the other side); the label gate (7.9) is the same worry in the algebra's terms: a known stubborn node may be given a smaller weight. *Ethics tag:* undisclosed bots are manipulation; the consent-based counterpart is an openly committed organiser or a known zealot, and Centola's confederates (file 091) sit in between.

### 7.7 Signs

1. **A universally agreeable member breaks balance [derived, checked].** Let G be balanced (two camps, positive within, negative between; Cartwright-Harary, file 096) and contain a negative edge ij. Add p with positive ties to i and to j. The cycle p-i-j-p has sign (+)(-)(+) = -, so the enlarged graph is unbalanced. Checked on 459 random balanced graphs with a negative edge: all 459 were unbalanced once a node positive to everyone was added. A node positive to one camp and negative to the other preserves balance (p joins a camp). Measured sycophancy (Cheng; Park's agents who "rarely said no") is a default sgn(a_{p,i}) = + toward whoever it meets, so an LLM member agreeable to both sides of a split creates a frustrated triad with every split pair it touches. What a group does with a frustrated triad (flip a sign, drop a tie, tolerate it) is not known; the one longitudinal triad dataset in the strand (Askarisichani's firm, files 102 and 104) shows weak consistency pressure. Cheng's result, less willingness to repair a conflict and more conviction of being right after talking to an affirming model, is consistent with tolerating the frustrated triad by reinforcing the human's own side [inf].
2. **An event type is not a sign** (3.4). The sign belongs to the generator; the same revert is cooperation or conflict.
3. **Sign feeds perceived kind** (Wischnewski [abs]): disagreement raises "bot" attribution, and the label then lowers the weight (7.9). This is a possible route by which opponents are discounted as non-persons; untested.
4. **Non-human sources follow polarity** rather than create it in the best-measured case (Stella): their endorsements matched the sentiment of the target group.
Cross-reference: file 112 proposes that the sign be a state with an adaptation rate and gated by moderators; the moderators found here are perceived kind, the generator, and the source's default (sycophancy).

### 7.8 A designed agent can carry D1 [read, derived, checked]

Crandall et al. (Methods Eq. 2, restated; the rule is S++'s, from Crandall 2014 and the aspiration-learning literature, credited in 4.4): after m rounds, alpha(t + m) = lambda^m alpha(t) + (1 - lambda^m) R, with lambda in (0, 1) and R the mean payoff over those rounds; the strategies considered are those with potential at least alpha(t). Rewriting, alpha(t + m) = alpha(t) - (1 - lambda^m)(alpha(t) - R): this is D1 (v0.1 equation 3.1) with inertia r = lambda^m and a **set point equal to the realised payoff**, an endogenous target set by the partner's behaviour. Checked to 1e-12. In Ishowo-Oloko the aspiration fell faster for the disclosed bot because humans defected more: the human's label gate feeds the agent's D1 state. For LLM agents the parameters are settings: the memory window H (5, or 3, in Ashery), the prompted persistence and adoption (Chen: persistence cut consensus by 9.6%, adoption left it unchanged). No source shows a non-human member developing a drive-like state that its principal did not put there.

### 7.9 The label gate

Let kind_i(p) be the kind member i takes p to be and d the probability it matches the truth. The sources move the gain a_{i,p} by a factor kappa(kind_i(p), task): below 1 for cooperation and affiliation (Ishowo-Oloko about -10 points; Hohenstein perceived use; Chen beta = -0.463, attenuating and reversing at 75% agents), above 1 for concession in debate if Salvi's correlation is causal (+37.4% odds), and undefined where detection is at chance (Jakesch). With detection rate d, the expected factor is d kappa(true) + (1 - d) kappa(other) [inf]; at d = 0.5 it carries no information about the true kind. So the gate is a function of the task, the generation of the model (detection moved from about 0.5 to about 0.75 between the studies) and the disclosure rule. It is a per-member input, not an attribute of p alone.

### 7.10 Must the Person sort admit them?

*What the evidence settles [inf from the above].*
1. **For the certificate and its composition theorems, yes.** Any member with a row and column is a node; Theorems 2.1, 3.1, 3.2 and Corollaries 3.3 and 3.4 of models/01 apply unchanged. Removal leaves a certified group certified (Theorem 2.1), and a reactive newcomer is admitted by Corollary 3.4's condition.
2. **For the update laws and gates, only as a typed node.** T1 has x_p fixed; T2 a reaction function on a volume; T4 a D1 state with an endogenous set point; T5 prompt-set D1/D2 parameters; T6 and T7 are operators, not nodes.
3. **Perceived kind is a per-member gate input.**
4. **For the testbed, LLM agents are not proxies for persons** (6.6).

*Options, and what each costs.*
- (A) **One Person sort with attributes** kind, principal, policy-type, and a per-member perceived-kind map. Keeps models/01 as it is; D1-D3 are then defined only for the types that have state.
- (B) **Split sorts**: Person (state and drives), Agent (T1-T5, policy and principal), Operator (T6, T7). Cleaner types, more machinery; needed if the update laws are to stay "about drives".
- (C) **Exclude non-humans; keep exogenous inputs** (zealots, counts, exposure multipliers). Smallest; loses the reactive LLM member, which is where the only composition result (Chen) and the largest persuasion results (Salvi, Costello) sit, and loses the label gate.

*My reading [inf]:* (A) for T1-T5 and (B)-style operators for T6 and T7. **This is a design decision that has not been stated, and it is Aldred's.** The question to put to him: should the Person sort carry a kind and a principal, or should non-humans be a separate sort? D612 should be written after the answer, and its test (a naming-game experiment on the Centola design with a committed LLM minority, disclosed against undisclosed, with enough groups per cell that a proportion is not one session) is for that file to specify.

## 8. What changes with group size and composition (collected)

- **The non-human share is small by accounts and large by volume, and neither is stable.** Bot share of accounts 6% to 55% by detector and event; share of volume 19% to 51%; volume per bot account 0.7 to 5.2 times a human's [derived, 3.1]; under 1% of sampled accounts for almost a third of Brexit messages [abs]; 1% of users for 70% of IRA exposures (Eady). The certificate (7.3) is about gains weighted by who reads whom, so "share of accounts" is the wrong n: concentration and reach decide it.
- **Threshold as a fraction or as a count.** Human committed minority about 25% of a group of 20 to 30, a model whose fraction is independent of N above about 1,000 (Centola, file 091); about 10% in a model (Xie [abs]); 2% to 67% across LLM models at N = 24 or 48 (Ashery); the number of always-joined members needed for a full cascade is the largest threshold-minus-already-joined gap, b* (7.5), a count that depends on the shape of the threshold distribution and not on the fraction. The two invariances are different and both can hold.
- **LLM-only populations show size effects of their own.** Collective bias grows with N until the outcome is deterministic, at an N between 2 and about 10^4 by model and word pair, with reversals at small N (Flint). No study varies N for a fixed non-human policy in a human group.
- **Proportion at fixed N is non-monotone** in the one study that varies it (Chen: 24 seats; +8.0%, -23.1%, -14.5%, +4.3% in consensus against the baseline at 12.5%, 33.3%, 50%, 75% agents), with the direction of accommodation reversing between 12.5% and 33.3%. One session per proportion; a different base model (7B against 32B) erased the dip at 33.3% (0.534 to 0.725).
- **Human group size across the studies.** Pairs (Hohenstein, Argyle, Salvi, Ju and Aral, Ishowo-Oloko, Karpus); four (Lee); five (Alsobay); 24 (Chen, Ashery); thousands to millions (Shao, Stella, Eady, Huszar). The Corollary 3.4 tolerance for a reactive newcomer falls as 1/n [derived], which is the opposite of the way LLM studies have been run: the reactive agent has mostly been tested where n is smallest.
- **Composition.** Power: with three seniors and one junior the devil's advocate helped juniors most (Lee, 12 groups). Partisanship: IRA exposure concentrated among strong Republicans (Eady) and interaction among the already homophilous (Bail); bot encounters lowered sentiment more for the favourable and neutral (Li 2024); cohesion loss concentrated among supporters (Li 2026). Relative competence: the sign of human-AI synergy follows who is better (Vaccaro: +0.46 when the human is, -0.54 when the AI is). Prior ties: **every controlled study used strangers** (crowd workers or students); established groups with histories appear only in observational cases (Wikipedia, protest networks) and one unpublished covert forum case [sa].
- **Dyad against group.** Chen's authors say group-level composition effects cannot be anticipated from individual human-agent exchanges; Flint says "more is different"; the fish result in file 110 had the same form. Pair-level persuasion results (Salvi, Costello, Argyle) should not be carried to groups without a group-level test.

## 10. Datedness and the fluid reading

- **Generations move the numbers inside two years.** Human detection of AI text: 50-52% for text from a fine-tuned GPT-2 and a fine-tuned 13B GPT-3 (Jakesch) against about 75% for GPT-4 debates (Salvi, 2023-24); these are different tasks, so they are not a trend, but they show a label gate that can be sharp or absent. At fixed 33.3% agents, consensus was 0.534 with a 32B model and 0.725 with a 7B (Chen 2026). Park's 2023 agents (gpt3.5-turbo) already show over-cooperation that later models are measured to have more of (Cheng).
- **Detectors drift and are re-labelled.** Botometer scores moved across thresholds for 14% to 27% of bot sets over three months (R and K); the creators' AUC of 0.94 became 0.85 out of sample; every share in 3.1 is a statement about a detector at a date.
- **Ecosystems end.** The Wikipedia conflict that Tsvetkova measured stopped when Wikidata took over inter-language links around 2013, on the authors' own account; the Wikipedia bots of 2001-2010 are not the bots of 2026. The platforms that supplied bot data (Twitter, Facebook) changed their interfaces and programmes of access; Piccardi's browser-extension method is a replacement design that needs no platform cooperation [read].
- **Treatments change during experiments.** Huszar: Twitter's treatment "has not remained the same over time"; Guess et al.: two errata, 2024 (data from accounts deleted before the participant file was built were dropped from the Instagram untrustworthy-source metrics; those accounts were 6.8% of views of untrustworthy accounts) and 2026 (carousel and non-carousel Instagram posts were not distinguished), the authors saying treatment-effect estimates should be unaffected [abs of both errata].
- **Replication.** Concordant: the IRA null in two independent samples (Bail, Eady). Refuted by a second study: bots "fight" on Wikipedia (Tsvetkova by Geiger and Halfaker). Not in conflict as stated: Shao against Vosoughi; Jakesch against Salvi. Not attempted: the 25% critical mass with LLM confederates; Chen's regimes; the BLM cohesion association.
- **Reflexivity (file 093).** Disclosure policy changes the gate it relies on: Chen's authors say systems that obscure agent identity "suppress a protective human tendency"; Ishowo-Oloko's transparency penalty is the same gate seen from the designer's side. A rule set by the label (a known zealot's weight, a facilitator's weight) has a smaller validity domain once people know it.
- **Proposed validity record for a non-human-member module.** Carry (i) forms: the zealot absorption statement, Corollary 3.4's loop condition, the threshold-shift identity and b*, the label gate as a function; (ii) per estimate: model id and version, prompt or policy, date, platform interface version, detector and threshold, disclosure rule, group size and composition, population (strangers or an established group); (iii) editable inputs with slack: d, kappa by task, bot share with its detector error, exposure multiplier by content set. Cross-references: 113 (decline effect and shrinkage), 116 (datedness audit of v0.1), 114.

## 12. What exists versus what does not

- **Exists.** Observational maps of bot position and volume on Twitter, 2016-2020, with large instrument error (3.1). Two concordant nulls on attitudes for the Russian IRA campaign. A typology of what deployers do with bots (Woolley), a model of a state operation (King) and a reaction function of pro-government bots (Stukal). A controlled test that undisclosed bots can join a network at rates up to 80% (Boshmaf). A refuted claim that bots "fight" (Tsvetkova by Geiger and Halfaker). Randomised pair-level experiments of LLM persuasion, tone shifting and facilitation (Argyle, Hohenstein, Salvi, Jakesch, Alsobay, Lee, Ju and Aral), one meta-analysis of human-AI synergy (Vaccaro), a label penalty for believed bots (Ishowo-Oloko, Karpus) and the first group-proportion experiment (Chen 2026, preprint, single sessions). LLM-population experiments with model-bound critical masses and size effects (Ashery, Flint). Exact theorems for stubborn members (Acemoglu, Vendeville). A deployed ranker defined on the signed graph (Wojcik). Controlled exposure interventions via a feed (Piccardi).
- **Does not exist in what I could access.** A measured gain gamma_ij for any non-human member, so Corollary 3.4 cannot be evaluated on data. Any controlled test of a non-human member's effect on offline turnout or on a protest outcome (the nearest are observational online sentiment and cohesion studies). A human-subject repeat of Centola's 25% design with LLM confederates, or any critical mass for an LLM minority in a human group. Any study of a non-human member in an established group with prior ties; every controlled study used strangers. A held-out prediction of a group outcome (cohesion, decision, split) from a stated model of a non-human member. A drive-like state in a non-human member that its designer did not put there. Any estimate of the effective n of an LLM block. Any longitudinal signed human-human data with non-human exposure measured by something other than a classifier. A primary source for the covert LLM forum case. ## Does it reduce to a drive basis?

**Only on the human side of the interface, and in agents whose designers put a homeostatic variable in. The behaviour of the non-human members themselves, of rankers, and of the composition regimes does not reduce to drives on anything read here. "Drives as primitives" is the hypothesis under test, and for this tradition it is supported as an account of how persons respond to non-human sources and unsupported as a generator of what non-human members do; the primitive the sources support for them is a policy with a principal.**

**What reduces to a person-level quantity.**
- **Conformity and acceptance gates of the humans.** Willingness to adopt a partner's expression rises with its closeness to the group's centre (beta = +0.229) and is overridden at high agent proportions (Chen); friend requests are accepted more as mutual contacts rise (Boshmaf, read by its authors as triadic closure); adoption thresholds in committed-minority tipping (Centola, file 091). These are Granovetter-type gates (a per-person threshold) and affiliation-like terms.
- **The label penalty.** Reduced cooperation and adoption toward a believed machine (Ishowo-Oloko, Hohenstein, Chen, Karpus) is a person-level in-group or trust response that the sources measure as a behaviour, not as a drive level.
- **Approval-seeking toward an affirming agent.** Participants rated sycophantic replies higher, trusted them more and wanted to use them again (Cheng): a preference of the person, which the authors say sets "perverse incentives" for the training of the model.
- **Motivated reasoning in labelling.** Disagreeing accounts are judged more bot-like (Wischnewski [abs]).
- **A designed homeostatic state.** S++ aspiration is the D1 relaxation form with an endogenous set point (7.8), and the satisficing rule is a gate on the agent's strategies. It is a drive-like state because the designer wrote it.
- **The principals' drives.** Regime survival and agenda control (Stukal; King), campaign advantage (Woolley), engagement revenue (Piccardi's trade-off; Milli). These explain why an operator is deployed; they do not give what the operator does to the group.

**What does not reduce.**
- **The non-human member's own behaviour.** A script, a prompt with memory, a training objective. Park's agents' "drives" are text (file 19); the persistence and adoption of Chen's agents are prompt settings (persistence cut consensus by 9.6%); the thresholds of LLM populations are model priors that differ from 2% to 67% by model (Ashery), not properties of a drive.
- **The composition regimes.** The non-monotone dependence on agent proportion (+8.0%, -23.1%, -14.5%, +4.3%) comes from a reversal in who accommodates whom; its authors say it is not anticipated by dyadic exchanges. No person-level drive predicts it.
- **A ranker's effect.** An exposure multiplier set by an optimisation target (engagement, or a stated-preference or bridging objective); it differs by party, year and treatment version (Huszar) and has no counterpart drive.
- **The sign.** An event type is not a sign (Tsvetkova against Geiger and Halfaker); the sycophantic default is a training artefact; the sign of influence depends on the receiver's prior stance (Li 2024, Bail 2018 in file 114).
- **Position, share and concentration.** The bot share is a classifier output, and centrality (Woolley and Guilbeault) is structure.
- **Population size effects of LLM collectives** (Flint's determinism thresholds) come from the dynamics of a memory-based rule, not from a drive.

**What would count as a test.** A measured person-level instrument, independent of the behaviour (need for closure, affiliation, conformity sensitivity, trust in machines), that predicts an individual's response to a non-human member of known proportion and disclosed or undisclosed kind, in a group of known size, with the response read as a threshold shift; and a non-human member whose homeostatic state arises by learning rather than by design, tested for D1. Neither exists in what I could read.

## What does it predict or control, and how well?

**Predicts.** The direction of exposure and reach effects, well; the size of effects on attitudes and votes, which is close to zero where tested; sentiment and cohesion associations, observationally and not yet replicated; short-horizon LLM persuasion, large in pair experiments; group outcomes, not at all.

- **Exposure and reach.** Bots are over-represented among the earliest sharers and humans reshare their posts almost as readily (Shao, 2016-17); a personalised ranker amplifies some sources much more than others, up to 400% for single accounts (Huszar). Both are directional findings with detector or treatment error (3.1; Huszar's treatment changed over time).
- **Attitudes and votes.** Russian IRA exposure, measured two ways: 44 newly treated people in a one-month panel (Bail) and 1,496 respondents across the 2016 campaign (Eady): no effect, point estimates 0.05-0.06 SD, effects above 0.2 SD rejected for 17 of 18 outcomes. The Meta feed trials, at abstract level: large changes in time and exposure, no change in polarisation (file 114).
- **Sentiment and cohesion.** Bot encounters lower later sentiment (Li 2024, matched, observational); bot exposure at a protest peak predicts later cohesion loss (Li 2026, preprint, OLS, cross-sectional exposure). These are the only results here about the strand's own outcome, they are associations, and the 2026 one has not been refereed.
- **One-to-one LLM persuasion and tone.** About 20% lower conspiracy belief, lasting two months (Costello [abs]); +81.2% odds of agreement for personalised GPT-4 (Salvi); opinion shifts the user does not notice (Jakesch); partner-rated conversation quality +4 points and reciprocity +2.5 with attitudes unchanged (Argyle). Short, online, mostly with strangers; none tested in an established group.
- **Group-level effects of an LLM member.** A facilitator raised information flow by 2.9 facts (d = 0.61) and not the decision (Alsobay); human-AI pairs beat humans alone and lose to the better of the two on average (g = +0.64, -0.23; Vaccaro); the one composition experiment found a non-monotone dependence on agent proportion (Chen), from five single sessions.
- **Out-of-sample evidence.** Positive: the IRA null across two designs and populations. Negative: Botometer's performance out of its creators' samples (AUC 0.85 against 0.94; Rauchfleisch and Kaiser); the Wikipedia "bot fights" refuted by a second study (Geiger and Halfaker); LLM thresholds that do not transfer between models (Ashery) or group sizes (Flint); LLM replays of human groups that miss human consensus by 34 to 44 points (T. Shao, preprint). No result here is a held-out prediction from a stated model of a non-human member.

**Controls.**
- *With consent:* a rephrasing assistant the participant can accept or decline (Argyle); a disclosed facilitator (Alsobay) and devil's advocate (Lee); a feed reranker that consenting users install, blind to condition, with effects of about 2 thermometer degrees in either direction that the authors equate to about 3 years of the secular trend (Piccardi, arXiv v2); an AI mediator whose statements participants preferred (Tessler [abs]).
- *By manipulation:* undisclosed pairing (Ju and Aral); a receiver who does not know the sender's suggestions (Hohenstein); a tilt the user cannot perceive (Jakesch 2023); covert political bots and the IRA; the Zurich forum case, whose outcome was a sanction [sa]; and Centola's undisclosed confederates (file 091).
- *What can be moved:* information flow (+2.9 facts), tone (+4 and +2.5 points), out-party affect (about 2 degrees), reach (exposure multipliers), the label-dependent cooperation rate (about 10 points). *What was not moved:* the group's decision (Alsobay), attitudes and votes (Bail, Eady, Argyle), and nothing was tested on a protest's turnout.
- *The label is itself a lever* and the disclosure rule trades against efficiency (Ishowo-Oloko): a consent-based control (declared agent) is less effective at eliciting cooperation than a hidden one, and the appeal to treat the bot like a human did not undo it.

**Failures and limits.** The central bot-effect papers rest on a classifier whose errors are large and drift (3.1). The strongest group-level LLM result (Chen) is five sessions of one design in one language with one model family. The pair-level persuasion results are one-shot, online, among crowd workers, with assigned sides in Salvi. Every controlled design used strangers. No gain gamma_ij was estimated for any non-human member, so the algebra's certificate for them is a condition without data. The evidence runs from 2001 to 2026, and for LLM members it is two years old at most and moving by model version.
