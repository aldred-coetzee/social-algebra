Cluster: minimal semantic bases and cross-linguistic structure of emotion and motive concepts, i.e. candidate primitives and compositional grammars that linguistics has already proposed. Context: Aldred's aim, "an algebra which expresses basic human drives and group drives, which we can compose into equations". **Summary.** Linguistics supplies three things the other clusters lack. (1) A candidate **primitive set of 65 words** (NSM) in which emotion and speech-act concepts are claimed to be definable by composition, which is a literal grammar for building drive terms from atoms. (2) **Empirical coordinates** for the space those terms live in: valence and arousal are universal across 2,474 languages (Jackson), but four dimensions, not two, are needed within three languages (Fontaine), and EPA (Osgood) is the oldest version of the same finding. (3) A **grammar of forces** (Talmy) and an **argument-structure grammar of experiencer and stimulus** that are, in effect, two-place operators with a typed outcome. None of it is quantitative dynamics: there are no drive levels, rates or equilibria here. It is a source of *types and composition rules*, not of laws.

## 1. Natural Semantic Metalanguage (NSM): Wierzbicka and Goddard

**Citations.** Wierzbicka, A. (1996). *Semantics: Primes and Universals*. Oxford UP. Wierzbicka, A. (1999). *Emotions across Languages and Cultures: Diversity and Universals*. Cambridge UP, doi:10.1017/CBO9780511521256 (Crossref confirmed, validation pass). Wierzbicka, A. (1992). Defining emotion concepts. *Cognitive Science* 16(4), 539-581, doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1604_4 (confirmed; the earlier statement of the script method). Goddard, C. & Wierzbicka, A. (eds.) (2002). *Meaning and Universal Grammar: Theory and Empirical Findings*, 2 vols., John Benjamins, doi:10.1075/slcs.60 (Crossref confirmed). Goddard, C. & Wierzbicka, A. (2014). *Words and Meanings: Lexical Semantics across Domains, Languages, and Cultures*. Oxford UP, doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199668434.001.0001 (Crossref confirmed; online 2013, print 2014) (the 65 figure was announced here, per a secondary account). Goddard (2018) is cited by secondary sources for the count; not located.

**Their vocabulary.** Semantic primes: indefinable meanings with lexical exponents in every language tested (30+ languages claimed, `[from abstract/standard account]`), combined by a universal mini-grammar. Explication: a paraphrase of a word's meaning using only primes. Scenario / prototype: an emotion explication is a script, not a feeling-label.

**Drives/operands: the 65 primes `[read]`, Wikipedia's table (wording follows the Wikipedia article 'Natural semantic metalanguage', CC BY-SA 4.0).**
- Substantives: I, YOU, SOMEONE, PEOPLE, SOMETHING/THING, BODY. Relational: KIND, PART. Determiners: THIS, THE SAME, OTHER/ELSE/ANOTHER.
- Quantifiers: ONE, TWO, SOME, ALL, MUCH/MANY, LITTLE/FEW. Evaluators: GOOD, BAD. Descriptors: BIG, SMALL.
- Mental predicates: THINK, KNOW, WANT, DON'T WANT, FEEL, SEE, HEAR. Speech: SAY, WORDS, TRUE.
- Actions/events/movement: DO, HAPPEN, MOVE. Existence/possession: BE (somewhere), THERE IS, BE (someone/something), (IS) MINE. Life/death: LIVE, DIE.
- Time: WHEN/TIME, NOW, BEFORE, AFTER, A LONG TIME, A SHORT TIME, FOR SOME TIME, MOMENT. Space: WHERE/PLACE, HERE, ABOVE, BELOW, FAR, NEAR, SIDE, INSIDE, TOUCH.
- Logical: NOT, MAYBE, CAN, BECAUSE, IF. Intensifier: VERY, MORE. Similarity: LIKE/AS/WAY.

Count: the table has 65 entries (recounted in the validation pass). DON'T WANT is listed as a prime in its own right beside WANT in the current (2014-on) inventory, not as WANT + NOT; it is a late addition. Count history per Wikipedia `[read, secondary]` (wording follows the Wikipedia article 'Natural semantic metalanguage', CC BY-SA 4.0): 14 (Wierzbicka 1972), 60 (2002), 65 (Goddard & Wierzbicka 2014; Ye 2017). The official Griffith NSM pages and chart (v19, April 2017) returned HTTP 403 to every fetch, so the list is not checked against the primary chart. *(Validation: an earlier "61 (Peeters 2006)" was unsupported and is removed; Peeters (ed.) 2006, Semantic Primes and Universal Grammar, Benjamins SLCS 81, exists.)*

**What matters for drives.** The primes include exactly the drive-relevant core a minimalist would pick: **WANT** and **DON'T WANT** (approach and avoidance, as primitives, not as derived valences), **FEEL**, **GOOD/BAD**, **KNOW/THINK**, **CAN**, **MAYBE**, and the social substantives **SOMEONE, PEOPLE, OTHER**. There is no prime for fear, desire, curiosity, anger or any named emotion: these are all claimed to be *composite*. There is no prime for "need", "drive", "hunger" or "power".

**Operators / equations.** None numeric. The combination rule is syntactic concatenation under the mini-grammar, with BECAUSE and IF as the only logical connectives and NOT as negation. Explication template for emotions `[read]` (Wierzbicka 1999: 75, as quoted by Kitis; paraphrased here):

The fear script, in our own words (a paraphrase of the structure, not a quotation; licensing audit 2026-10-30 replaced the verbatim ~82-word blockquote, which is Wierzbicka 1999: 75, Cambridge UP): X felt something bad because X thought something like this. The thought runs: I do not know what will happen; BAD things CAN happen; I DON'T WANT them to happen; I WANT to DO something because of this, if I CAN; I do not know if I CAN do anything. Because X thought this, X feels something bad. The primes carrying the structure are WANT, DON'T WANT, KNOW, CAN, MAYBE/CAN and BAD.

*(Validation, before the 2026-10-30 paraphrase: the Kitis PDF was re-read and the then-verbatim quotation matched it line for line; Kitis attributes it to "Wierzbicka (1990, 1999)", page 1999: 75. The primary book was not reached. Note that Kitis, "Emotions as discursive constructs: the case of the psych-verb 'fear'", is a **critique** of this approach: she argues fear verbs originate in motion/action meanings and do discourse work the explication misses.)*

Afraid (X was afraid) `[read]` swaps the cause for a thing: "something bad can happen to me because of this / I don't want this to happen / I don't know if I can do anything now". Structure of every emotion explication: **[FEEL something because THINK something] + prototype thought (built from WANT / DON'T WANT / KNOW / CAN / BAD / MAYBE / HAPPEN) + [FEEL good/bad]**. Distinct emotions differ in the prototype thought, e.g. a pleased/delighted pair differs only in scenario and evaluation `[search summary]`.

Fear, read as an equation: the thought is DON'T KNOW(what happens) AND MAYBE(BAD happens) AND DON'T WANT(that) AND WANT(act) AND DON'T KNOW(CAN act). That decomposes fear into a **want-vector, an uncertainty about outcome, and an uncertainty about own capability**, which is close to the appraisal variables in 17 and to Fontaine's power axis (section 3), reached by pure semantic analysis.

**Shame: a real explication** `[read, secondary]` (validation pass). Goddard (forthcoming at the time; "Vocabulary of emotions and its development in English, German and ...") as quoted in Peeters, B. (2020), Language makes a difference: breaking the barrier of "shame", *Lublin Studies in Modern Languages and Literature* 44(1), 27-37, doi:10.17951/lsmll.2020.44.1.27-37:

> Someone X is ashamed =
> a. this someone X thinks like this at this time:
>    "people can know something bad about me
>    if people know about it, they can't not think something bad about me
>    I don't want this"
> b. because of this, this someone feels something bad
> c. like people often feel when they think like this

This is the current NSM *template* form (model thought, then a feeling caused by it, then "like people often feel"), which differs in layout from the 1999 fear script above. Shame's operands are PEOPLE, KNOW, THINK BAD about ME, and DON'T WANT, so it is an inherently **social** drive in the notation: it needs other minds as arguments, which fear does not.

**Envy: still not found.** Koselak (2008), "Cette personne a quelque chose que je n'ai pas. Une approche contrastive de réactions du type de jalousie", CMLF 2008, doi:10.1051/cmlf08050 (Crossref confirmed), compares French *jalousie/envie* with Polish, Swedish, German and English; the NSM bibliography rates it an approximate application of NSM. Unread. Envy is plausibly [SOMEONE ELSE has something GOOD; I don't have it; I WANT it; feel BAD], but that is the original author's paraphrase, **not** a quotation.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** The programme's claim is a universality claim: any language's emotion words can be explicated in primes and translated through them; proposed as a falsifiable hypothesis `[from abstract/standard account]`. No theorems.

**Empirical standing (blunt).** The primes are tested by finding exponents in many languages, not by experiment on human subjects; the explications are expert analyses by the proponents, with limited independent validation. A Finnish study (not named in the search notes, so untraced; re-find before relying on it) is reported to find there is little empirical proof that the emotion explications correspond to what speakers mean `[search summary]`. Critics (outside this search) object that the "primitive" set is English-biased and the inventory has moved from about 14 (1972) to 65, `[standard account]`. Treat explications as structured hypotheses, not data.

**Operand/operator translation.** Strong. Operands: the substantives (I, YOU, SOMEONE, PEOPLE) and states typed by the mental predicates. Operators: the predicates themselves (WANT, FEEL, KNOW, CAN, DO) as relations between a person and a proposition or an outcome; BECAUSE, IF, NOT, MAYBE as connectives. The algebra could adopt NSM as its **definitional layer**: WANT and DON'T WANT are already the two-drive core (approach/avoidance), every named drive being a derived script. The cost: no magnitude. VERY and MORE are the only quantity words, so any levels need to be added.

## 2. Jackson et al. 2019: emotion colexification across 2,474 languages

**Citation.** Jackson, J. C., Watts, J., Henry, T. R., List, J.-M., Forkel, R., Mucha, P. J., Greenhill, S. J., Gray, R. D. & Lindquist, K. A. (2019). Emotion semantics show both cultural variation and universal structure. *Science* 366(6472), 1517-1522, doi:10.1126/science.aaw8160 (Crossref confirmed, including the full nine-author list, in the validation pass).

**Method `[from abstract/standard account]`.** Colexification: two concepts expressed by one word in a language (e.g. one word for "pity" and "love"). Build a network over 24 emotion concepts, linking those that colexify, from the CLICS database, for 2,474 spoken languages in 20 families.

**Findings `[abstract read via PubMed in validation pass]`.** (a) Networks differ across families, and the difference tracks geographic proximity: culture-specific structure. (b) Every family organises the concepts mainly along **hedonic valence** and **physiological activation (arousal)**; valence and activation were the strongest predictors of clustering (a Yale HRAF summary says valence was the top predictor in 13 of 19 families, unchecked). Example (press and secondary accounts): "surprise" colexifies with "hope" and "want" in Tai-Kadai languages but with "fear" in Austronesian. *(Validation: an earlier example, "grief" and "fear" sharing words, was not found in any source and is replaced.)* Secondary sources give 20 families for the sample; a later paper by the team mentions 19, which may be where "13 of 19" comes from.

**Operands / operators.** Operands: 24 emotion concepts as nodes. Operator: colexification as an undirected link. No equations.

**Theorems.** None. The result is a pair of dimensions inferred from a graph.

**Empirical standing.** Large, but colexification is a coarse proxy (polysemy, not usage), the CLICS database is of uneven quality across languages, and the 24 concepts were pre-chosen in English (`[standard account]`, my own caveat). The valence and arousal result agrees with Russell 1980 and Osgood, which makes it a robust coordinate system but also unsurprising. It does not support "universal discrete emotions", which sits against Ekman (see 15).

**Translation.** Supports a **continuous valence-arousal state space** as the minimal common coordinate system for any drive, and warns that anything beyond those two axes is culture-sensitive. For the algebra: any state vector must expose valence and arousal as projections, whatever else it carries.

## 3. Russell, and Fontaine et al. 2007: how many dimensions?

**Citations.** Russell, J. A. (1980). A circumplex model of affect. *J. Personality and Social Psychology* 39(6), 1161-1178, doi:10.1037/h0077714 (Crossref confirmed). Russell, J. A. (2003). Core affect and the psychological construction of emotion. *Psychological Review* 110(1), 145-172, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.110.1.145 (confirmed). Fontaine, J. R. J., Scherer, K. R., Roesch, E. B. & Ellsworth, P. C. (2007). The world of emotions is not two-dimensional. *Psychological Science* 18(12), 1050-1057, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.02024.x (confirmed).

**Their vocabulary.** Core affect (Russell): a two-dimensional state, pleasure and arousal, with emotion categories as constructions on top. GRID (Fontaine): 144 features across six components (appraisal, psychophysiological change, motor expression, action tendency, subjective feeling, regulation) rated for the applicability to emotion words in three languages (English, French, Dutch).

**Findings `[abstract read via search result in validation pass]`.** Four dimensions are needed, in order of importance: **evaluation-pleasantness (valence), potency-control (power), activation-arousal, unpredictability (novelty)**. Power comes second in importance, ahead of arousal, in the original. Per-dimension variance shares are **not** confirmed (full text not reached); do not quote numbers. Later replication: Gillioz, C., Fontaine, J. R. J., Soriano, C. & Scherer, K. R. (2016). Mapping emotion terms into affective space: further evidence for a four-dimensional structure. *Swiss Journal of Psychology* 75(3), 141-148, doi:10.1024/1421-0185/a000180 (Crossref confirmed; abstract via repository records): 80 French terms, 68 features, 156 raters; the four dimensions recur, and the novelty dimension "mostly differentiated surprise from other emotions", while also varying within the fear and joy families.

**Operators / equations.** Linear dimensional models: an emotion term is a point in R^4. No dynamics.

**Theorems.** None; the work is a factor analysis, with the claim that two dimensions lose a substantial amount of structure. Concretely, power separates fear (low power) from anger (high power), which valence-plus-arousal cannot, since both are negative and high-arousal.

**Empirical standing.** The four-factor result has been replicated in several languages and for emotion words, but the GRID design rates words by how much each feature "applies" (a lexical, not experiential, measure) and the instrument is English-built. Russell's own position is that the circumplex is a useful description of core affect, not a claim that emotions are only two numbers.

**Translation.** Fear versus anger is exactly the distinction a drive algebra must carry, and valence-arousal cannot. So the state of an agent needs a **power/control scalar** as a first-class operand, which is what Atkinson's expectancy, Miller's gradients and appraisal theory all also supply (see 16 and 17). *(Validation correction.)* The earlier text said novelty/unpredictability "is the dimension where curiosity lives". That is the survey author's inference, not a finding: in GRID and in Gillioz 2016 the fourth dimension is, empirically, a **surprise** dimension (with some spread inside the fear and joy families). Curiosity is not reported as loading on it (GRID's word set contains "interest"; where it falls was not checked). Appraisal theory does tie interest to novelty-plus-coping appraisals (see 17), so the mapping is a hypothesis worth testing, not a result. The further inference, that the dimension's fourth rank shows curiosity is "peripheral in the lexicon", does not follow: the rank mostly reflects how few surprise-type terms the 24-word set held.

## 4. Osgood: the semantic differential and the EPA universals

**Citations.** Osgood, C. E., Suci, G. J. & Tannenbaum, P. H. (1957). *The Measurement of Meaning*. U. Illinois Press. Osgood, C. E., May, W. H. & Miron, M. S. (1975). *Cross-Cultural Universals of Affective Meaning*. U. Illinois Press (catalog records confirm the title, xix + 486 pp.; place of publication sources disagree).

**Findings `[from abstract/standard account]`.** Concepts rated on bipolar adjective scales load on three factors: **Evaluation, Potency, Activity (EPA)**. The 1975 project covered about 30 language communities, used 12 scales (four per dimension) on 620 concepts, with 40 teenaged male raters per concept (per a secondary analysis). The pan-cultural data were reported lost after the project centre was disrupted, so the main data cannot be re-analysed (secondary report, single source).

**Operators / equations.** None in the original. Heise's ACT builds on this (cross-ref 03): events change EPA by a linear transformation function. That is where the equations live.

**Theorems / predictions.** The claim that three dimensions are universal.

**Empirical standing.** Replicated widely, but the 1975 data are not recoverable; the original sample is teenage boys; the scales are English-derived. Fontaine (section 3) reads EPA as a subset of four.

**Translation.** EPA (= valence, power, arousal) is the three-axis state vector that Fontaine extends. If the algebra's operand is a point in such a space, ACT supplies the only worked, estimable operator calculus (03).

## 5. Lakoff & Kövecses: the folk hydraulic model in metaphor

**Citations.** Lakoff, G. & Kövecses, Z. (1987). The cognitive model of anger inherent in American English. In Holland & Quinn (eds.), *Cultural Models in Language and Thought*, Cambridge UP, 195-221. Kövecses, Z. (1990). *Emotion Concepts*. Springer. Kövecses (2000). *Metaphor and Emotion: Language, Culture, and Body in Human Feeling*. Cambridge UP (catalog confirmed). Neither the 1987 chapter nor the 1990 book was read. *(Validation: the 1987 chapter has doi:10.1017/CBO9780511607660.009, Crossref confirmed, pp. 195-221; Kövecses 1990 confirmed in catalogs, Springer-Verlag, New York; Yu, N. (1995), Metaphorical expressions of anger and happiness in English and Chinese, Metaphor and Symbolic Activity 10(2), 59-92, doi:10.1207/s15327868ms1002_1, confirmed.)*

**Findings `[from abstract/standard account]`, partly supported by search results.** ANGER IS THE HEAT OF A FLUID IN A CONTAINER (the 1987 wording; Kövecses later also writes ANGER IS A HOT FLUID IN A CONTAINER), resting on THE BODY IS A CONTAINER FOR THE EMOTIONS: the body is the container, anger is heat, pressure rises, the person "explodes" or "boils over", and control is holding the lid on. Search results indicate: English and Chinese both treat anger as heat and pressure but diverge at the second level, English as a fluid in a heated container and Chinese as a gas (Yu 1995, per a summary); Hungarian can use the head as the container; the pressurised-container image appears "to varying degrees" in English, Japanese, Chinese, Hungarian and others (Kövecses 2000, as cited by a secondary source).

**Operators / equations.** None. The "model" is a verbal schema with parts: container, substance, heat, pressure, a stage sequence (cause, anger exists, attempt at control, loss of control, act). It is a causal chain, which some formalise as a script.

**Theorems.** None. Predicts that speakers' idioms follow the schema.

**Link to 16.** The model is the folk version of Lorenz's psychohydraulic reservoir: a quantity accumulates, a valve holds, release is an event. Lorenz's model was dropped as science in the 1950s-60s (see 16), but here it survives as the *human* theory of anger. That is useful twice: (1) the lay model of a drive, which people use to reason about and manage their own and others' drives, is hydraulic, so a predictive model of social behaviour may have to include that people *act as if* drives are pressure; (2) the model supplies the discrete operators absent in Lorenz: **suppress (hold the lid), vent, boil over, cool**.

**Empirical standing.** Metaphor analysis is corpus-based and descriptive. The strong claim, that the metaphor *shapes* anger experience, has weak experimental support (`[standard account]`). The cross-linguistic overlap is real but is a partly shared embodied source domain (heat) plus translation and contact effects, not independent evidence.

**Translation.** Operand: a bounded container with a scalar level. Operators: fill, heat, cap, vent. Not a source of dynamics, but a source of naming the control interventions.

## 6. Talmy: force dynamics

**Citations.** Talmy, L. (1988). Force dynamics in language and cognition. *Cognitive Science* 12(1), 49-100, doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1201_2 (Crossref confirmed). Talmy, L. (2000). *Toward a Cognitive Semantics*, vol. 1, ch. 7. MIT Press (reported as the later source; not read).

**Their vocabulary `[from abstract/standard account]`, with a secondary summary.** Two force entities: **Agonist** (the focal entity) and **Antagonist** (the opposing one). Each has an **intrinsic force tendency**, toward action or toward rest, and a **relative strength** (stronger/weaker). The **resultant** is action or rest of the agonist. The language encodes these in verbs (*keep, let, make, prevent, help, despite*), conjunctions (*although, because*), and modals (*can, must, may*). Examples in the summary: "The shed kept standing despite the gale" (agonist rest-tendency stronger than antagonist, so rest); "The ball kept rolling because of the wind" (antagonist overrides the rest tendency and the agonist moves).

**Operators.** The four basic **steady-state** patterns are a small truth table: agonist's intrinsic tendency (action/rest) x which party is stronger -> resultant (action/rest); the antagonist is present throughout. Talmy then adds **shifting** patterns, where the antagonist enters or leaves: **onset causation** (a stronger antagonist arrives and changes the agonist's state) versus **extended causation** (a stronger antagonist keeps the agonist from its tendency), and **letting**, which is the antagonist *disengaging* (onset letting, "the plug's coming loose let the water flow") or *staying disengaged* (extended letting). Preventing = a stronger antagonist blocks an action tendency; helping and hindering are treated as further, less central patterns. *(Validation correction: the earlier text had the antagonist "present/absent" inside the steady-state table and letting only as "removal"; presence/absence belongs to the shifting patterns, and letting has an onset and an extended form.)* Calling the whole a "discrete interaction table" is fair for the steady-state core and the onset/extended x causing/letting grid; it is less fair for Talmy's fuller system (secondary agonists, the psychological and social extensions), which is a typology described in prose rather than a closed table. Talmy extends it to psychological force: wanting something vs. inhibition, "I can't bring myself to" (an agonist with an action tendency blocked by an internal antagonist) and to social force: "she forced him to", "the pressure of society".

**Equations.** None numeric. Strength is a binary comparison in the original. Later work (a review citing Mulder 2007) adds degrees of strength.

**Theorems.** None. But the table is **exhaustive over a stated case set**, so statements like "if the antagonist is weaker and the agonist has an action tendency, the agonist acts" are derivable.

**Empirical standing.** Cognitive-linguistics classic; evidence is linguistic (the semantics of causal and modal expressions) plus psychology of causal reasoning, where Wolff's force-dynamic model of causation (Wolff, P. (2007), Representing causation, *J. Experimental Psychology: General* 136(1), 82-111, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.136.1.82, Crossref confirmed in validation; content not read) predicted lay causal judgments from vector composition. Disputed point: some constructions do not fix the outcome (a paper on "apply force" verbs) so that the resultant is not always determined.

**Translation.** This is the closest existing thing in linguistics to a **grammar of forces between entities**. Operands: agents, each carrying a tendency (a drive) with sign (act/rest) and a strength. Operators: the binary interaction "agonist, antagonist -> outcome", which includes the interaction *between two people* (the antagonist can be another person) and *within one person* (two drives). It is a qualitative version of Lewin's vector sum and Miller's approach-avoidance (16), in a typed form. It is the best candidate for the algebra's **interaction table** at the discrete level, to be refined by magnitudes drawn from 16.

## 7. Lexical approaches to motives and values

**Citations and status.** The psycholexical hypothesis (Galton 1884; Allport & Odbert 1936; Goldberg 1990, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.59.6.1216, Crossref confirmed): the most important individual differences become encoded as single words, and the more important, the more synonyms. Applied to values: Bardi, Calogero & Mullen (2008), A new archival approach to the study of values and value-behavior relations: validation of the value lexicon, *J. Applied Psychology* 93(3), 483, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.93.3.483; De Raad, Morales-Vives, Barelds & Van Oudenhoven (2016), Values in a cross-cultural triangle, *J. Cross-Cultural Psychology*, doi:10.1177/0022022116659698; De Raad, Timmerman, Morales-Vives & Renner (2017), The psycho-lexical approach in exploring the field of values, *JCCP*, doi:10.1177/0022022117692677 (all Crossref confirmed in validation; that the 2017 paper is a "reply to Schwartz" is not confirmed) `[content from search summaries only; no primary checked]`. An Estonian study of value-related words self-rated against the Schwartz Values Survey found six factors (benevolence, self-enhancement, broadmindedness, hedonism, conservatism, self-realization) `[search summary]`. I did not find a lexical study aimed at *motives* specifically (as distinct from traits or values); the Saucier "Big Seven" paper I had in mind did not surface and is unconfirmed.

**What they do.** Collect value or motive words from a dictionary, have people rate their applicability, factor-analyse. The output is a taxonomy, not a dynamics. Cross-ref 15 (taxonomies).

**Theorems.** None. The method gives a *claim of completeness*: the lexicon bounds the candidate set.

**Empirical standing.** The Big Five came out of this method and replicate widely; the value-lexicon structure differs from Schwartz's circumplex, in the cited exchange, which is the interesting point: the lexicon-derived and the theory-derived structures disagree and the dispute is open `[search summary]`. Language-bounded: it finds the motives that English (or the sampled language) names.

**Translation.** Method for *testing completeness* of the drive set: "does the lexicon name a drive that the algebra cannot express?" Not a source of the drive set itself.

## 8. Emotion verbs: experiencer and stimulus

**Citations.** Belletti, A. & Rizzi, L. (1988). Psych-verbs and theta-theory. *Natural Language & Linguistic Theory* 6(3), 291-352, doi:10.1007/BF00133902 (Crossref confirmed in validation). Pesetsky, D. (1995). *Zero Syntax: Experiencers and Cascades*. MIT Press. Landau, I. (2010). *The Locative Syntax of Experiencers*. MIT Press, doi:10.7551/mitpress/8387.001.0001 (confirmed; Crossref dates the online edition 2009). Levin, B. (1993). *English Verb Classes and Alternations*. U. Chicago Press. None read.

**Findings `[search summary]`.** Psych verbs split into three Italian classes: experiencer as subject (*temere*, fear), experiencer as direct object (*preoccupare*, worry), and dative experiencer (*piacere*, please). Pesetsky treats the *fear/frighten* contrast as a difference in thematic role (stimulus as subject matter versus as cause), with a hierarchy placing experiencer above subject matter and below cause, which explains why object-experiencer verbs behave causatively. Analysis is disputed (Pesetsky 1994, Chung 2000, Landau 2010).

**Operators.** Two-place predicates over (Experiencer, Stimulus): *fear(E, S)* and *frighten(S, E)*. The same state is expressed with either argument as subject. The causative reading of *frighten* factors it into *cause(S, become-afraid(E))*.

**Equations / theorems.** None in the numeric sense. Syntactic generalisations only.

**Translation.** A typing discipline. Drive terms are **relations** (Experiencer, Stimulus), and some are inherently causal: the stimulus acts on the experiencer. In the algebra, this means a fear operand carries *whom it is directed at*, and the transmission operator (agent S induces drive in agent E) has a linguistic basis. Grammatical form does not say anything about magnitudes.

## 9. Speech-act verbs as social operators

**Citations.** Wierzbicka, A. (1987). *English Speech Act Verbs: A Semantic Dictionary*. Academic Press (Sydney); bibliography pp. 388-394 per catalog (catalogs and the NSM bibliography confirm, validation pass). Wierzbicka, A. (1985). Challenge, dare, defy (paper on the same material, per a search result). Austin (1962) and Searle (1969), cross-ref 04 and 12.

**Findings `[search summary]`.** No speech-act verb is defined by another speech-act verb; the only verb of speaking in explications is SAY, treated as an indefinable. Components are drawn from the same prime inventory: SAY, WANT, THINK, KNOW, GOOD, BAD, DO, CAN. The 2014 volume has a chapter on English speech-act verbs (suggesting, apologizing, complimenting) `[search summary]`. I could not verify the typical shape of an entry; here is how it would run on my reading of the approach, flagged as my construction, not a quotation: *request* = I say: I want you to do X; I think you can do X; I don't say you must; I think you could say no.

**Operators.** Each speech act is a **compound of primes: the speaker's wants and beliefs about the hearer's wants, beliefs and actions, plus a statement**. This is a decomposition of social operators into WANT/KNOW/THINK/CAN over the two parties.

**Theorems.** None.

**Empirical standing.** Semantic analyses by one author's school; no experiments testing them against speakers that I found.

**Translation.** If the algebra's operators are acts between people (Aldred's first framing) and its operands are drives (the new one), this is the route that connects them: **an act = an expression of the speaker's WANT, directed at the hearer's drive state**. A request tries to create a want-to-act in the hearer, a threat tries to create a fear, a promise changes the hearer's CAN. That is the vocabulary for "acts trigger, express and transmit drives" (`notes/2026-10-29-drives-as-the-core.md`).

## What exists vs. what does not

**Exists.**
- A claimed minimal vocabulary (NSM, 65 primes), in which WANT and DON'T WANT, FEEL, GOOD/BAD, KNOW/THINK, CAN, MAYBE, SOMEONE/PEOPLE are primitive and every named emotion is a *script* built from them, with fear fully worked out by Wierzbicka.
- A robust empirical coordinate system: valence and arousal (Jackson, 2,474 languages), valence/power/arousal (Osgood, ~30 communities), valence/power/arousal/novelty (Fontaine, three languages; the novelty axis is chiefly a surprise axis).
- A discrete grammar of forces between entities (Talmy), including internal and interpersonal ones.
- A typed relational form for emotion verbs (experiencer, stimulus, causation).
- A compositional definition of social acts as expressions of wants and beliefs (Wierzbicka's speech-act verbs).
- A folk model of a drive as pressure in a container (Lakoff & Kövecses), supplying lay control operators.

**Does not exist.**
- Any magnitudes, rates or equilibria. Language gives types and composition, not dynamics. Every equation in the algebra has to come from 16, 20-23, 27.
- Any validation of NSM explications against how people actually use or experience emotion words, beyond expert analysis.
- A worked NSM account of the social drives most relevant here (envy, status-seeking, loyalty, curiosity) that could be confirmed; fear and shame (Goddard via Peeters 2020) were read, envy was not found.
- A formal semantics for NSM that makes explications into logical formulas with defined inference (the grammar is informal, so "theorems" would be new work).
- A bridge between Talmy's binary strengths and graded drive levels; the bridge to Miller, Lewin and Revelle's CTA (16) is open work.
- Any prediction of behaviour from these representations.

## Clearly missing important work (added in validation)

- **Ortony, Clore & Collins (1988), *The Cognitive Structure of Emotions*** (Cambridge UP). The OCC model derives emotion types compositionally from appraisals of events, agents and objects (desirability, praiseworthiness, appealingness), which is the nearest thing to a typed composition grammar for emotions outside NSM; it sits directly between section 1 and the appraisal material in 17.
- **Warriner, Kuperman & Brysbaert (2013)**, norms of valence, arousal and dominance for about 14,000 English lemmas (Behavior Research Methods), and Mohammad's NRC VAD lexicon (2018). These give *numbers* for the Osgood/Russell coordinates per word, which the "language gives no magnitudes" verdict overlooks: language-derived magnitudes exist for positions, though still not for dynamics.
- **Harkins & Wierzbicka (eds.) (2001), *Emotions in Crosslinguistic Perspective*** (Mouton de Gruyter) and the NSM emotion-template papers by Goddard: the main sources for explications beyond fear.
- **Lindquist / Barrett psychological construction** papers that frame the Jackson result, and **Scherer's component process model** (for GRID's origin); probably covered in 15 and 17, so cross-reference rather than duplicate.
