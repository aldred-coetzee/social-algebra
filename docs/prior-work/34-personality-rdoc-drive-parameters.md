# Prior work 34: personality as drive parameters, and research taxonomies of drive systems

Date: 2026-10-29. Survey for the social-algebra strand. Cluster: individual differences in drives (traits as the settings of a motivational system) and the research taxonomies (RDoC) that decompose that system. Context: Aldred's aim, "an algebra which expresses basic human drives and group drives, which we can compose into equations, which we can then use to start making theorems and use to control or predict behavior". Cross-references: 15 (taxonomies), 16 (Revelle's CTA, Keramati-Gutkin), 17 (curiosity/fear/desire), 19 (computational agents), 22 (neuroscience).

**Method and honesty note.** Tags: `[read]` = read at source this session; `[from abstract/standard account]` = a search summary, a Crossref abstract, a secondary page, or background knowledge not checked against the primary text. What was actually read: the NIMH RDoC constructs page (fetched, domain/construct list); DeYoung's Cybernetic Big Five Theory (the 2014 author preprint PDF, sections 2-5, read); Fleeson & Law 2015 (PMC full text, first 100k of 140k characters); Huys, Maia & Frank 2016 (PDF, the RL and symptom-network sections). Not read: Mischel & Shoda 1995 (the available PDF is a scanned image with no text layer), Fleeson 2001, Fleeson & Jayawickreme 2015 (no open full text found), Cloninger 1993, Corr 2008, Zuckerman, Eysenck, Ashton & Lee, Gray & McNaughton. DOIs below were each resolved via Crossref this session, unless listed under Unconfirmed.

**Summary.** This cluster contributes one structural idea and one warning. The structural idea: personality traits can be read as the **parameters of a shared motivational machine** (reward approach, threat avoidance, exploration, goal protection, cooperation) rather than as free-standing labels. DeYoung's CB5T states this most fully, but states it verbally: no equations, no operators beyond the five-stage cycle. The only formal parameterisations in the cluster are in computational psychiatry (reward sensitivity as a scalar multiplying reward in an RL update), and these are task-bound. The warning: the trait-to-drive mappings are loose. RDoC deliberately refuses to commit to a drive list, the Big Five are lexical rather than motivational in origin, Cloninger's seven-factor model did not replicate cleanly, and Fleeson's data show that most behavioural variation is within-person, so a trait gives a location and a spread for a distribution, not a prediction of a single act. For the algebra, the useful lesson is that **a person is (drive system structure) x (parameter vector), plus a state that moves within a trait-set distribution**. The parameter vector is what individual differences are; it is where "operand attributes" live. Nothing here is social in the group sense: every model is of one person's system, with Agreeableness, Honesty-Humility, RDoC's Social Processes and the social-learning models added in section 9b the only places other people enter.

## 1. NIMH Research Domain Criteria (RDoC)

**Citations.**
- Insel, T., Cuthbert, B., Garvey, M., Heinssen, R., Pine, D. S., Quinn, K., Sanislow, C., & Wang, P. (2010). Research Domain Criteria (RDoC): Toward a new classification framework for research on mental disorders. *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 167(7), 748-751. doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.09091379 (Crossref: eight authors, 2010-07).
- Cuthbert, B. N. (2014). The RDoC framework: facilitating transition from ICD/DSM to dimensional approaches that integrate neuroscience and psychopathology. *World Psychiatry*, 13(1), 28-35. doi:10.1002/wps.20087.
- Cuthbert, B. N., & Insel, T. R. (2013). Toward the future of psychiatric diagnosis: the seven pillars of RDoC. *BMC Medicine*, 11:126. doi:10.1186/1741-7015-11-126.
- Morris, S. E., Sanislow, C. A., Pacheco, J., Vaidyanathan, U., Gordon, J. A., & Cuthbert, B. N. (2022). Revisiting the seven pillars of RDoC. *BMC Medicine*, 20, 220. doi:10.1186/s12916-022-02414-0 (Crossref; not read).
- Constructs list: NIMH RDoC "Constructs" page, https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/constructs `[read]`.

**Their vocabulary.** *Domain* (a broad functional system), *construct* (a function within it), *subconstruct*, *unit of analysis* (genes, molecules, cells, circuits, physiology, behaviours, self-reports, paradigms). Explicitly **dimensional and transdiagnostic**: the aim is to define functional dimensions that cut across DSM/ICD categories, from normal to disordered, studied at several levels at once. `[from abstract/standard account]` for the aim; the construct list is `[read]`.

**Drives/operands (the six domains as currently listed `[read]`).**
1. **Negative Valence Systems**: Acute Threat ("Fear"), Potential Threat ("Anxiety"), Sustained Threat, Loss, Frustrative Nonreward.
2. **Positive Valence Systems**: Reward Responsiveness (reward anticipation, initial response to reward, reward satiation); Reward Learning (probabilistic and reinforcement learning, reward prediction error, habit); Reward Valuation (reward probability, delay, effort).
3. **Cognitive Systems**: Attention, Perception, Declarative Memory, Language, Cognitive Control (goal selection/updating/maintenance, response selection/inhibition, performance monitoring), Working Memory.
4. **Social Processes**: Affiliation and Attachment; Social Communication (reception/production of facial and non-facial communication); Perception and Understanding of Self (agency, self-knowledge); Perception and Understanding of Others (animacy perception, action perception, understanding mental states).
5. **Arousal and Regulatory Systems**: Arousal, Circadian Rhythms, Sleep-Wakefulness.
6. **Sensorimotor Systems**: Motor Actions (planning/selection, sensorimotor dynamics, initiation, execution, inhibition/termination), Agency and Ownership, Habit, Innate Motor Patterns.

The page is the current list; the original matrix had five domains and Sensorimotor Systems was added as the sixth in **January 2019** (NIMH science update, "Sensorimotor Domain Added to the RDoC Framework", 2019; found by search, the date in its URL and summary, not opened) `[validation pass]`. The live page also lists Cognitive Systems subconstructs not given above (Perception: visual, auditory, olfactory/somatosensory/multimodal; Working Memory: active maintenance, flexible updating, limited capacity, interference control) and "Habit" appears twice, once under Positive Valence (Reward Learning) and once under Sensorimotor `[read, validation pass]`. Note what is **absent**: there is no domain for curiosity/exploration (information-seeking), no construct for status/dominance, no construct for sex, hunger or other homeostatic needs, and no construct for belonging-as-threat or for guilt/shame. Affiliation is the only social drive; the rest of Social Processes are perceptual and communicative capacities, not drives. Compare the taxonomies in 15 and the language-of-drives file 32.

**Operators / equations.** None. RDoC is a classification, not a model. It prescribes a *grid* (constructs x units of analysis) to be filled with measurements. `[read]` (construct list); `[from abstract/standard account]` (grid design).

**Theorems or derived predictions.** None. The framework's testable claim is methodological: that functionally defined dimensions will show better biological correspondence than DSM categories. That is a programme claim, not a theorem.

**Empirical standing.** Be blunt: RDoC is a funding-and-research framework, not an empirically validated model of drives, and the NIMH itself treats it as a work in progress to be revised (the 2022 "Revisiting the seven pillars" paper is a self-critique `[from title only]`). Its constructs are chosen by expert workshop, with the criterion that there be convergent behavioural and circuit evidence, so they reflect what neuroscience can currently assay (threat circuitry, striatal reward prediction error) rather than what a theory of motivation needs. Known critiques from the standard account, not checked this session: construct-level self-report and behavioural measures correlate weakly with each other, reflecting the general problem that task measures have poor reliability for individual differences; and the framework under-represents context and development. `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Operand/operator translation.**
- It is a menu of candidate primitive drives with a pedigree: Fear (acute threat), Anxiety (potential threat), Loss, Frustration (frustrative nonreward), Wanting (reward anticipation), Liking (initial response), Satiation, Effort-valuation, Delay-valuation, Attachment. Several are not drives but *parameters or sub-processes* (delay, effort, probability are arguments to a valuation, i.e. discount parameters; reward prediction error is a learning signal). That is itself a design lesson: RDoC separates **drive (positive/negative valence systems)** from **valuation parameters** from **learning**. An algebra could follow: operands = valence-system states; parameters = valuation discount functions; operators = learning updates.
- The distinction **acute versus potential versus sustained threat** is a clean three-way split of fear by time scale and certainty (immediate and certain / anticipated and uncertain / prolonged), usable as three operands or one operand with a time-scale index.
- Social Processes gives only Affiliation and Attachment as a motivated social system. Everything social in the algebra beyond affiliation has to come from elsewhere (see 20, 21).

## 2. DeYoung: Cybernetic Big Five Theory (CB5T)

**Citation.** DeYoung, C. G. (2015). Cybernetic Big Five Theory. *Journal of Research in Personality*, 56, 33-58. doi:10.1016/j.jrp.2014.07.004 (Crossref: first online 2014; issue date 2015-06). Companion: DeYoung, C. G. (2014). A cybernetic big five theory for personality psychology. *Personality and Individual Differences*, 60, S18. doi:10.1016/j.paid.2013.07.381 (Crossref; a one-page conference abstract in a supplement, not a separate paper; not read). Read this session: the 2014 author preprint, https://scottbarrykaufman.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/DeYoung-2014-CB5T-JRP.pdf, which matches the DOI of the JRP article. Pagination 33-58 confirmed by Crossref.

**Their vocabulary** `[read]`. *Cybernetic cycle* of five stages: (1) goal activation, (2) action selection, (3) action, (4) outcome interpretation, (5) goal comparison. *Goals* (cybernetic reference values; "attractor" if approach, "repulsor" if avoidance), *interpretations*, *strategies* (called "operators" in the problem-solving tradition, Newell & Simon 1972). *Traits* = "relatively stable parameters of evolved psychobiological cybernetic mechanisms"; *characteristic adaptations* = learned contents of memory (goals, interpretations, strategies), "attractor states" of the system. *Psychological entropy* (uncertainty about "What is happening?" and "What should I do?", after Hirsh, Mar & Peterson 2012). *Metatraits*: Stability and Plasticity. *Aspects*: Assertiveness/Enthusiasm (Extraversion), Volatility/Withdrawal (Neuroticism), Intellect/Openness, Industriousness/Orderliness, Compassion/Politeness.

The central definition `[read]`: the Big Five are the major dimensions of covariation among traits because "each of the five traits corresponds to interpersonal variation in one of the major functional categories of intrapersonal mechanism involved in the operation of the human cybernetic system." Explicit warning from the author: a one-to-one mapping of one trait per cycle stage "will not work" because the mechanisms run in parallel and influence multiple stages; the cycle is serial only because action is a bottleneck.

**Drives/operands: the mapping `[read]` (Table 1 and sections 4.1-4.5, 5).**

| Trait | Cybernetic function | Notes (author's) |
|---|---|---|
| Extraversion | Behavioural exploration and engagement with specific rewards (goals to approach) | Most obviously stage 1 (goal activation) and also stage 5; incentive reward, BAS. Aspects (Table 1): Assertiveness = "incentive reward sensitivity: drive toward goals" (wanting, dopamine); Enthusiasm = "consummatory reward sensitivity" (mostly liking, opioids), but the author says Enthusiasm "encompasses individual differences in desire as well as enjoyment" |
| Neuroticism | Defensive responses to uncertainty, threat and punishment | Most obviously stage 5 (goal comparison); mismatch triggers defensive systems; sensitivity of those systems. Aspects (Table 1): Volatility = active defence (mapped to RST's FFFS), Withdrawal = passive avoidance, inhibition in response to uncertainty or error (mapped to RST's BIS) |
| Openness/Intellect | Cognitive exploration and engagement with information | Reward value of information; "at the core of Openness/Intellect are curiosity, imagination, creativity" (text) |
| Conscientiousness | Protection of non-immediate or abstract goals and strategies from disruption | Most obviously the first three stages; chimpanzees are the only other species in which a Conscientiousness-like factor has been found, suggesting the mechanisms evolved relatively recently |
| Agreeableness | Altruism and cooperation; coordination of goals, interpretations and strategies with those of others | Not strictly necessary for the basic cycle; "the only one of the five that exists because humans are social animals". Compassion = automatic affect (empathy, care), Politeness = top-down restraint of aggression |
| Stability (metatrait, N-, C, A) | Protection of goals, interpretations and strategies from disruption by impulses | Serotonin (hypothesised as the major substrate) |
| Plasticity (metatrait, E, O) | Exploration: creation of new goals, interpretations and strategies | Dopamine |

Three further structural claims `[read]`: (i) **reward and punishment/threat are defined cybernetically** as stimuli that signal progress toward or definite inability to attain a goal, and threat as a decrease in the probability of attaining it, so threat and reward are relations between a stimulus and a goal, not intrinsic properties of the stimulus; (ii) **the unknown is both threatening and promising**: increases in psychological entropy are innately threatening (Neuroticism) and also act as incentive reward (exploration, Plasticity) because they signal the possibility of reducing entropy; (iii) a testable hypothesis the author states: after subgoal attainment, Assertiveness and Enthusiasm should make **opposite** predictions of further goal pursuit (Enthusiasm predicting coasting).

**Operators / equations.** None. No equations appear in the preprint `[read]`. The "operators" in the text are the Newell-Simon problem-space sense (strategies mapping current state to goal state). The structure is a loop with a comparator: state s, goal g, mismatch m = d(s, g), threshold behaviour on m (continue, change strategy, abandon goal, activate defence). This is the Powers/Carver-Scheier lineage covered in 16; CB5T adds *which person-level parameter scales which stage*.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Verbal hypotheses only: dopamine-linked Assertiveness predicts wanting-type measures; opioid-linked Enthusiasm predicts liking; Neuroticism predicts reactivity to error, threat and expectancy violation; Agreeableness predicts experiencing others' distress as mismatch; Plasticity and Stability are complementary yet in "dynamic tension" (the opposite of stability is instability, of plasticity is rigidity, and the two are positively correlated in single-rater data but "typically uncorrelated in multi-informant studies", i.e. nearly uncorrelated given high-quality measurement `[read, wording corrected in validation]`). No derivation of the Big Five count (five) from the model; the argument runs backwards from the five to five function classes. The author concedes the theory is a framework: "to delineate all of the processes" in detail is beyond it `[read]`, paraphrased.

**Empirical standing.** Mixed, and the key points are the author's own. Extraversion-reward-sensitivity: the author calls the evidence "considerable" but the fMRI/EEG studies "small, rendering these findings less than conclusive" `[read]`. The dopamine-Extraversion link rests substantially on Depue & Collins 1999 (Behavioral and Brain Sciences, doi:10.1017/s0140525x99002046, Crossref) and later pharmacological and genetic work; the neuroanatomy findings (Conscientiousness and dorsolateral PFC volume, Extraversion and medial orbitofrontal volume) come from a small number of structural MRI studies by DeYoung and colleagues `[read]` as described in the paper, with small samples by modern standards. Brain-structure personality correlates have poor replicability in large samples: Avinun, Israel, Knodt & Hariri (2020), *NeuroImage*, 220, 117092, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.117092, found no significant Big Five-morphometry associations in 1,107 students beyond one weak, unsupported one, concluding any effects are "very small" `[abstract read, validation pass]`; cf. Marek et al. (2022), Reproducible brain-wide association studies require thousands of individuals, *Nature*, 603, 654-660, doi:10.1038/s41586-022-04492-9 (Crossref). Conscientiousness is, in the author's words, "the least well understood, mechanistically." CB5T is a theory of *traits and characteristic adaptations*, so behaviour in a situation is predicted only through goals and interpretations that the theory leaves to the person's memory contents; it is not predictive in the sense of an engineering model.

**Operand/operator translation.** This is the best single source for the "individual differences as parameters" slot:
- A person = a cybernetic loop **plus a parameter vector** (reward sensitivity E, threat/uncertainty reactivity N, information-seeking O, goal-protection C, cooperative coordination A, with Stability and Plasticity as two global gains).
- Each parameter scales a *named drive*: E scales wanting/approach; N scales avoidance/defence; O scales curiosity; A scales coordination with others' goals. **The trio mapping is this survey's, not DeYoung's**: curiosity ~ O (and Plasticity), fear ~ N, desire ~ E. DeYoung never lines up curiosity, fear and desire as a set; what he does say is consistent with it (curiosity is named as core to Openness/Intellect; Neuroticism is defence against threat, with Volatility as fight-flight and Withdrawal as anxious inhibition; desire sits in Extraversion, in Assertiveness as "drive toward goals" and also in Enthusiasm, which he says "encompasses individual differences in desire"). So "desire ~ Assertiveness" alone is too narrow. That is a mapping of *individual variation* in those drives, not a model of the drives (see 17).
- The comparator is the operator: mismatch between interpreted state and goal drives the next cycle, and its output class (continue, switch strategy, abandon goal, defend) is a discrete decision. That is composable with the Miller/Atkinson forms in 16.
- Agreeableness is the only parameter that takes **another agent's goals as input** ("coordination of goals... with those of others"), and the author's examples (others' distress registered as a mismatch) give the minimal two-agent coupling: agent i's comparator sees agent j's state. That is the bridge to dyadic models in 20.
- Gap: the theory is **diagnostic** (it explains why five traits) rather than **generative** (it gives no dynamics).

## 3. Big Five and HEXACO and their drive links

**Citations.**
- Ashton, M. C., & Lee, K. (2007). Empirical, theoretical, and practical advantages of the HEXACO model of personality structure. *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 11(2), 150-166. doi:10.1177/1088868306294907 (Crossref).
- DeYoung, C. G., Quilty, L. C., & Peterson, J. B. (2007). Between facets and domains: 10 aspects of the Big Five. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 93(5), 880-896. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.93.5.880 (Crossref).
- Carver, C. S., & White, T. L. (1994). Behavioral inhibition, behavioral activation, and affective responses to impending reward and punishment: The BIS/BAS scales. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 67(2), 319-333. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.67.2.319 (Crossref).
- Nettle, D. (2006). The evolution of personality variation in humans and other animals. *American Psychologist*, 61(6), 622-631. doi:10.1037/0003-066x.61.6.622 (Crossref).

**Their vocabulary.** Big Five (Extraversion, Neuroticism, Openness, Conscientiousness, Agreeableness) from lexical analysis; HEXACO's six are Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, eXtraversion, Agreeableness (versus anger), Conscientiousness, Openness to Experience. It adds **Honesty-Humility** (facets sincerity, fairness, greed avoidance, modesty), and redefines Agreeableness (forgiveness, gentleness, flexibility, patience) and Emotionality (fearfulness, anxiety, dependence, sentimentality) `[from abstract/standard account]`. Facets, aspects, domains.

**Drives/operands.** The Big Five did not come from motivation theory; they are the factors of trait-descriptive words, so their link to drives is *post hoc* `[read]`, DeYoung says the lexical origin as a premise of CB5T (traits identified by analysis of descriptors, then interpreted). Standard links, hypotheses not identities `[from abstract/standard account]`:
- Extraversion ~ reward/approach sensitivity (BAS); Neuroticism ~ punishment/threat sensitivity; BIS/BAS scales by Carver & White measure self-reported drive-like dispositions (BIS: reactions to anticipated punishment; BAS: drive, fun-seeking, reward responsiveness) `[from abstract/standard account]`.
- HEXACO Honesty-Humility ~ **reciprocal-altruism-avoiding-exploitation** (Ashton & Lee tie H to fairness and non-exploitation and, with Agreeableness, to reciprocal altruism, and tie Emotionality to kin altruism) `[from abstract/standard account]`; this is the first trait axis in this cluster that is intrinsically a *social stance toward others' interests*, plausible as an operand-level parameter for "how much do I weight another's payoff" (cf. 21 on social preferences).
- Nettle (2006) treats the Big Five as the outputs of **trade-offs** (e.g. extraversion: more mating success and exploration against more injury risk and mortality), explaining why variation persists. `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Operators / equations.** None; all are factor models (linear latent-variable structure on questionnaire items). `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Theorems.** None.

**Empirical standing.** The Big Five structure is the most replicated descriptive structure in personality in English and many languages, but 5 vs 6 factors is a live dispute: Ashton & Lee argue HEXACO is more replicable across languages, which the Big Five camp contests (the CB5T preprint itself notes that a six-factor solution "may be more replicable than the Big Five across" some languages `[read]`). Trait-to-biology correlations are modest and often small-sample; trait-to-behaviour correlations for single acts are modest (the classic "personality coefficient" of about .3, `[from abstract/standard account]`). Stability of rank-order is high across adulthood; mean-level change is small.

**Operand/operator translation.** Use the Big Five/HEXACO as the *measurement basis for a parameter vector*, not as primitives: they give a replicable, low-dimensional coordinate system for individual differences. Honesty-Humility and Agreeableness are the candidates for parameters that couple an agent to others.

## 4. Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (Gray, McNaughton, Corr)

**Citations.** Gray, J. A., & McNaughton, N. (2000). *The Neuropsychology of Anxiety* (2nd ed.). Oxford University Press (book; the Crossref chapter entries I found are 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198522713.003.0002 "Ethology and anxiety" and McNaughton 2024, 10.1093/oso/9780198843313.003.0012 "Anxiety and personality"; neither read). Corr, P. J. (Ed.) (2008). *The Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory of Personality*. Cambridge University Press: introduction doi:10.1017/cbo9780511819384.002, Corr & McNaughton, "Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory and personality", pp. 155-187, doi:10.1017/cbo9780511819384.006 (Crossref; not read). Cross-ref 16 (Gray as inhibition model).

**Their vocabulary.** Behavioural Approach System (BAS), Behavioural Inhibition System (BIS), Fight-Flight-Freeze System (FFFS). In the revised theory `[from abstract/standard account, via a search summary]`: BAS responds to all appetitive cues; FFFS to all aversive stimuli (the fear system, with active avoidance and escape); BIS handles **goal conflict** (e.g. approach-avoidance, mixed signals), resolving it by risk assessment and anxiety, and is distinct from fear. Gray's original (1970s-80s) version had BIS for conditioned punishment and frustrative non-reward.

**Drives/operands.** Three motivational systems whose *sensitivities* are the personality dimensions: BAS ~ Extraversion/impulsivity; FFFS ~ fear-proneness; BIS ~ anxiety/Neuroticism. Corr's programme: personality dimensions are individual differences in the sensitivity of these systems. `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Operators / equations.** The theory is neural and functional; no behaviour equation. Joint-subsystem reading (e.g. BIS as comparator that raises the weight of avoidance when BAS and FFFS both fire) is verbal. `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Theorems.** None. Predictions are of the form: high-BAS individuals show more approach to cues of reward, high-BIS more behavioural inhibition under conflict.

**Empirical standing.** Contested on measurement: the BIS/BAS questionnaires (Carver & White) pre-date the BIS/FFFS split and do not cleanly separate BIS from FFFS (Heym, Ferguson & Lawrence 2008, An evaluation of the relationship between Gray's revised RST and Eysenck's PEN: distinguishing BIS and FFFS in Carver and White's BIS/BAS scales, *Personality and Individual Differences*, 45, 709-715, doi:10.1016/j.paid.2008.07.013, Crossref; not read); one search-summarised developmental study found BAS lab and caregiver measures converged with physiological correlates while BIS measures did not `[from abstract/standard account, search snippet]`. The revision of 2000 produced hypotheses that conflict with predictions of the original theory (Pickering & Corr, as summarised `[search snippet]`). The three-system structure is influential and a good fit to RDoC's valence split (Positive vs Negative Valence, with Potential Threat resembling BIS).

**Operand/operator translation.** Strongest compact decomposition into three drive operands (approach, fear, conflict-anxiety), with individual sensitivity as a gain on each. The BIS-as-conflict-detector idea gives a **second-order operator**: a quantity that is not a drive but is computed from two drives being simultaneously active. This echoes the Miller gradient equilibrium in 16. A candidate algebraic rule: conflict(a, f) = min or product of the activation of approach and fear.

## 5. Cloninger's psychobiological model of temperament and character

**Citation.** Cloninger, C. R., Svrakic, D. M., & Przybeck, T. R. (1993). A psychobiological model of temperament and character. *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 50(12), 975. doi:10.1001/archpsyc.1993.01820240059008 (Crossref; abstract not available via Crossref; not read). Earlier three-dimension version (1986-87) `[from abstract/standard account]`, not cited by DOI.

**Their vocabulary.** Four **temperament** dimensions: Novelty Seeking, Harm Avoidance, Reward Dependence, Persistence; three **character** dimensions: Self-Directedness, Cooperativeness, Self-Transcendence. Temperament is claimed heritable and tied to monoamine systems: novelty seeking ~ dopamine (behavioural activation), harm avoidance ~ serotonin (behavioural inhibition), reward dependence ~ norepinephrine (maintenance) `[from abstract/standard account]`. Measured by the Temperament and Character Inventory (TCI).

**Drives/operands.** Novelty Seeking (approach to novelty/appetitive cues), Harm Avoidance (inhibition to aversive cues), Reward Dependence (maintenance of behaviour on social reward cues), Persistence. Closest to a drive-parameter model of the pre-Big-Five generation.

**Operators / equations.** None; a dimensional/ neurotransmitter-mapped typology, with a combinatorial scheme of high/low on the first three temperaments yielding eight personality-disorder-like profiles. `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Theorems.** None.

**Empirical standing: weak, and be blunt.** The seven-factor structure has not replicated cleanly. Herbst, Zonderman, McCrae & Costa (2000), Do the dimensions of the Temperament and Character Inventory map a simple genetic architecture?, *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 157(8), 1285-1290, doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.157.8.1285 (Crossref; abstract read in validation; note the authors are the NEO/Big Five group, a rival camp) reported, in 946 Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging participants, that the TCI factor structure in a large Baltimore sample did not reveal the hypothesised structure and that candidate genetic associations (Novelty Seeking, Harm Avoidance) were null, concluding "no support for the temperament-character model at either the biological or psychological level." **Corrected in validation:** the follow-up was not a reply from Cloninger's group. doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.158.8.1339 is a letter by Lichtermann, Ekelund, Peltonen & Järvelin (Finnish sample) reporting clear Harm Avoidance and Novelty Seeking factors; Herbst and colleagues replied (doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.158.8.1339-a, pp. 1339-1340, from search result, not opened) that the difference was method (four factors from 12 temperament subscales vs seven from all 25), that restricting their own analysis to four factors also recovered a Novelty Seeking factor, but that the gene associations still did not replicate. Other samples (Japanese expedition members, Finnish cohort) reproduce four temperament and three character factors partly; Turkish data reproduce Novelty Seeking and Harm Avoidance but weaker Reward Dependence and Persistence `[search snippets]`. Overall: **Harm Avoidance is robust and overlaps Neuroticism; Novelty Seeking is moderately robust; Reward Dependence and Persistence are weak.** The TCI's three character scales largely overlap Big Five Conscientiousness and Agreeableness. Twin-study relationships to Big Five are in Gillespie, Cloninger, Heath & Martin (2003), *Personality and Individual Differences*, doi:10.1016/s0191-8869(03)00042-4 (Crossref; not read). A structural MRI paper links TCI dimensions to brain volumes (Gardini, Cloninger & Venneri, 2009, *Brain Research Bulletin*, doi:10.1016/j.brainresbull.2009.03.005, Crossref; not read). The neurotransmitter-to-trait assignments are not supported by the later pharmacology and are best treated as historical.

**Operand/operator translation.** Useful as a **historical precedent** for naming drive parameters after neurotransmitter systems, and as a caution: that mapping failed to generalise. Do not build on the monoamine assignments.

## 6. Eysenck's arousal theory and Zuckerman's sensation seeking

**Citations.**
- Eysenck, H. J. (1967). *The Biological Basis of Personality*. Thomas. (Standard citation; no DOI.) Eysenck & Eysenck (1985), *Personality and Individual Differences: A Natural Science Approach*, Plenum. (Standard citation; Crossref search returned nothing relevant; not read.)
- Zuckerman, M., Buchsbaum, M. S., & Murphy, D. L. (1980). Sensation seeking and its biological correlates. *Psychological Bulletin*, 88(1), 187-214. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.88.1.187 (Crossref, three authors; a review; not read).
- Zuckerman, M., Kuhlman, D. M., Joireman, J., Teta, P., & Kraft, M. (1993). A comparison of three structural models for personality: The Big Three, the Big Five, and the Alternative Five. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 65(4), 757-768. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.65.4.757 (Crossref; not read).

**Their vocabulary.** Eysenck: Extraversion-Introversion, Neuroticism, Psychoticism (PEN); the **ascending reticular activating system** sets cortical arousal; introverts have higher baseline arousal (and therefore seek less stimulation), extraverts lower (and seek more); neuroticism reflects limbic (visceral brain) reactivity. Zuckerman: **Sensation Seeking** (thrill and adventure, experience seeking, disinhibition, boredom susceptibility), optimal level of arousal/ stimulation, "Alternative Five" structure (Impulsive Sensation Seeking, Neuroticism-Anxiety, Aggression-Hostility, Activity, Sociability; measured by the ZKPQ). `[from abstract/standard account]` for both.

**Drives/operands.** A single **arousal level** with an **optimal setting** that differs by person: the individual difference is the set-point (an optimal-arousal homeostat), and behaviour (stimulus-seeking, withdrawal) is the regulator. This is Hull-like drive reduction with a person-specific set-point, and Berlyne's arousal-optimum in the curiosity literature (cross-ref 15, 17).

**Operators / equations.** Eysenck gave no equations; the classic form is an inverted-U performance/hedonic function of arousal with a person-specific optimum, qualitatively `[from abstract/standard account]`. Revelle's CTA (in 16) is the quantitative descendant that replaces the single arousal axis by a dynamic of tendencies, and Revelle & Condon (2015) "A model for personality at three levels", *Journal of Research in Personality*, 56, 70-81, doi:10.1016/j.jrp.2014.12.006 (Crossref; not read), is a recent framing; the Dynamics of Action equations themselves are in file 16 `[read]` there.

**Theorems.** None in the formal sense. Predictions, e.g. introverts show greater cortical arousal responses to stimulation or sensory-threshold differences, are the testable consequences.

**Empirical standing.** Blunt: the **arousal theory of introversion-extraversion has weak support**; later reviews of direct arousal measures (EEG, electrodermal) find mixed or null results, and Gray's reinforcement-sensitivity account displaced it as the neural explanation (Gray's theory itself is only partly supported, see section 4) `[from abstract/standard account]`. Zuckerman's sensation seeking is better supported as a **trait scale** with reasonably consistent correlates (risk-taking, age and sex differences, some dopaminergic and MAO correlates, `[from abstract/standard account]`), and it is a good fit to the dopamine/novelty-reward family (Extraversion/Plasticity; Novelty Seeking). The Alternative Five (Zuckerman) is one competing structure to the Big Five, not widely adopted.

**Operand/operator translation.** Operand: an agent with a **target level** for some internal variable (stimulation, arousal, uncertainty) and a gain. This gives the algebra a way to say that *the same environment is a different demand on different agents*, via the set-point offset. It is the cleanest "personality = offset parameter in a homeostat" idea, though empirically weak for arousal per se.

## 7. Whole Trait Theory: traits as density distributions of states

**Citations.**
- Fleeson, W. (2001). Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality: Traits as density distributions of states. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 80(6), 1011-1027. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.80.6.1011 (Crossref; abstract not available; not read; summarised via secondary sources).
- Fleeson, W., & Gallagher, P. (2009). The implications of Big Five standing for the distribution of trait manifestation in behavior: Fifteen experience-sampling studies and a meta-analysis. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 97(6), 1097-1114. doi:10.1037/a0016786 (Crossref confirms title and pages; not read).
- Fleeson, W., & Jayawickreme, E. (2015). Whole Trait Theory. *Journal of Research in Personality*, 56, 82-92. doi:10.1016/j.jrp.2014.10.009 (Crossref and a search result; no open full text found; not read).
- Fleeson, W., & Law, M. K. (2015). Trait enactments as density distributions: The role of actors, situations, and observers in explaining stability and variability. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 109(6), 1090-1104. doi:10.1037/a0039517 (Crossref; PMC full text, the first 100k of 140k characters, `[read]`).

**Their vocabulary.** *State* (a momentary enactment of a trait's content, rated on trait adjectives), *density distribution* (a person's distribution of states over time, described by **location (central tendency), size (spread), and shape (skew, kurtosis)**), *trait* = the distribution, *descriptive part* versus *explanatory part* (social-cognitive mechanisms), *actor*, *situation*, *observer*. Whole Trait Theory (WTT): the descriptive side is density distributions; the explanatory side is a set of **social-cognitive mechanisms** (goals, interpretations of situations, affect) that produce the states. `[read]` for the Fleeson-Law paper's statement of this; `[from abstract/standard account]` for the WTT paper.

**Drives/operands.** The operand is the **state vector at time t** (one entry per Big Five dimension), and the person is the *distribution of that vector over time*. Drives are not named; the paper assigns the production of states to goals and construals `[read]` ("social cognitive mechanisms make good candidates", and the paper "does not test the mechanisms it names").

**Operators / equations.** The distributional description: trait_k(person) = the density f_k(x) over states x within a time window, summarised by mean mu, SD sigma, shape; stability = test-retest of mu and sigma. No dynamic equation links successive states. `[read]` for the use of location/size/shape and split-half stability; no generative model is given in the Fleeson-Law paper, and the WTT explanatory part is verbal.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Empirical regularities, not derivations: central tendencies of the distributions are highly stable individual differences, and the variability itself is an individual difference.

**Empirical standing (read this paper; the other three are secondary).** In the Fleeson-Law lab study (97 targets, 183 observers, 20 hour-long lab sessions per target in standardised situations), with observer ratings, **most variability in trait enactment is within-person**: within-person shares ranged from 57 percent (emotional stability) to 78 percent (extraversion; agreeableness 60, intellect 66, conscientiousness 73). First-half to second-half correlations of distribution means were .71-.87 in the averages section (extraversion .79, agreeableness .82, conscientiousness .71, emotional stability .87, intellect .84; rater not stated there), and .66-.81 for observer ratings in Table 4 (corrected in validation: the survey had attributed the first set to observers), and the same stability held across changing lab situations, from which the authors conclude stability reflects the actor, not the situations. `[read]`. Caveats stated by the authors `[read]`: lab artificiality, self-report and rater effects (agreement was weak for some traits, e.g. agreeableness .03 between targets and primary observers and .11 between primary and secondary observers; corrected in validation, where the survey had run the two together as a target-observer range), situation was standardised not measured, scheduling not fully random, mechanisms untested. From the Fleeson & Gallagher meta-analysis as summarised in a search result: within-person variability is so high that a typical person manifests nearly all levels of all traits in daily behaviour, with stable individual differences in the mean and also in the variability, skew and kurtosis `[search summary]`. The two criticisms (is stability the person or the situations they encounter; is the variability real or a rating artifact) are acknowledged, and Fleeson & Law is the attempt to answer them.

**Operand/operator translation.** This is the **bridge from state dynamics to trait**, and it states precisely what a trait parameter can and cannot predict:
- A trait is the **stationary distribution of a state process**, not the value of the state. In the algebra, the drive level at time t is the operand; the person's trait vector is the *parameters of the distribution it is drawn from* (location, scale, shape). Individual difference parameters therefore belong to the **generator**, not to the instantaneous operand.
- This gives a quantitative target: a theory with drive dynamics (16, 19) should *reproduce* the stationary distribution of states Fleeson measures, including the stable variance. That is a validation criterion, not a theory.
- Most variance is within-person (57-78 percent here): any prediction of an individual's next act from trait alone has a low ceiling. That matches the survey-wide warning in the first synthesis about individual-level prediction (cross-ref 07).

## 8. Mischel & Shoda: CAPS and if-then signatures

**Citations.**
- Mischel, W., & Shoda, Y. (1995). A cognitive-affective system theory of personality: Reconceptualizing situations, dispositions, dynamics, and invariance in personality structure. *Psychological Review*, 102(2), 246-268. doi:10.1037/0033-295x.102.2.246 (Crossref).
- Shoda, Y., Mischel, W., & Wright, J. C. (1994). Intraindividual stability in the organization and patterning of behavior: Incorporating psychological situations into the idiographic analysis of personality. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 67(4), 674. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.67.4.674 (Crossref).
- Mischel, W., & Shoda, Y. (1998). Reconciling processing dynamics and personality dispositions. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 49, 229. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.49.1.229 (Crossref).

I could not read the 1995 paper: the open PDF is a scanned image without a text layer (confirmed in validation). Everything below is `[from abstract/standard account]`, via search summaries that agree with each other. A readable secondary account, read in validation: Ayduk, Ö., & Gyurak, A. (2008). Applying the cognitive-affective processing systems approach to conceptualizing rejection sensitivity. *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*, 2(5), 2016-2033. doi:10.1111/j.1751-9004.2008.00143.x `[read]` (PMC2772175): it lists the five unit types (encodings/construals; expectations/beliefs; feelings/emotions; goals/values; competencies/self-regulatory abilities), describes CAPS as an activation network with excitatory and inhibitory links, and gives a social if-then signature: high rejection-sensitive people are "aggressive if rejected but accommodative if they are not". Also relevant, not read: Shoda, Y., LeeTiernan, S., & Mischel, W. (2002). Personality as a dynamical system: Emergence of stability and distinctiveness from intra- and interpersonal interactions. *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 6(4), 316-325. doi:10.1207/S15327957PSPR0604_06 (Crossref), the CAPS network simulation extended to two interacting people, i.e. the nearest thing in this cluster to a dyadic personality dynamic (cross-ref 20).

**Their vocabulary.** *Cognitive-affective personality system (CAPS)*; **cognitive-affective units (CAUs)**: encodings (construals of self, others, situations), expectancies and beliefs, affects, goals and values, competencies and self-regulatory plans; **situation features**; **if...then behavioural signature**: a person's stable profile of behaviour conditional on psychological situation type ("if A, then she X, but if E, then she Y"), found in a residential summer camp (children's aggression varied by situation type but the *pattern* was stable) `[from abstract/standard account]`. The units are held to be linked in a **network of activation and inhibition**, with individual differences in the *accessibility* of units and in the *organisation* (connection weights) of the network; processing is described as connectionist. `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Drives/operands.** CAUs. Goals and affects are among them; no named drives. Situations are *psychological*, i.e. the encoding the person produces, not the external stimulus (a situation can be thought, imagined or planned `[search summary]`).

**Operators / equations.** The 1995 paper is a conceptual theory; its formalisation is verbal plus a later **connectionist network** version (Shoda & Mischel and colleagues, 1990s; the standard account `[from abstract/standard account]`, not cited by DOI here). The operator structure is a **conditional map**: behaviour = signature(situation type), with the signature being the person's invariant. In notation: B = f_P(S), where f_P is a person-specific function from situation features to behaviour, an equation of the form that "the person" is a function, stable across time, and not a fixed value.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** The substantive prediction: the same person is predictably variable; two people with equal average aggression can differ in their situation-conditional profile; **within-person variation is not noise but pattern**. Tested in summer-camp data (Shoda, Mischel & Wright 1994) `[from abstract/standard account]`.

**Empirical standing.** The finding that stable if-then profiles exist is supported by camp data and later work, but the if-then profiles are measured within one sample and context, and critics note that Big Five consistency and situational variation can be reconciled without a signature (WTT's response, above, treats variability as a distribution and leaves the conditioning to future work). CAPS is a framework, not a quantitatively fitted model. Fleeson & Law note that interpretations of situations "make a likely candidate" for the actor's contribution, and they report that observers' ratings of the same standardised situation differ across actors `[read]`: this is the evidence that a conditional structure is warranted but not a test of CAPS.

**Operand/operator translation.** This is the nearest thing in the cluster to an **operator model**: an individual is a function from situation (what is encoded) to response. For the algebra: an agent's *type* is a map from situation features (which could include the other agent's act) to an action or drive change. It is the same object as a strategy in game theory (21) or a transition table in a role algebra (01), with the novel point that **the situation is the agent's own encoding**. It supports a clean separation: the algebra's operands could be *encoded situations* with an agent-specific encoding function, rather than objective states.

## 9. Computational psychiatry of reward and threat drives (Huys, Rutledge and relatives)

**Citations.**
- Huys, Q. J. M., Maia, T. V., & Frank, M. J. (2016). Computational psychiatry as a bridge from neuroscience to clinical applications. *Nature Neuroscience*, 19(3), 404-413. doi:10.1038/nn.4238 `[read]` (PDF, the RL and symptom-network sections).
- Huys, Q. J. M., Pizzagalli, D. A., Bogdan, R., & Dayan, P. (2013). Mapping anhedonia onto reinforcement learning: a behavioural meta-analysis. *Biology of Mood & Anxiety Disorders*, 3:12. doi:10.1186/2045-5380-3-12 (Crossref; not read, characterised through Huys et al. 2016).
- Huys, Daw, & Dayan (2015). Depression: A decision-theoretic analysis. *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, 38, 1-23. doi:10.1146/annurev-neuro-071714-033928 (Crossref abstract read, not the full text).
- Huys, Eshel, O'Nions, Sheridan, Dayan, & Roiser (2012). Bonsai trees in your head: how the Pavlovian system sculpts goal-directed choices by pruning decision trees. *PLoS Computational Biology*, 8(3). doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002410 (Crossref; title as given by Crossref "Bonsai Trees in Your Head: How the Pavlovian System Sculpts Goal-Directed Choice..."; not read).
- Rutledge, R. B., Skandali, N., Dayan, P., & Dolan, R. J. (2014). A computational and neural model of momentary subjective well-being. *PNAS*, 111(33), 12252-12257. doi:10.1073/pnas.1407535111 (Crossref; model equation checked against PMC4143018 in validation).
- Huys, Q. J. M., & Browning, M. (2021). A computational view on the nature of reward and value in anhedonia. *Current Topics in Behavioral Neurosciences*, pp. 421-441. doi:10.1007/7854_2021_290 (Crossref; two authors, corrected from "et al."; not read).

**Their vocabulary.** Reward sensitivity, learning rate, prediction error, model-free versus model-based valuation, Pavlovian bias, decision trees, "pessimistic evaluation of decision variables", mood as an integrated variable. Huys, Maia & Frank distinguish **theory-driven** (mechanistic models whose parameters are fitted to individuals) from **data-driven** (machine-learning on high-dimensional data) computational psychiatry `[read]`.

**Drives/operands.** Reward sensitivity (how much a received reward counts), learning rate (how fast values update), the valuation of threat/loss (as pessimistic evaluation in the depression analysis), and the balance of model-free (habit) and model-based (goal-directed) control. Depression's anhedonia is a loss of **reward sensitivity specifically**, distinct from alterations in dopaminergic prediction-error learning `[read]` (Huys 2016: "anhedonia in depression related specifically to a loss of reward sensitivity in a manner distinct from that of dopaminergic manipulations affecting learning").

**Operators / equations.** The RL model has two components, an algorithm for internal learning/evaluation and a **link function** from internal values to choice probabilities `[read]` (Huys 2016). The standard forms, which are not printed in the passage I read `[from abstract/standard account]`:
- prediction error delta_t = rho * r_t - Q_t(a); update Q_{t+1}(a) = Q_t(a) + epsilon * delta_t, where **rho is reward sensitivity** and **epsilon the learning rate**;
- softmax link: P(a) proportional to exp(beta * Q(a)), beta the inverse temperature.
The 2016 paper uses simulated data to show that fitting such a model's parameters gives a better classifier of two groups than raw data (AUC 0.87 against 0.74, 200 simulated subjects separated by the learning-rate parameter) `[read]`; this is a methodological point (mechanistic parameters as features), and is **simulated, not clinical** evidence. Rutledge et al.'s momentary-happiness model: happiness_t = w0 + w1 * sum(gamma^(t-j) * CR_j) + w2 * sum(gamma^(t-j) * EV_j) + w3 * sum(gamma^(t-j) * RPE_j) (certain reward, expected value, reward prediction error, with forgetting factor gamma, fitted mean 0.61; 26 lab subjects plus 18,420 smartphone-app participants) `[read in validation, PMC4143018]`; it makes affect a **weighted, exponentially discounted sum of recent reward-related quantities**.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Model-based predictions rather than theorems: a reduction in rho produces reduced response vigour and apparent apathy without any change in learning rate; a Pavlovian bias against threatening branches prunes decision trees, giving pessimism without distortion of probabilities; mood as a running average creates self-reinforcing loops. The 2016 review reports dynamic network analyses showing **loops of mutually reinforcing symptoms that could stabilise each other**, and increased variance before transitions between depressed and non-depressed states `[read]`: early-warning signals of the critical-slowing-down kind (cross-ref 30).

**Empirical standing.** Honest reading of Huys, Maia & Frank (`[read]`): the paper is a programmatic review, and the approach has a good record on *explaining* group differences in task parameters (anhedonia and reward sensitivity, model-based deficits in addiction and compulsivity) but a weak record on **clinical prediction**: biomarker and treatment-prediction studies cited in the same review show modest accuracy, and the review says the absence of strong correlates in psychiatry makes it hard to model them in their own right `[read]`, paraphrased. Task-parameter reliability for individual differences is a recognised problem (not checked here, `[from abstract/standard account]`). Most of the empirical base is individual-level tasks with monetary rewards and electric shocks; **social reward and social threat are thinner, but not absent**: see section 9b, added in validation.

**Operand/operator translation.**
- The cleanest *parameterised drive* in the cluster: **reward sensitivity rho multiplies the reward input**, learning rate epsilon controls the update, beta the exploitation. A person's drive profile is a **parameter vector (rho, epsilon, beta, Pavlovian bias...)**, fitted from behaviour, and these parameters are exactly what RDoC's Positive Valence constructs (reward responsiveness, learning, valuation) name in words.
- Depression as **reduced rho** and anxiety as **overweighted threat valuation** are concrete operators on a parameter vector: pathology equals a displacement of parameters, and treatment equals a displacement back. That is the only place in the cluster where "controlling behaviour" is a literal parameter intervention.
- Mood as a running discounted sum gives the algebra a template for a **slow state variable integrating fast events** (see 19, 27).

## 9b. Computational models of social drives (added in validation, 2026-10-29)

The original survey called this thin and did not survey it. It is smaller than the non-social literature but real, and it is the part of computational psychiatry closest to the algebra's social operands. All entries below were resolved via Crossref and characterised from their abstracts (Europe PMC) `[abstract read]`; none was read in full.

- **Primer.** Lockwood, P. L., & Klein-Flügge, M. C. (2021). Computational modelling of social cognition and behaviour: a reinforcement learning primer. *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*, 16(8), 761-771 (online 2020). doi:10.1093/scan/nsaa040. RL accounts of prosocial learning, observational learning, mentalising and impression formation; the entry point.
- **Prosocial learning as a parameter.** Lockwood, P. L., Apps, M. A. J., Valton, V., Viding, E., & Roiser, J. P. (2016). Neurocomputational mechanisms of prosocial learning and links to empathy. *PNAS*, 113(35), 9763-9768. doi:10.1073/pnas.1603198113. People learn to obtain rewards for others more slowly than for themselves; a prosocial prediction error in subgenual ACC; individual differences linked to empathy. Algebraically: a separate learning rate for other-regarding outcomes, i.e. a per-beneficiary parameter.
- **Learning whom to trust.** Behrens, T. E. J., Hunt, L. T., Woolrich, M. W., & Rushworth, M. F. S. (2008). Associative learning of social value. *Nature*, 456, 245-249. doi:10.1038/nature07538 (Crossref; abstract not checked). Standard account: separate prediction errors for one's own reward and for an adviser's reliability.
- **Social hierarchy.** Kumaran, D., Banino, A., Blundell, C., Hassabis, D., & Dayan, P. (2016). Computations underlying social hierarchy learning: distinct neural mechanisms for updating and representing self-relevant information. *Neuron*, 92(5), 1135-1147. doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2016.10.052. A Bayesian scheme tracking each individual's **power** beats an Elo-style (chess-rating) RL model on behaviour and neural data; MPFC selectively updates one's *own* hierarchy; amygdala/hippocampus code rank even when not required. This is a fitted model of rank inference, the nearest computational object to the status operand that section 10 says is missing, though it models learning about status, not the drive to gain it.
- **Social anxiety as biased self-learning.** Koban, L., et al. (2017). Social anxiety is characterized by biased learning about performance and the self. *Emotion*, 17(8), 1144-1155. doi:10.1037/emo0000296. Rescorla-Wagner model of affective updating after judges' feedback on a speech (21 SAD, 35 controls): controls show a positivity bias, absent in SAD. Hopkins, A. K., Dolan, R., Button, K. S., & Moutoussis, M. (2021). A reduced self-positive belief underpins greater sensitivity to negative evaluation in socially anxious individuals. *Computational Psychiatry*, 5(1), 21-. doi:10.5334/cpsy.57. Fear of negative evaluation goes with a higher self-negative learning rate, underpinned by a lower prior belief in self-positive attributes; belief-based and associative models fit about equally at group level.
- **Self-esteem from social feedback.** Will, G.-J., Rutledge, R. B., Moutoussis, M., & Dolan, R. J. (2017). Neural and computational processes underlying dynamic changes in self-esteem. *eLife*, 6, e28098. doi:10.7554/eLife.28098. Self-esteem moves with **social prediction errors** (expected vs received approval), the Rutledge happiness form transplanted to social appraisal; parameters load on an "interpersonal vulnerability" symptom dimension.
- **Dyadic cooperation in psychopathology.** King-Casas, B., Sharp, C., Lomax-Bream, L., Lohrenz, T., Fonagy, P., & Montague, P. R. (2008). The rupture and repair of cooperation in borderline personality disorder. *Science*, 321(5890), 806-810. doi:10.1126/science.1156902 (Crossref; content standard account): repeated trust game, with failure to repair cooperation after ruptures. A two-agent, multi-round paradigm, so closer to an interaction than the single-agent tasks above.
- **Rejection sensitivity in CAPS.** Ayduk & Gyurak (2008), section 8: a verbal, if-then account of a social-threat drive.

**Translation.** These give fitted, per-person parameters for social inputs: an other-regarding learning rate, valence-asymmetric learning rates for social feedback about the self, a prior on self-worth, a self-esteem state driven by social prediction errors, and a power estimate per group member. All are still single-agent models of how one person processes feedback from others; only the trust-game work is genuinely interactive.

## 10. What exists vs what does not (blunt)

**Exists.**
- A replicable low-dimensional coordinate system for individual differences (Big Five, HEXACO) and a defensible functional reading of it (CB5T): five parameter families (reward approach, threat/uncertainty defence, information seeking, goal protection, cooperation) and two global gains (Stability, Plasticity), mapped by the author to dopamine, serotonin and opioid systems.
- A clean three-system motivational split (approach, fear, conflict) with sensitivities as individual differences (RST), and a six-domain research taxonomy (RDoC) that separates valence systems, valuation parameters and learning.
- Quantitative evidence that a person's state distribution is stable while most variation is within-person (Fleeson), and a conditional-signature account of how situation enters (Mischel and Shoda).
- Fitted, equation-level drive parameters (reward sensitivity, learning rate, inverse temperature) in computational psychiatry, with real, task-bound clinical findings on anhedonia.

**Does not exist.**
- **Any equation set that derives a trait from drive dynamics.** CB5T, RST, Cloninger, Eysenck are verbal. The reverse bridge (a dynamical drive model whose stationary distribution reproduces Fleeson's density distributions) has not, as far as I found, been written.
- **A group-level parameter.** Every model here parameterises one person. The only traits that take another agent as input are Agreeableness and Honesty-Humility, and they are scales, not couplings.
- **Strong social-drive parameters.** RDoC's Social Processes has affiliation and attachment only. Computational models of social learning exist (section 9b: prosocial learning rates, biased self-evaluation learning in social anxiety, social-prediction-error self-esteem, Bayesian rank inference), but they parameterise how one person *learns from* others, not a drive toward status or belonging. There is no standard parameterisation of status-seeking, belonging-threat, or dominance as a drive.
- **Predictive validity at the individual-act level.** Trait scores do not predict single acts well; within-person variance dominates (57-78 percent in Fleeson & Law's observer data).
- **Theorems.** Nothing here produces one. At best there are parameter-sensitivity corollaries (for example, reduced rho leaves learning intact).

**For the algebra (suggestions, flagged as mine).** (1) Treat the drive vector as a *state* and the trait vector as the *parameters* of the dynamics and of the stationary distribution; (2) use CB5T's five functions as the checklist of parameter families a person-model must carry, and RDoC's separation of valence, valuation and learning as the layer structure; (3) use the Huys-style reward-sensitivity/learning-rate/inverse-temperature triple as a concrete, fittable starting parameterisation for the approach drive; (4) use CAPS's conditional signature as the way a situation (including another agent's act) enters, with the person-specific encoding as part of the agent; (5) treat Agreeableness/Honesty-Humility as the slot where another agent's drive state is an input.
