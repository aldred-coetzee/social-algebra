# Prior work 35: applied control of drives, behaviour change and persuasion

Date: 2026-10-29. Survey for the social-algebra strand. Cluster: the applied sciences in which operators on another agent's motivation are catalogued, applied and measured. Context: Aldred's aim, "an algebra which expresses basic human drives and group drives, which we can compose into equations, which we can then use to start making theorems and use to control or predict behavior".

**Method and honesty note.** (Validation pass 2026-10-29: see the log at the end; `[read]` marks a figure checked against the abstract or, for Maier et al., the full letter.) No primary paper was read in full this session. Every DOI below was resolved through the Crossref search API (title, first author, year and container matched). Every effect size is `[from abstract/standard account]`: taken from an abstract or search summary of the abstract, plus my background knowledge where stated. Numbers marked `[unverified]` were not found in any source this session. Nothing here has been checked against a full text, and the effect-size numbers should be re-read at source before any theorem leans on them. Equations are given as the field states them; most of this cluster has no equations, only boxes and arrows, and I say so.

**Summary.** This cluster is the nearest thing in the literature to an operator catalogue on motivation. Its structure is three-layered:

1. **Models of what the operators act on**, mostly *products or conjunctions of two or three appraisals*: threat × coping (Rogers), fear × efficacy (Witte), motivation × ability × prompt (Fogg), capability × opportunity × motivation (Michie), attitude/norm/control → intention → behaviour (Ajzen). Multiplication is claimed constantly and almost never tested as multiplication.
2. **Operators**, i.e. techniques: the 93-item BCT taxonomy, implementation intentions, goal-setting, motivational-interviewing moves, message frames, inoculation. Each is a named intervention with a target construct. This is an operator vocabulary, but with no composition rules; it has only "co-occurrence in a trial" and a handful of meta-regression findings about which pairs help.
3. **Resistance**: reactance (Brehm) is the drive that fires *in response to the operator itself*. This is the first clear case in the survey of a drive whose input is another agent's attempt to move a drive.

Three blunt findings. (a) Raising a *motive* (risk, attitude, intention) moves behaviour much less than it moves the motive: the experimental intention-to-behaviour conversion is d 0.66 to 0.36, and risk appraisals d+ .31 to .23. (b) Operators that act on the *link* between motive and action (planning, monitoring, ability, prompts) are the ones with the best-supported effects. The algebra's drive-to-behaviour map should be a separate, lossy operator, not an identity. (c) The "backfire" question is split: the best aggregate meta-analysis finds no backfire, a cluster of critics says that finding hides it, and nudge effects fall to near zero after publication-bias correction. Effects that look small on average may be a mixture of large and null, so any operator in the algebra should carry a *context-conditioned* effect, not a constant.

## 1. Fear appeals: Rogers' protection motivation theory

**Citations.** Rogers, R. W. (1975). A protection motivation theory of fear appeals and attitude change. *Journal of Psychology*, 91(1), 93-114. DOI 10.1080/00223980.1975.9915803 (Crossref-confirmed). Maddux, J. E., & Rogers, R. W. (1983). Protection motivation and self-efficacy: a revised theory of fear appeals and attitude change. *J. Exp. Soc. Psychol.*, 19, 469-479. DOI 10.1016/0022-1031(83)90023-9 (Crossref-confirmed). Floyd, D. L., Prentice-Dunn, S., & Rogers, R. W. (2000). A meta-analysis of research on protection motivation theory. *J. Applied Social Psychology*, 30(2), 407-429. DOI 10.1111/j.1559-1816.2000.tb02323.x (Crossref-confirmed). Sheeran, P., Harris, P. R., & Epton, T. (2014). Does heightening risk appraisals change people's intentions and behavior? *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(2), 511-543. DOI 10.1037/a0033065 (Crossref-confirmed).

**Their vocabulary.** Threat appraisal (perceived severity, perceived vulnerability, intrinsic and extrinsic rewards of the maladaptive response), coping appraisal (response efficacy, self-efficacy, response costs), protection motivation (intention), adaptive vs maladaptive coping.

**Drives/operands.** Fear is not itself an operand in the revised theory; it is treated as a mediator that was dropped in favour of cognitive appraisal. The operands are two appraisals and an intention. The theory is, in effect, a model of one drive (self-protection) whose strength is computed from appraisals.

**Operators / equations.** No equation in the theory `[standard account]`. The structure is: protection motivation = f(threat appraisal, coping appraisal), where threat appraisal = severity + vulnerability + maladaptive rewards (the last subtracts) and coping appraisal = response efficacy + self-efficacy − response costs `[standard account]`. This is additive within each appraisal. Whether the two appraisals combine additively or multiplicatively is *not* settled by the theory; the 1975 version claimed an interaction, the 1983 revision lists additive main effects `[standard account]`. Operators on the system are the message manipulations: raise severity, raise vulnerability, raise efficacy, lower costs.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Each appraisal component increases protection motivation (monotone). The empirical results below are what the monotone predictions amount to; there are no derived non-obvious results.

**Empirical standing.** Floyd et al. (2000) pooled 65 studies, about 30,000 participants, over 20 health issues; overall d = 0.52 `[from abstract as quoted by secondary sources; abstract itself not retrieved]`. A second, independent PMT meta-analysis appeared in the same journal and year: Milne, S., Sheeran, P., & Orbell, S. (2000). Prediction and intervention in health-related behavior: a meta-analytic review of protection motivation theory. *J. Applied Social Psychology*, 30(1), 106-143 (added in validation; DOI not resolved). Higher severity, vulnerability, response efficacy and self-efficacy went with more adaptive intentions/behaviour; lower maladaptive rewards and response costs likewise `[from abstract]`. A popular source ranks coping variables above threat variables (`[unverified]` against the full text; a COVID-era PMT meta-analysis also found coping appraisals the strongest predictors `[from search summary]`). Caveat: Floyd's pooled studies are largely correlational or measure intentions, which is the weak link. Sheeran et al. (2014) restricted to *experiments* that raised risk appraisal and got small effects: d+ = .31 on intention (k = 217), d+ = .23 on behaviour (k = 93); but when risk appraisal, response efficacy and self-efficacy were raised *together* the effect jumped to d+ = .98 on intention and .45 on behaviour `[read: abstract]`. Reading: the correlational PMT picture overstates the lever; the conjunction of threat and coping is where the experimental effect lives. That supports a *product-like* or at least conjunctive form for the operator, with the caveat that conjunctive manipulation also just means "more intervention".

**Operand/operator translation.** Operand: an agent with a self-protection drive whose level is a function of two appraisal sub-states (threat, coping). Operators: "message that raises severity", "message that raises efficacy". The open design question for the algebra is whether threat and coping enter as sum, product or min. The data above (conjunction gives roughly 3x the effect of threat alone on intention) is mildly in favour of a conjunctive form but does not discriminate product from min.

## 2. Extended Parallel Process Model and the fear-appeal meta-analyses

**Citations.** Witte, K. (1992). Putting the fear back into fear appeals: the extended parallel process model. *Communication Monographs*, 59(4), 329-349. DOI 10.1080/03637759209376276 (Crossref-confirmed). Witte, K., & Allen, M. (2000). A meta-analysis of fear appeals: implications for effective public health campaigns. *Health Education & Behavior*, 27(5), 591-615. DOI 10.1177/109019810002700506 (Crossref-confirmed). Leventhal, H. (1970). Findings and theory in the study of fear communications. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 5, 119-186. DOI 10.1016/s0065-2601(08)60091-x (Crossref-confirmed). Peters, G.-J. Y., Ruiter, R. A. C., & Kok, G. (2013 print; online 2012). Threatening communication: a critical re-analysis and a revised meta-analytic test of fear appeal theory. *Health Psychology Review*, 7(sup1), S8-S31. DOI 10.1080/17437199.2012.703527 (Crossref-confirmed; volume and pages from standard account). Tannenbaum, M. B., Hepler, J., Zimmerman, R. S., Saul, L., Jacobs, S., Wilson, K., & Albarracín, D. (2015). Appealing to fear: a meta-analysis of fear appeal effectiveness and theories. *Psychological Bulletin*, 141(6), 1178-1204. DOI 10.1037/a0039729 (Crossref-confirmed). Kok, G., Peters, G.-J. Y., Kessels, L. T. E., ten Hoor, G. A., & Ruiter, R. A. C. (2018). Ignoring theory and misinterpreting evidence: the false belief in fear appeals. *Health Psychology Review*, 12(2), 111-125. DOI 10.1080/17437199.2017.1415767 (Crossref-confirmed). White, B. X., & Albarracín, D. (2018). Investigating belief falsehood. Fear appeals do change behaviour in experimental laboratory studies. A commentary on Kok et al. (2018). *Health Psychology Review*, 12(2), 147-150. DOI 10.1080/17437199.2018.1448292 (Crossref- and PubMed-confirmed; first author is B. X. White, corrected from "K. M."). de Hoog, N., Stroebe, W., & de Wit, J. B. F. (2007). The impact of vulnerability to and severity of a health risk on processing and acceptance of fear-arousing communications: a meta-analysis. *Review of General Psychology*, 11(3), 258-285. DOI 10.1037/1089-2680.11.3.258 (Crossref-confirmed in validation; findings not read).

**Their vocabulary.** Perceived threat (severity × susceptibility), perceived efficacy (response efficacy × self-efficacy), danger control (adaptive: accept message, change behaviour), fear control (maladaptive: denial, defensive avoidance, reactance). Leventhal: two parallel processes, danger control and fear control, run in parallel.

**Drives/operands.** Fear as an emotion is an operand and an *output* of threat appraisal; the control it triggers has two modes. The EPPM adds the idea that *which mode* the agent enters depends on efficacy, not on fear intensity.

**Operators / equations.** The EPPM is a decision-tree model, usually drawn as a flowchart: if perceived threat is low, no motivation; if threat is high and perceived efficacy is high, danger control; if threat is high and efficacy is low, fear control `[standard account]`. Written as a rule: mode = danger-control iff efficacy > threat (the "efficacy exceeds threat" crossing), else fear control `[standard account]`; this comparison form is how the model is often stated and is the precise piece an algebra could take up. Within threat and efficacy the combination is stated as a product in the theory (severity × susceptibility; response efficacy × self-efficacy) but, `[standard account]`, tested in practice as additive main effects.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** (1) A threat message without efficacy produces fear control, i.e. a *negative* effect. (2) Threat × efficacy interaction: the effect of threat on adaptive behaviour is positive at high efficacy only. (3) Threshold behaviour: fear control triggers when threat exceeds efficacy. Prediction (1) is the only one that is novel, and it is the contested one.

**Empirical standing, bluntly.**
- Witte & Allen (2000): strong fear appeals produce higher severity and susceptibility, and are more persuasive; strong fear plus high efficacy gives the greatest behaviour change; strong fear plus low efficacy gives the greatest defensive responses (denial, avoidance, reactance) `[from abstract]`. The effect sizes were not in the abstract; `[unverified]` here.
- Tannenbaum et al. (2015): 127 articles, 248 independent samples, N = 27,372; average composite effect on attitudes, intentions and behaviours d = 0.29 (random effects); larger when messages include efficacy statements, depict high susceptibility and severity, and recommend one-time rather than repeated behaviours; larger for audiences with more women; "there are no identified circumstances under which they backfire" `[read: abstract]`.
- Peters et al. (2013): inclusion restricted to full factorial orthogonal manipulations of threat and efficacy with behaviour as outcome; in the six qualifying studies a significant threat × efficacy interaction: threat had an effect only under high efficacy (d = 0.31), efficacy only under high threat (d = 0.71) `[read: abstract]`. (An earlier draft said "others cite it as finding a negative effect"; the abstract reports no negative effect, so that was removed.) de Hoog et al. (2007) findings on processing are `[from secondary citation; not read]`.
- Kok et al. (2018) argue that proper tests need threat × efficacy designs with behaviour as outcome, that the extant evidence "does not support positive effects of fear appeals", and that the intuition behind them holds only "under specific, rare circumstances" `[read: abstract]`; the specific charge that Tannenbaum's composite of attitudes, intentions and behaviour hid a weak behaviour link is `[standard account; not in the abstract]`. The issue carried eight commentaries; White & Albarracín's (no abstract) argues from its title that fear appeals do change behaviour in laboratory experiments; its content was not read. The dispute is live. Both sides agree efficacy matters.
- My read, blunt: a modest positive average (d about 0.3) on a composite is reasonably well supported; the effect on *actual behaviour* outside the lab is weaker and contested (this matches the Sheeran 2014 gap of .31 to .23); the EPPM's distinctive claim, that low efficacy flips the sign through fear control, has weaker direct support than the model's popularity suggests. No one has shown a clean sign-flip meta-analytically.

**Operand/operator translation.** Operand: an agent with a threat state T and an efficacy state E (both graded). Operator: a message that increments T and/or E. Mode switching is the piece worth taking: a *threshold on the difference* (E − T or E/T) selects between two response types, which in an algebra is a piecewise operator or a gated one. That is the same structure as the Dollard & Miller crossing point in file 16 section 3: approach = efficacy-driven, avoidance = threat-driven. In effect the EPPM restates Miller's approach-avoidance conflict in message terms. Whether this equivalence is real or a resemblance is a research question for the model file.

## 3. Elaboration Likelihood Model and its critics

**Citations.** Petty, R. E., & Cacioppo, J. T. (1986). *Communication and Persuasion: Central and Peripheral Routes to Attitude Change*. Springer. Chapter DOI 10.1007/978-1-4612-4964-1_1 (Crossref-confirmed as "The Elaboration Likelihood Model of Persuasion" in that volume). Petty, R. E., Cacioppo, J. T., & Schumann, D. (1983). Central and peripheral routes to advertising effectiveness: the moderating role of involvement. *J. Consumer Research*, 10(2), 135-146. DOI 10.1086/208954 (Crossref-confirmed). Kruglanski, A. W., & Thompson, E. P. (1999). Persuasion by a single route: a view from the unimodel. *Psychological Inquiry*, 10(2), 83-109. DOI 10.1207/s15327965pl100201 (Crossref-confirmed as a Psychological Inquiry item; page numbers `[standard account]`).

**Their vocabulary.** Elaboration likelihood, central route (scrutiny of argument quality), peripheral route (cues: source credibility, number of arguments), motivation and ability to process, involvement.

**Drives/operands.** Motivation to process and ability to process are the two operands; the model is about how much the *recipient* thinks, not what they want.

**Operators / equations.** None stated as equations. Rule `[standard account]`: if motivation and ability to elaborate are high, argument quality dominates attitude change and the change is durable; if either is low, peripheral cues dominate and the change is fragile. The same variable (e.g. source expertise) can act as cue, argument or biaser depending on elaboration level (the multiple-roles postulate).

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Argument quality × involvement interaction (Petty et al. 1983); durability asymmetry (central > peripheral).

**Empirical standing.** The 1983 advertising result and the interaction replicated broadly in the field's own literature `[standard account]`. Critics: Kruglanski & Thompson's unimodel argues there is *one* route (inference from evidence), and that "cues" and "arguments" differ in length and complexity, not kind, so the two-route taxonomy is unfalsifiable as stated (the model can reassign any variable to either role after the fact) `[from standard account of the critique]`. I did not obtain effect sizes. Stiff's 1986 critique of source and message factors is cited in Crossref-found replies (Petty et al. 1987 reply, DOI 10.1080/03637758709390229) but I did not read it.

**Operand/operator translation.** Takes one lesson: operators on beliefs and attitudes depend on a *gate* variable (processing capacity) of the target. The same message is two different operators for two states of the target. In the algebra that makes persuasion operators state-conditioned, not fixed maps. It does not give a drive.

## 4. Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP)

**Citation.** Fogg, B. J. (2009). A behavior model for persuasive design. *Proceedings of the 4th International Conference on Persuasive Technology*. DOI 10.1145/1541948.1541999 (Crossref-confirmed). See also Fogg & Hreha (2010), Behavior Wizard, *Persuasive Technology* (LNCS 6137), 117-131, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-13226-1_13 (Crossref-confirmed). A formalisation exists: van Eijk (2015), "Requirements for relaxation coaching: a formalization of the Fogg behavior model", DOI 10.5220/0005427600310036 (Crossref-confirmed; not read).

**Their vocabulary.** Motivation (sensation: pleasure/pain; anticipation: hope/fear; belonging: acceptance/rejection), ability (time, money, physical effort, brain cycles, social deviance, non-routine), prompts (spark, facilitator, signal).

**Drives/operands.** The three core motivators are paired opposites, which is a small drive taxonomy: pleasure/pain, hope/fear, acceptance/rejection `[standard account]`.

**Operators / equations.** Credit and naming: the 2009 paper (Fogg's) states the model as three factors, motivation, ability and **trigger**, which "must occur at the same moment" (abstract, `[read]`); it was then often written B = MAT. The B = MAP form, with "prompt" replacing "trigger", is Fogg's later relabelling (behaviormodel.org; *Tiny Habits*, 2019/2020) `[standard account]`. Behaviour occurs when motivation, ability and a trigger/prompt coincide. The model is drawn as a plane with an "action line"; a behaviour above the curve fires when a prompt arrives, below it does not. The action line is a decreasing curve in (ability, motivation): lower ability needs higher motivation. Stated informally as B = M × A × P in some summaries `[standard account]`; the 2009 paper presents it as the shape of the curve and not a formula, `[standard account]`, and I did not read it. A multiplicative form is implied by a hyperbola-like threshold but never fitted.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** (1) Trade-off between motivation and ability: making the behaviour easier is a substitute for raising motivation. (2) No prompt, no behaviour, however high M and A.

**Empirical standing, bluntly.** A design heuristic, not a tested model. I found no meta-analysis. It has been cited in persuasive-technology and habit-app design; the formalisation work exists, but I saw no fit to data. Prediction (1) is the reason to keep it: it is the ability-substitutes-for-motivation claim, which matches Hull/Spence-style products (file 16 section 1) and Miller's gradients, and has empirical support in the nudge/friction literature in general (see section 12 caveat).

**Operand/operator translation.** Agent state (M, A) plus an external event P. An action line is a threshold surface, so behaviour is a thresholded product-like function. Operators: raise M, raise A (simplify), deliver P. The clean separation of *three* kinds of operator (motive, capacity, trigger) is what the algebra should copy; the multiplicative claim should be left open.

## 5. COM-B, the Behaviour Change Wheel and the Theoretical Domains Framework

**Citations.** Michie, S., van Stralen, M. M., & West, R. (2011). The behaviour change wheel: a new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions. *Implementation Science*, 6, 42. DOI 10.1186/1748-5908-6-42 (Crossref-confirmed). Michie, S., Atkins, L., & West, R. (2014). *The Behaviour Change Wheel: A Guide to Designing Interventions*. Silverback. (Book; no DOI sought.) West, R., & Michie, S. (2020). A brief introduction to the COM-B model of behaviour and the PRIME theory of motivation. *Qeios*. DOI 10.32388/ww04e6 (Crossref-confirmed, preprint). Michie, S., et al. (2005). Making psychological theory useful for implementing evidence based practice: a consensus approach. *Quality and Safety in Health Care*, 14, 26-33. DOI 10.1136/qshc.2004.011155 (Crossref-confirmed). Cane, J., O'Connor, D., & Michie, S. (2012). Validation of the theoretical domains framework. *Implementation Science*, 7, 37. DOI 10.1186/1748-5908-7-37 (Crossref-confirmed). Atkins, L., et al. (2017). A guide to using the Theoretical Domains Framework. *Implementation Science*, 12, 77. DOI 10.1186/s13012-017-0605-9 (Crossref-confirmed).

**Their vocabulary.** COM-B: Capability (physical, psychological), Opportunity (physical, social), Motivation (reflective, automatic) produce Behaviour. The Behaviour Change Wheel puts COM-B at the hub, wraps it with nine intervention functions (education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modelling, enablement) and seven policy categories (communication/marketing, guidelines, fiscal measures, regulation, legislation, environmental/social planning, service provision) `[standard account]`. TDF: 14 domains (older versions 12) of determinants `[standard account]`.

**Drives/operands.** Motivation is split into reflective (plans, evaluations) and automatic (wants, needs, desires, impulses, habits) `[standard account]`. Automatic motivation is the nearest thing in the clinical-behaviour literature to a "drive". Capability and opportunity are separate non-motivational operands (the agent's skills, and the situation's affordances including social).

**Operators / equations.** COM-B is a system diagram, not an equation: C, O and M each can drive B, and B feeds back on all three; no functional form is given `[standard account]`. What the Wheel contributes is a *mapping from component to operator*: each COM-B deficit is matched with intervention functions (e.g. a capability deficit: education, training, enablement; an automatic-motivation deficit: incentivisation, coercion, modelling, environmental restructuring) `[standard account]`. That mapping is a typed operator table, which is exactly the shape the algebra needs.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** None; it is a design framework. The implicit claim is that choosing the intervention by diagnosed deficit beats choosing by habit.

**Empirical standing, bluntly.** Widely used (national guidance, thousands of citations), but the evidence that Wheel-guided design beats unguided design is thin; I found no head-to-head trial. The mapping tables were derived by consensus among authors, not from data `[standard account]`. COM-B is accused of being too general to be falsified: it is largely a classification of causes. The authors' own PRIME theory is the more formal sibling and I did not read it beyond the title.

**Operand/operator translation.** The useful piece is the *typed* structure: operands are agent states of type C, O, M; operators have a declared target type. In our algebra this is a sort system: an operator is applicable to a sort. It also gives a social-opportunity sort (other people as part of the opportunity), which is where the strand's group structure enters the individual-level model.

## 6. The Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy and its ontology

**Citations.** Michie, S., Richardson, M., Johnston, M., Abraham, C., Francis, J., Hardeman, W., Eccles, M. P., Cane, J., & Wood, C. E. (2013). The behavior change technique taxonomy (v1) of 93 hierarchically clustered techniques: building an international consensus for the reporting of behavior change interventions. *Annals of Behavioral Medicine*, 46(1), 81-95. DOI 10.1007/s12160-013-9486-6 (Crossref-confirmed). Abraham, C., & Michie, S. (2008). A taxonomy of behavior change techniques used in interventions. *Health Psychology*, 27(3), 379-387. DOI 10.1037/0278-6133.27.3.379 (Crossref-confirmed in validation): 26 techniques. Michie, S., Abraham, C., Whittington, C., McAteer, J., & Gupta, S. (2009). Effective techniques in healthy eating and physical activity interventions: a meta-regression. *Health Psychology*, 28(6), 690-701. DOI 10.1037/a0016136 (Crossref- and PubMed-confirmed; full citation added in validation, the draft cited "Michie et al. 2009" without one). Michie et al. (2015). Behaviour change techniques: the development and evaluation of a taxonomic method. *Health Technology Assessment*, 19(99). DOI 10.3310/hta19990 (Crossref-confirmed). Carey, R. N., Connell, L. E., Johnston, M., et al. (2019; online 2018). Behavior change techniques and their mechanisms of action: a synthesis of links described in published intervention literature. *Annals of Behavioral Medicine*, 53(8), 693-707. DOI 10.1093/abm/kay078 (Crossref-confirmed). Larsen, K. R., et al. (2017; online 2016). Behavior change interventions: the potential of ontologies for advancing science and practice. *J. Behavioral Medicine*, 40(1), 6-22. DOI 10.1007/s10865-016-9768-0 (Crossref-confirmed). Michie, S., et al. (2021). Representation of behaviour change interventions and their evaluation: development of the upper level of the Behaviour Change Intervention Ontology. *Wellcome Open Research*, 5, 123. DOI 10.12688/wellcomeopenres.15902.2 (Crossref-confirmed; title ends past my truncated output so the "upper level" wording is `[standard account]`). Norris, E., et al. (2024). A data extraction template for the behaviour change intervention ontology. *Wellcome Open Research*. DOI 10.12688/wellcomeopenres.20872.1 (Crossref-confirmed).

**Their vocabulary.** Behaviour change technique (BCT): "an observable, replicable, and irreducible component of an intervention designed to alter or redirect causal processes that regulate behavior" `[standard account]`. 93 BCTs in 16 clusters (counts `[read: abstract]`): goals and planning; feedback and monitoring; social support; shaping knowledge; natural consequences; comparison of behaviour; associations; repetition and substitution; comparison of outcomes; reward and threat; regulation; antecedents; identity; scheduled consequences; self-belief; covert learning `[standard account; the list of cluster names is from memory, unverified against the paper]`. Example BCTs: goal setting (behaviour), problem solving, action planning, self-monitoring of behaviour, feedback on behaviour, social support (unspecified), instruction on how to perform the behaviour, credible source, demonstration, prompts/cues, habit formation, reward (outcome), incentive, verbal persuasion about capability, framing/reframing, commitment `[standard account]`.

**Drives/operands.** None directly; BCTs are interventions. Carey et al. link BCTs to *mechanisms of action* (MoAs), the constructs a technique is supposed to move (e.g. self-efficacy, intention, goals, beliefs about consequences, habit, social influences).

**Operators / equations.** The taxonomy is a controlled vocabulary: operators are named, numbered (e.g. 1.1 goal setting) and grouped. Carey et al. is a relation: technique → mechanism, strength = how often the link is cited in the literature, not effect size `[from abstract/standard account]`. The ontology (BCIO) is a formal ontology (OWL-based `[standard account]`) with entities for intervention, BCT, mechanism of action, mode of delivery, source, population, setting, outcome behaviour. No algebraic composition: "intervention = set of BCTs" is the only composition, a union.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** None. The programme is *descriptive* then *meta-regression*.

**Empirical standing.** Michie et al. (2009) (cited above) is the prototype: 122 evaluations, N = 44,747, overall effect 0.31 (95% CI 0.26-0.36, I2 = 69%); self-monitoring explained the most among-study heterogeneity (13%); self-monitoring combined with at least one other control-theory technique: 0.42 versus 0.26 `[read: abstract]`. The overall lesson from technique-level meta-regression is that effects of individual BCTs are small, confounded and heterogeneous, with high I2; the 93-item list is more complete than any evidence about its elements. Inter-coder reliability of applying the taxonomy is a recurring issue (Wood et al. 2015, *Translational Behavioral Medicine* 5(2), 134-148, DOI 10.1007/s13142-014-0290-z, Crossref-confirmed as a coder-training study; not read). I did not find or read the 2024+ BCT Ontology release beyond the Norris 2024 template and Michie 2021 upper level papers above, so the claim of a completed "BCTO" with versioned BCT definitions is `[unconfirmed]`.

**Operand/operator translation.** The nearest existing artefact to an *operator inventory* for the algebra. Direct use: seed the operator list with the 16 clusters as coarse operator families, the 93 as fine ones, and Carey's MoA links as the operator → operand-type map. Warnings: (i) these are *intervention components*, not primitive social acts, so many are programme-level ("provide feedback on a schedule"); (ii) they are defined for one-way delivery by a practitioner, not for symmetric interaction; (iii) no BCT has a measured effect in isolation that survives meta-regression with confidence.

## 7. Theory of Planned Behaviour and the intention-behaviour gap

**Citations.** Ajzen, I. (1991). The theory of planned behavior. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 50(2), 179-211. DOI 10.1016/0749-5978(91)90020-t (Crossref-confirmed). The impact of changing attitudes, norms, and self-efficacy on health-related intentions and behavior: a meta-analysis. *Health Psychology*, 35(11), 1178-1188. DOI 10.1037/hea0000387 (Crossref-confirmed). Sniehotta, F. F., Presseau, J., & Araujo-Soares, V. (2014). Time to retire the theory of planned behaviour. *Health Psychology Review*, 8(1), 1-7. DOI 10.1080/17437199.2013.869710 (Crossref-confirmed). Rhodes & Dickau (2012), experimental intention-behaviour in physical activity, *Health Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/a0027290 (Crossref-confirmed; not read).

**Their vocabulary.** Attitude toward the behaviour, subjective norm, perceived behavioural control (PBC) → intention → behaviour (PBC also directly to behaviour). Each antecedent is expectancy-value: belief strength × evaluation (attitude), normative belief × motivation to comply (norm), control belief × power (PBC).

**Operators / equations `[standard account]`.** Intention I = w1·A + w2·SN + w3·PBC (regression weights, fitted). Each component is a sum of products: A = Σ b_i·e_i; SN = Σ n_j·m_j; PBC = Σ c_k·p_k. B = f(I, PBC). The expectancy-value form is the only genuinely equational structure in the cluster: a weighted sum over products. Operators: change beliefs (b, e, n, m, c, p).

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Intention (mediated) predicts behaviour; a belief change propagates up through the chain. The chain makes quantitative predictions (R2 for I and B).

**Empirical standing, bluntly.**
- Prediction: Armitage & Conner (2001), 185 independent studies to end-1997: TPB accounted for 27% of variance in behaviour and 39% in intention; subjective norm generally a weak predictor of intention `[read: abstract]`. Sheeran (2002) meta-analysed 10 meta-analyses (422 studies, N = 82,107): sample-weighted r+ = 0.53 intention-behaviour, i.e. about 28% of variance `[from secondary sources incl. Sheeran & Webb 2016; not read]`. McEachan et al. is the prospective updated meta-analysis; numbers not retrieved.
- Intervention: the decisive result. Webb & Sheeran (2006): 47 experimental tests; a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced only a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36) `[read: abstract]`. Sheeran et al. (2016): 204 experimental tests; changing attitudes, norms and self-efficacy gave d+ = .48 / .49 / .51 on intention and .38 / .36 / .47 on behaviour; effects were generally not moderated, though larger for interventions to increase (vs decrease) behaviour `[read: abstract]`. **Corrected in validation**: the draft gave .50/.41/.50 and .37/.20/.46 and concluded norms were much the weakest lever on behaviour; the abstract shows the three levers are of similar size, self-efficacy largest (.47) and least attenuated, norms most attenuated (.49 to .36) but not weak. The draft's claim that the authors "conclude correlational studies overestimate" is not in the abstract (which concludes the results lend experimental support to the theories) and was removed.
- Critique: Sniehotta et al. (2014) argue the TPB is unfalsifiable in practice, has not produced effective interventions, and should be retired; commentary pieces (Armitage 2014; Ogden 2014) defend or concede parts `[from Crossref titles; content from standard account]`. Hardeman, Johnston, Johnston, Bonetti, Wareham & Kinmonth (2002, *Psychology & Health* 17(2), 123-158; DOI 10.1080/08870440290013644a, Crossref-confirmed) review TPB-based interventions; their finding that few interventions used the theory systematically is `[standard account]`.
- The practical conclusion matches section 1: moving an attitude or intention is easy; moving behaviour from it is lossy. The "gap" means roughly half the intenders do not act (the Sheeran 2002 figure of about half is `[standard account]`).

**Operand/operator translation.** Operands: belief-valued attitudes, norms, control, intention. Operator: belief change. The weighted sum-of-products is a candidate form for *composite motive strength* inside the algebra, with the explicit warning that the fitted weights are population averages and the dyad-level prediction is poor (as in file 07's individual-prediction warning). Subjective norm is the only social term and enters as perceived expectation, not as an actual other agent; making it a relation to real agents is an algebra extension, not something TPB does.

## 8. Goal-setting theory

**Citations.** Locke, E. A., & Latham, G. P. (2002). Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation: a 35-year odyssey. *American Psychologist*, 57(9), 705-717. DOI 10.1037/0003-066x.57.9.705 (Crossref-confirmed). Mento, A. J., Steel, R. P., & Karren, R. J. (1987). A meta-analytic study of the effects of goal setting on task performance: 1966-1984. *OBHDP*, 39, 52-83. DOI 10.1016/0749-5978(87)90045-8 (Crossref-confirmed; the pages `[standard account]`). Harkin, B., Webb, T. L., Chang, B. P. I., et al. (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence. *Psychological Bulletin*, 142(2), 198-229. DOI 10.1037/bul0000025 (Crossref-confirmed).

**Their vocabulary.** Goal specificity, goal difficulty, goal commitment, self-efficacy, feedback, task complexity, learning goals vs performance goals.

**Operators / equations `[from abstract/standard account]`.** No equation. Core relations: performance increases linearly with goal difficulty up to the limit of ability; specific difficult goals beat "do your best"; moderators are commitment, feedback and self-efficacy; task complexity and the learning-goal variant limit the effect. Effect sizes: goal difficulty d from .52 to .82; specific difficult goals vs do-your-best d from .42 to .80 (Locke & Latham 1990 meta-analyses, as cited in the 2002 paper) `[from search summary]`. An often-quoted claim that the effect holds in "90% of studies" was not found in any retrieved text and is `[unverified]`.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** Linear difficulty-performance relation; commitment as a moderator (goal effect ~ difficulty × commitment).

**Empirical standing.** Among the most replicated in organisational psychology, in task performance with measurable output. Outside the work context, a multi-behaviour review gave a much smaller pooled d = 0.34 `[from a secondary summary, unverified]`. Goal-monitoring (Harkin et al.): 138 studies, N = 19,951; the interventions raised the frequency of monitoring (d+ = 1.98, 95% CI 1.71-2.24) and goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32-0.48); monitoring frequency mediated the effect; larger when outcomes were reported or made public and when physically recorded `[read: abstract]`. That is a separate lever from the goal itself: monitoring operates on the feedback loop. Critical caveats: Ordóñez, L. D., Schweitzer, M. E., Galinsky, A. D., & Bazerman, M. H. (2009). Goals gone wild: the systematic side effects of overprescribing goal setting. *Academy of Management Perspectives*, 23(1), 6-16. DOI 10.5465/amp.2009.37007999 (Crossref-confirmed in validation; content `[standard account]`).

**Operand/operator translation.** The operand is a goal: a target value of a performance variable, plus a commitment level. Operators: set a goal (specificity, difficulty), give feedback, record progress publicly. The goal as a *reference value* links to Powers' control theory (file 16) and to Carver & Scheier (not re-surveyed here): goal setting is an operator on the setpoint of a feedback loop, monitoring is an operator on the loop's measurement, and public recording makes the loop social. That is the cleanest connection between this cluster and the algebra's single-agent model layer.

## 9. Implementation intentions

**Citations.** Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: strong effects of simple plans. *American Psychologist*, 54(7), 493-503. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493 (Crossref-confirmed in validation). Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38, 69-119. DOI 10.1016/s0065-2601(06)38002-1 (Crossref-confirmed). Sheeran, P., Listrom, O., & Gollwitzer, P. M. (2024/2025). The when and how of planning: meta-analysis of the scope and components of implementation intentions in 642 tests. *European Review of Social Psychology*, 36(1), 162-194. DOI 10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563 (Crossref-confirmed; year differs between online-first 2024 and issue 2025).

**Their vocabulary.** Goal intention ("I intend to achieve X") vs implementation intention ("if situation Y, then I will do Z"), situational cue, strategic automation of action initiation, shielding from distraction.

**Operators / equations.** An if-then plan: IF cue(s) THEN response(r). It binds a situational cue to a response so that the cue triggers the response with reduced conscious deliberation. A single, small formal operator: cue → action link creation. `[standard account]`

**Theorems or derived predictions.** The plan increases the accessibility of the cue and automates the response (both support found in the 2006 meta-analysis `[from abstract]`). Predicts largest effects where the failure is *initiation* (not motivation).

**Empirical standing.** The 2006 meta-analysis: 94 independent tests, d = .65 on goal attainment (medium to large) `[read: abstract]`. Benefits: starting goal pursuit, shielding ongoing pursuit from interference, disengaging from failing courses, conserving capacity `[from abstract]`. The 2024/2025 update (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer): across 642 independent tests, implementation intentions were effective for cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes (.27 ≤ d ≤ .66); larger with a contingent if-then format, high goal motivation and rehearsal `[read: abstract]`. Caution: the 2006 d = .65 is a pooled mean over mainly short-term outcomes; the 2025 range is the current figure. Dablander-type publication-bias reanalysis of the intention-change literature exists (a "Reply to Dablander et al." result surfaced in search) and I did not read it, so whether the effect shrinks under bias correction is **open** `[unconfirmed]`.

**Operand/operator translation.** The cleanest *single operator* in this cluster. In the algebra: operator PLAN(cue, response) acts on an agent and adds a conditional rule to the agent's habit/policy set. It does not change drive strength; it changes the *coupling* of a situation to an action. That is exactly the "drive vs link" split flagged in the summary: motive-raising operators give a lossy gain; link-making operators (plans, prompts, environment) give the larger measured effects.

## 10. Self-determination-based interventions and motivational interviewing

**Citations.** Ng, J. Y. Y., Ntoumanis, N., Thogersen-Ntoumani, C., Deci, E. L., Ryan, R. M., Duda, J. L., & Williams, G. C. (2012). Self-determination theory applied to health contexts: a meta-analysis. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 7(4), 325-340. DOI 10.1177/1745691612447309 (Crossref-confirmed). Ntoumanis, N., Ng, J. Y. Y., Prestwich, A., et al. (2021). A meta-analysis of self-determination theory-informed intervention studies in the health domain. *Health Psychology Review*, 15(2), 214-244. DOI 10.1080/17437199.2020.1718529 (Crossref-confirmed). Sheeran, P., Wright, C. E., Avishai, A., et al. (2020). Self-determination theory interventions for health behavior change: meta-analysis. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 88(8), 726-737. DOI 10.1037/ccp0000501 (Crossref-confirmed). Miller, W. R., & Rollnick, S. (2013). *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change* (3rd ed.). Guilford. (Book; no DOI sought.) Burke, B. L., Arkowitz, H., & Menchola, M. (2003). The efficacy of motivational interviewing: a meta-analysis of controlled clinical trials. *J. Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 71(5), 843-861. DOI 10.1037/0022-006x.71.5.843 (Crossref-confirmed). Lundahl, B. W., Kunz, C., Brownell, C., Tollefson, D., & Burke, B. L. (2010). Deci & Ryan's SDT basics are covered in file 15 and not repeated.

**Their vocabulary.** SDT: basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness), autonomous vs controlled motivation, autonomy-supportive vs controlling interpersonal style. MI: collaboration, evocation, autonomy; change talk vs sustain talk; discrepancy; OARS skills (open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries) `[standard account]`; "righting reflex" and ambivalence.

**Drives/operands.** The needs in SDT are exactly drive-like operands. MI's operand is *ambivalence*, a two-sided state of approach and avoidance toward one behaviour: structurally identical to Miller's approach-avoidance conflict (file 16 section 3), and recognised in MI as "sustain talk vs change talk".

**Operators / equations.** No equations. Operators are interpersonal styles and speech acts: need-supportive (provide choice and rationale, acknowledge the other's perspective, minimise pressure) vs controlling (pressure, rewards used as control). MI operators: elicit change talk, reflect, avoid argument.

**Theorems or derived predictions.** SDT: autonomy support → need satisfaction → autonomous motivation → persistence and well-being. MI: arguing for change increases sustain talk (a reactance-type prediction); eliciting the person's own reasons increases change talk and subsequent change.

**Empirical standing, bluntly.**
- Ng et al. (2012): 184 independent data sets; practitioner autonomy support relates to patient need satisfaction, which relates to better mental and physical outcomes `[from abstract]`. Mostly **correlational**; the path model is not causal evidence. Ntoumanis et al. (2021, 73 intervention studies): small-to-medium changes in most SDT constructs and in health behaviour; increases in need support and autonomous motivation (not controlled motivation or amotivation) went with positive behaviour change; small positive changes in physical and psychological health `[read: abstract]`. Sheeran et al. (2020, JCCP): 56 articles, 65 independent tests of RCTs; d+ = .23 on health behaviour; publication and small-sample bias present, but effects stayed significant after correction (d+ ≥ .15); autonomous motivation and perceived competence mediated `[read: abstract]`.
- MI: Burke et al. (2003): effects .25 to .57 against no-treatment or placebo comparisons for alcohol, drugs, diet/exercise; no support for smoking or HIV risk; equivalent to other active treatments `[read: abstract]`. Lundahl et al. (2010): 119 studies, g = 0.28 against weak comparison groups, small and durable; g = 0.09 against specific active treatments, non-significant `[from abstract as quoted in search results]`. Blunt: MI's advantage over doing anything structured is near zero; its advantage over doing nothing is small to medium. The sustain-talk mechanism has process-study support (Apodaca et al. 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.jsat.2015.09.001, found by Crossref search on therapist behaviours and client change talk/sustain talk; not read).

**Operand/operator translation.** (a) Autonomy is an operand-like need whose *violation* triggers reactance; so "controlling" operators have a built-in cost to the other party's autonomy state. This is a candidate negative-feedback term in the algebra: operator application decreases the target's autonomy, which raises their resistance to the next operator. (b) MI treats the target's own approach/avoidance conflict as the object and uses operators that shift weight toward approach *by eliciting the target's own utterances* rather than adding pressure: an operator that acts via the target's own output (self-persuasion) rather than direct injection. Nothing in the surveyed apparatus formalises this.

## 11. Reactance and inoculation: resistance to operators

**Citations.** Brehm, J. W. (1966). *A Theory of Psychological Reactance*. Academic Press. (Book; not DOI-verified.) Brehm, S. S., & Brehm, J. W. (1981). *Psychological Reactance: A Theory of Freedom and Control*. Roozenbeek, J., van der Linden, S., Goldberg, B., Rathje, S., & Lewandowsky, S. (2022). Maertens, R., Roozenbeek, J., Basol, M., & van der Linden, S. (2021). Long-term effectiveness of inoculation against misinformation: three longitudinal experiments. *J. Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 27(1), 1-16. DOI 10.1037/xap0000315 (Crossref-confirmed).

**Reactance, vocabulary and structure.** Freedom, threat to freedom, reactance as a motivational state to restore the threatened freedom, boomerang effect, forbidden-fruit effect. Brehm's magnitude postulates `[standard account]`: reactance rises with the importance of the freedom, the proportion of freedoms threatened, and the strength of the threat. Dillard & Shen (2005) propose reactance is an intertwined mix of anger and negative cognitions, rather than an unmeasurable motivational state. Rains (2013), 20 studies, N = 4,942: the **intertwined** model (reactance as a latent factor indicated by anger and counterarguing) fit best `[from abstract as quoted in search results]`. No effect sizes retrieved.

**Reactance as a drive.** It is a counter-drive triggered by an *operator*, not by a need state. Its input is perceived pressure/control; its output is movement away from the pressured option and toward its opposite, sometimes. There is no equation in the original beyond the postulates; magnitude is described as increasing in the three factors. This is the strand's first explicit instance of **a drive whose input is another agent's operator**, which is directly relevant to a theorem about repeated application of operators: control attempts have a feedback cost.

**Backfire.** The boomerang effect is the classic backfire result; I did not find a meta-analytic effect size for backfire in the retrieved material. Message features that raise reactance are `[standard account]`: forceful language ("must", "ought"), explicit threats to choice, perceived manipulative intent. Li, Z., & Shi, J. (2026; online 2025). Message effects on psychological reactance: meta-analyses. *Human Communication Research*, 52(1), 38-52. DOI 10.1093/hcr/hqaf016 (Crossref-confirmed; not read) is the lead for effect sizes.

**Inoculation, vocabulary and structure.** McGuire (1964): a weakened attack (threat plus refutational preemption) triggers the target to build counterarguments, producing resistance to later, stronger attacks. The biological analogy is explicit. Operators: threat ("you will be attacked"), refutational preemption (supply the counterarguments), and the 2019+ "technique-based" version that trains on manipulation techniques rather than specific claims `[standard account]`.

**Empirical standing, bluntly.** Banas & Rains (2010): a reliable resistance effect, reportedly average d = 0.43 over 41 experiments `[unconfirmed: secondary blog only; abstract not retrievable in validation]`; both refutational-same and refutational-different treatments protect (supporting "umbrella protection"); the role of threat is questioned; effects decay with delay `[from search summaries of citing sources]`. Roozenbeek et al. (2022): seven preregistered studies, six randomised (n = 6,464) and a YouTube field study (n = 22,632) `[read: abstract]`; five short videos on manipulation techniques (emotional language, incoherence, false dichotomies, scapegoating, ad hominem) improved recognition of manipulation, confidence, discernment of trustworthy content and sharing decisions `[from abstract]`; I could not retrieve the field effect size. Maertens et al. (2021) report durability across longitudinal experiments and boosters; numbers not retrieved. A later inoculation meta-analysis exists: Lu, C., Hu, B., Li, Q., Bi, C., & Ju, X.-D. (2023). Psychological inoculation for credibility assessment, sharing intention, and discernment of misinformation: systematic review and meta-analysis. *J. Medical Internet Research*, 25, e49255. DOI 10.2196/49255: 42 studies, N = 42,530; misinformation credibility d = −0.36, credibility discernment d = 0.20, sharing discernment d = 0.18, no significant effect on misinformation sharing intention (d = −0.35, CI −0.79 to 0.09) `[read: abstract]`. It is criticised by Loughnan, van Stekelenburg, Pouwels & Kleemans (2025), *JMIR* 27, e64430, DOI 10.2196/64430, who argue at least 11 studies (35.7% weight) should have been excluded from one outcome category (Crossref-confirmed; summary from search). So the misinformation-inoculation literature has enough controversy over effect size and ecological validity that the d = 0.43 figure should be treated as a rough prior, not an estimate.

**Operand/operator translation.** Reactance: a resistance state R of the target, updated by the operator's perceived control: R' = R + g(perceived pressure). Because R reduces the effect of the *next* operator, operators don't commute with themselves: applying the same push twice is not 2× the effect. Inoculation: an operator applied *before* an attack that changes the target's response function to the attack class. It is an operator on operators (a second-order operator), exactly the structure a composable algebra needs a place for. Neither has a measured functional form.

## What exists vs what does not

**Exists.**
- An operator catalogue (93 BCTs, 16 clusters, with a formal ontology in progress) and a typed target scheme (COM-B, TDF). It is the best existing seed for operator names and sorts.
- A small number of quantified operator effects with meta-analytic support, mostly in the 0.2-0.5 range, usually weaker on behaviour than on intention.
- Exactly one very small, concrete operator with decent support: the if-then plan.
- A clear structural lesson: motive-raising operators leak; link-making and capacity-raising operators are better supported. And resistance (reactance, inoculation) is real and operator-triggered.
- Models of the conjunction type (threat × coping, M × A × P, C × O × M, attitude × norm × control) that agree qualitatively on *what* goes in and disagree on *how it combines*.

**Does not exist.**
- Any algebra of operators here. Composition is "set of techniques in one trial"; there are no identities, no commutation results, no inverses.
- Any tested multiplicative form. The products are asserted (EPPM, B = MAP, expectancy-value) but the fits are additive regressions; a direct multiplicative-vs-additive-vs-min comparison on threat and efficacy is what I did not find.
- Any model of **repeated or sequenced operators**, apart from reactance's qualitative postulates and the planning and monitoring literature. Order effects, saturation and habituation of an operator are not modelled.
- Any **dyadic or group** operator model. All of this is one communicator acting on one audience member, measured in population means. Subjective norm is the only social variable, and it is a belief, not a relation.
- Any bias-adjusted effect estimates for most operators; the nudge case suggests they could shrink a lot.
- Any validation of the *drive* interpretation: the constructs here are appraisals, intentions and beliefs, not drives in the sense of file 15, so the cluster says little about curiosity, fear-as-drive or desire except through fear appeals and ambivalence.
