Date: 2026-10-29. Survey for the social-algebra strand. Cluster: where incentives, authority and fairness act on drives, often with explicit formulas. Context: Aldred's aim, "an algebra which expresses basic human drives and group drives, which we can compose into equations, which we can then use to start making theorems and use to control or predict behavior".

**Summary.** This cluster contains the field's cleanest *explicit* organisational formulas (the MPS product, the Lazear-Rosen first-order condition, Adams's ratio) and, almost without exception, they are the part that has worn worst empirically. What survives is the qualitative structure: pay and fairness are read as *information about the relationship and about oneself*, so an incentive is an operator that acts on drives *and* on the agent's model of the principal. The one rigorous theory of that (Bénabou-Tirole) is a signalling model, not a drive model. The big meta-analyses (justice, psychological safety, leadership, breach) give correlations, almost all cross-sectional and single-source, and no equations at all. Individually these are the weakest evidence in the survey for causal operators, which is the algebra's need.

## 1. Adams's equity theory (1963)

**Citation.** Adams, J. S. (1963). Towards an understanding of inequity. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*, 67(5), 422-436. doi:10.1037/h0040968 (Crossref confirmed). Fuller statement: Adams (1965), "Inequity in social exchange", in Berkowitz (ed.), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* vol. 2, 267-299, doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(08)60108-2 (Crossref confirmed in validation).

**Their vocabulary.** Inputs (what a person brings: effort, skill, seniority), outcomes (what they receive: pay, status), referent other, inequity, tension, "restoring equity".

**Drives/operands.** A person p, a referent o, and two bundles (I, O) each. The drive is an aversive tension generated by inequity, whose size is claimed proportional to the discrepancy. [standard account]

**Operators / equations.** Equity: O_p / I_p = O_o / I_o. Inequity when unequal, in either direction (overpaid or underpaid). Tension ∝ |O_p/I_p − O_o/I_o|. Responses to reduce it (Adams's list): alter own inputs, alter own outcomes, cognitively distort inputs/outcomes, leave the field, act on the other (change their inputs/outcomes), change the referent. [from abstract/standard account]

**Derived predictions.** The famous 2x2 from experimental pay work: hourly overpaid workers raise inputs (quality/quantity); piece-rate overpaid workers lower quantity and raise quality; hourly underpaid lower output; piece-rate underpaid raise quantity at the cost of quality. [standard account]

**Empirical standing.** Underpayment effects are the robust half. Greenberg (1990), "Employee theft as a reaction to underpayment inequity: the hidden cost of pay cuts", *J. Applied Psychology* 75, 561-568, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.75.5.561 (Crossref confirmed): theft rose in plants with temporary pay cuts [title confirmed; effect from standard account]. Overpayment guilt effects are weak and inconsistent across the lab literature [standard account]. The theory is vague about referent choice and the weights in I and O, so it can fit most results post hoc; people differ in sensitivity (Huseman, R. C., Hatfield, J. D. & Miles, E. W. (1987). A new perspective on equity theory: the equity sensitivity construct. *Academy of Management Review*, 12(2), 222-234. doi:10.2307/258531, Crossref confirmed in validation; content from standard account). Blunt: the ratio is a heuristic, not a measurement law.

**Translation.** Operands: pairs of agents with attached (input, outcome) vectors. Operator: a relation, not a scalar attribute, `Equ(p,o) = O_p/I_p − O_o/I_o`. This is one of the few dyadic quantities in the drives literature. Fehr-Schmidt (survey 21) is the same idea with explicit asymmetric weights (envy vs guilt) and a utility form; prefer it when an equation is needed. The ratio form is awkward in an algebra (division, zero inputs); the difference form is cleaner.

## 2. Job Characteristics Model and the Motivating Potential Score

**Citations.** Hackman, J. R. & Oldham, G. R. (1976). Motivation through the design of work: test of a theory. *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*, 16(2), 250-279. doi:10.1016/0030-5073(76)90016-7 (Crossref confirmed). Fried, Y. & Ferris, G. R. (1987). The validity of the Job Characteristics Model: a review and meta-analysis. *Personnel Psychology*, 40, 287-322. doi:10.1111/j.1744-6570.1987.tb00605.x (confirmed). Humphrey, S. E., Nahrgang, J. D. & Morgeson, F. P. (2007). Integrating motivational, social, and contextual work design features: a meta-analytic summary and theoretical extension of the work design literature. *J. Applied Psychology*, 92(5), 1332-1356. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.92.5.1332 (confirmed).

**Vocabulary.** Five core job dimensions: skill variety (SV), task identity (TI), task significance (TS), autonomy (A), feedback (F). Three critical psychological states: experienced meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, knowledge of results. Outcomes: internal work motivation, satisfaction, performance, low absence/turnover. Moderator: growth-need strength (GNS), plus knowledge/skill and context satisfaction.

**Equation.** MPS = ((SV + TI + TS) / 3) × A × F. [standard account; the formula as asked by the brief]. Meaningfulness is carried by the first three dimensions averaged, responsibility by A, knowledge of results by F; the product means a zero in A or F zeroes the score. The model is a chain: job dimensions → psychological states → outcomes, moderated by GNS.

**Theorems / predictions.** The multiplicative form predicts a *non-compensatory* job: no amount of variety offsets absent autonomy. That is the one structural claim the formula carries over an additive index.

**Empirical standing.** Blunt: the multiplicative MPS did not beat a simple additive composite in the meta-analysis of the early literature (Fried & Ferris 1987) [from abstract/standard account, not re-read]. The core dimensions correlate with satisfaction and internal motivation; the moderator (GNS) and the mediation by the three states are weaker. Humphrey et al. pooled 259 studies and 219,625 participants; 14 work characteristics explained on average 43% of the variance in 19 worker attitudes and behaviours; motivational characteristics alone explained 34% of the variance in job satisfaction, social characteristics added 17% and work-context characteristics a further 4% [abstract read, PubMed 17845089]. The same abstract gives social characteristics an incremental 40% for organisational commitment and 24% for turnover intentions, larger than for satisfaction, so "the social side adds a little" would be wrong. The same summary says autonomy and social support were the strongest predictors of satisfaction (press report, weak). Most data are self-report, same-source, cross-sectional.

**Translation.** Operand: a job as a vector of five scalars; operator: a (multiplicative) map to a motivation scalar. It is a 16-style multiplicative law (cf. Vroom, Hull in 16) with an unusually explicit non-compensatory zero. Useful as a worked example of "zero factor kills the product". Not a social operator: the "other" appears only as a feedback source.

## 3. Herzberg's two-factor theory

**Citations.** Herzberg, F., Mausner, B. & Snyderman, B. B. (1959). *The Motivation to Work*. Wiley. Review: House, R. J. & Wigdor, L. A. (1967). Herzberg's dual-factor theory of job satisfaction and motivation: a review of the evidence and a criticism. *Personnel Psychology*, 20, 369-390. doi:10.1111/j.1744-6570.1967.tb02440.x (title confirmed). The 1959 book is not online here.

**Claim.** Satisfaction and dissatisfaction are two independent dimensions, not ends of one. Motivators (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement) raise satisfaction; hygiene factors (pay, supervision, policy, conditions) only prevent dissatisfaction. [standard account]

**Equations.** None.

**Empirical standing.** Blunt: the support came from one method, the critical-incident interview, in which people credit themselves for good events and blame the environment for bad ones (the self-serving attribution that House & Wigdor and later critics point to) [standard account]. Studies with other methods do not reproduce a clean split; pay and recognition often act on both sides. The theory is treated now as a historical source for intrinsic-versus-extrinsic, not as a result.

**Translation.** Nothing formal. Its surviving content is a hint that dissatisfaction removal and satisfaction creation are different operators, a distinction crowding (section 5) formalises better.

## 4. McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

**Citation.** McGregor, D. (1960). *The Human Side of Enterprise*. McGraw-Hill. Not resolvable via Crossref; background only.

**Claim.** Managers hold assumptions about employees (X: dislike work, need control; Y: self-directed, seek responsibility), and those assumptions shape management practice, which in turn shapes the behaviour that confirms them. [standard account]

**Equations/theorems.** None. The self-fulfilling loop is the structure: practice P(assumption) → behaviour → evidence → assumption.

**Standing.** A framing, not a tested theory. Its descendant is the experimental finding that being treated as untrustworthy (monitored, fined) changes behaviour, which is the crowding evidence in section 5.

**Translation.** A feedback loop between a principal's belief operand and an agent's drive, the shape later formalised as Bénabou-Tirole.

## 5. Motivation crowding-out

### 5.1 Reviews and meta-analyses

- Frey, B. S. & Jegen, R. (2001). Motivation crowding theory. *J. Economic Surveys*, 15(5), 589-611. doi:10.1111/1467-6419.00150 (confirmed). Review of the economics literature; conclusion that crowding-out exists under specified conditions, with crowding-in also possible [from abstract/standard account].
- Deci, E. L., Koestner, R. & Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Psychological Bulletin*, 125(6), 627-668. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627 (confirmed). 128 studies. Effects on free-choice behaviour: engagement-contingent d = −0.40, completion-contingent −0.36, performance-contingent −0.28; on self-reported interest, engagement-contingent −0.15 and completion-contingent −0.17; positive feedback +0.33 (free choice) and +0.31 (self-reported interest). All tangible rewards together (92 studies): d = −0.34 (CI −0.39, −0.28) on free choice; expected tangible rewards: d = −0.36 (CI −0.42, −0.30), both heterogeneous; unexpected and task-noncontingent tangible rewards: no significant effect. Tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than for college students; verbal rewards less enhancing for children [read: abstract and results sections].
- Cerasoli, C. P., Nicklin, J. M. & Ford, M. T. (2014). Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives jointly predict performance: a 40-year meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(4), 980-1008. doi:10.1037/a0035661 (confirmed). k = 183, N = 212,468; intrinsic motivation predicts performance at ρ = .21 to .45 whether or not incentives are present; in crowding-out fashion it matters less when incentives are directly tied to performance; intrinsic motivation predicts quality of performance better, incentives quantity [abstract read, PubMed 24491020].

**Blunt standing.** Deci et al. is a contested meta-analysis (Cameron, J. & Pierce, W. D. (1994), *Review of Educational Research* 64(3), 363-423, doi:10.3102/00346543064003363; Eisenberger, R. & Cameron, J. (1996), "Detrimental effects of reward: reality or myth?", *American Psychologist* 51(11), 1153-1166, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.51.11.1153; both Crossref confirmed in validation. They argued the undermining effect is small and limited to expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards. Deci et al. 1999 is in large part a reply to them, and itself reports no effect for unexpected or task-noncontingent rewards, so the two camps agree more on the conditions than on the framing). The psychology finding is about *interest in a task afterwards* in mostly educational and lab settings. Cerasoli's title says incentives and intrinsic motivation both predict performance, which constrains any claim that incentives simply destroy motivation in workplaces. Frey & Jegen emphasise conditions: crowding-out is a conditional, not a general, law.

### 5.2 Gneezy & Rustichini

- Gneezy, U. & Rustichini, A. (2000). A fine is a price. *J. Legal Studies*, 29(1), 1-17. doi:10.1086/468061 (confirmed). Ten private day-care centres in Haifa observed for 20 weeks: 4 weeks baseline; from week 5 a NIS 10 fine for parents arriving more than 10 minutes late in six centres, four controls; late arrivals rose steadily over 2-3 weeks and settled at a level higher than baseline; the fine was removed at the start of week 17 and in the following 4 weeks lateness stayed at the high level [read]. The authors explicitly do not claim the effect generalises ("no parent would come late if a very large fine were involved") and give an incomplete-contract / information model of it (parents learn the manager is "mild"). Authors' reading: the fine tells parents the "price" of lateness, converting a social obligation into a purchasable good, and the information in the penalty changes how the situation is read.
- Gneezy, U. & Rustichini, A. (2000). Pay enough or don't pay at all. *Quarterly J. Economics*, 115(3), 791-810. doi:10.1162/003355300554917 (confirmed). Two experiments [read]. (a) IQ test, 50 questions, 160 students, NIS 60 show-up fee plus 0, 10 agorot (0.1 NIS), 1 NIS or 3 NIS per correct answer: means 28.4, 23.07, 34.7, 34.1. Low pay < no pay only at the 10% level (Mann-Whitney p = .0875); 1 NIS and 3 NIS above no pay at p ≈ .07 and indistinguishable from each other. (b) Donation collection by 180 Israeli high-school students: no pay 238.6, 1% commission 153.6, 10% commission 219.3; 1% < no pay at p ≈ .10; 10% > 1% (p ≈ .05) but **not** above no pay (p = .71). So "high payment beats both" holds only weakly in (a) and fails in (b): the robust pattern is the non-monotonicity, a dip at small pay, not that paying enough beats not paying.
- Gneezy, U., Meier, S. & Rey-Biel, P. (2011). When and why incentives (don't) work to modify behavior. *J. Economic Perspectives*, 25(4), 191-210. doi:10.1257/jep.25.4.191 (confirmed). Review; main message that incentives can backfire via the information they convey and the identity they change [standard account].

**Standing.** Blunt: the day-care result is one field study with small numbers and one location; it is cited far beyond its evidential weight and I know of no clean direct replication [standard account, unverified]. Its value is as a counterexample to "monotone in price".

### 5.3 Bénabou & Tirole (2003)

**Citation.** Bénabou, R. & Tirole, J. (2003). Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. *Review of Economic Studies*, 70(3), 489-520. doi:10.1111/1467-937X.00253 (confirmed). Readable copy not obtained legitimately; re-read needed. Sections 1-2 and all proposition statements [from abstract/standard account].

**General frame (section 1, "the looking-glass self").** Principal learns β (task difficulty or agent ability), picks a policy p (bonus, help, delegation, surveillance, disclosure); agent sees p and a private signal σ, forms β̂(σ, p), chooses effort e*(p, β̂). The principal's first-order condition has three terms: E_σ[∂U_P/∂p + ∂U_P/∂e·∂e*/∂p + ∂U_P/∂e·∂e*/∂β̂·∂β̂/∂p | β] = 0 (their eq. 1). The third term is the new one: the agent reads the policy as information. Two channels: a *profitability effect* (the principal's payoff from a policy depends on β, a sorting condition) and a *trust effect* (the principal's information bears only on the agent's payoff, and she is unsure of the agent's σ).

**Section 2 model.** Binary effort; cost c, success probability θ, agent's success payoff V, principal's W, bonus b on success. Agent works iff θ(V + b) ≥ ĉ(σ, b) = E[c | σ, b], giving a threshold σ*(b). Principal maximises θ[1 − G(σ*(b) | c)](W − b). Signal satisfies MLRP.

**Propositions [from abstract/standard account; readable copy not obtained legitimately; re-read needed].**
- Prop. 1 (cost private to principal): a bonus raises effort in the short run, a principal who knows the task is harder offers a bigger bonus, and the bonus lowers the agent's later view of the task.
- Prop. 2: the same result when the principal's private information is about ability rather than cost.
- Prop. 3 (two ability types, no lump sums): the able agent gets a low bonus; the less able one a mix, and the chance of no bonus rises with initial self-confidence.
- Prop. 4 (lump sums allowed): a unique separating equilibrium; a higher-powered scheme motivates in the short run and permanently damages self-confidence.
- Props. 5-6: delegation raises effort and signals ability; help lowers effort and signals the opposite.
- Props. 7-8 (section 4): the principal may disclose bad news and hide good news to keep authority, so the relationship turns more antagonistic over time.

The abstract's summary line is exact: incentives are "only weak reinforcers in the short run, and negative reinforcers in the long run". Two corrections to the earlier text: the long-run harm is to the agent's *beliefs* (self-confidence, task attractiveness), not a separate reputation mechanism; and the paper covers empowerment, help and ego bashing, not only bonuses. Note also the authors' warning (the "improper causal attributions" remark): because bonuses are offered precisely where tasks are hard, an observer will *underestimate* the true incentive effect.

**Standing.** It is a theoretical reconciliation, not an estimated model. It explains the direction of Gneezy-Rustichini type results but each case is only qualitatively consistent.

**Translation.** This is the most algebra-like object in the section, and the one with a stated first-order condition for "an operator read as information" (eq. 1 above): the agent's drive depends on a *belief about the principal's information*, so the operand has two layers (drive, belief-about-other) and the incentive operator acts on the second layer first. Closely related to psychological games (21).

## 6. Principal-agent models with intrinsic motivation, mission and identity

- Besley, T. & Ghatak, M. (2005). Competition and incentives with motivated agents. *American Economic Review*, 95(3), 616-636. doi:10.1257/0002828054201413 (confirmed: title, venue, year, volume, pages). Agents and principals who share a mission value the output directly; motivated agents accept lower-powered incentives, and matching motivated agents to motivated principals is efficient [from abstract/standard account]. No equation retrieved.
- Akerlof, G. A. & Kranton, R. E. (2005). Identity and the economics of organizations. *J. Economic Perspectives*, 19(1), 9-32. doi:10.1257/0895330053147930 (confirmed). Utility depends on identity: U_j = U_j(a_j, a_−j, I_j), with I_j a function of own and others' actions, the person's social category c_j, characteristics ε_j relative to the prescribed behaviour P for the category [standard account; the form is from the 2000 QJE paper and the JEP, not read]. Insider/outsider identification with the organisation can substitute for pay. Cross-ref 10.

**Standing.** Both are theory with illustrations (Akerlof-Kranton uses case studies, e.g. the US Army and public schools; the specific cases are from standard account). Little structural estimation. Blunt: they add *a utility term* for mission or identity, which shifts the optimal contract, but they do not model how the identity is built or lost over time.

**Translation.** Operand: an agent with (effort, category). Identity is an attribute that changes the payoff of actions, not a drive that changes by interaction. Useful as the economists' version of "a group-level drive acting on an individual". The algebra can ask where Akerlof-Kranton's `P` (the prescription) comes from; they take it as given.

## 7. Tournament theory (Lazear & Rosen 1981)

**Citation.** Lazear, E. P. & Rosen, S. (1981). Rank-order tournaments as optimum labor contracts. *J. Political Economy*, 89(5), 841-864. doi:10.1086/261010 (confirmed).

**Model [read, section I, eqs. 1-10; restated here in our own words; access route not recorded, to be confirmed].** Two risk-neutral workers j, k. Lifetime output q_j = μ_j + ε_j, where μ_j is "investment" (skill or average output, chosen before luck is realised) at cost C(μ), C′, C″ > 0, and ε_j is i.i.d. noise with variance σ². Winner gets W₁, loser W₂. P = Pr(q_j > q_k) = Pr(μ_j − μ_k > ξ) = G(μ_j − μ_k), where ξ = ε_k − ε_j has density g and cdf G (their eq. 3). Worker maximises P·W₁ + (1−P)·W₂ − C(μ_j); reaction function (W₁ − W₂)·g(μ_j − μ_k) − C′(μ_j) = 0 (eq. 5); at the symmetric Nash equilibrium **C′(μ) = (W₁ − W₂)·g(0)** (eq. 6). The brief's form is therefore correct, with g the density of the *difference* of the two noise terms. Effort depends on the spread, prize levels only on entry. Zero profit gives Vμ = (W₁ + W₂)/2 (eq. 7); the optimal prize structure sets V = C′(μ), the same efficient allocation as a piece rate (eq. 9); W₁,₂ = Vμ ± V/2g(0) (eq. 10), read as each player posting an entrance fee and playing a fair winner-take-all gamble. A pure-strategy equilibrium needs enough noise (their footnote 2): "contests are feasible only when chance is a significant factor".

**Derived predictions.** (1) Effort rises with the spread. (2) Noisier output (lower g(0)) reduces effort at fixed spread, so the optimal spread rises with σ (stated in the paper for normal ε). (3) Because only rank matters, common shocks that hit both workers drop out [standard account; L-R's own emphasis is on cheaper monitoring of rank than of levels]. (4) Sabotage of rivals and reduced cooperation under large spreads are **not in Lazear & Rosen 1981**; they come from Lazear, E. P. (1989). Pay equality and industrial politics. *J. Political Economy*, 97(3), 561-580. doi:10.1086/261616 (Crossref confirmed in validation; content from standard account).

**Standing.** Empirical support from sports: Ehrenberg, R. G. & Bognanno, M. L. (1990). Do tournaments have incentive effects? *J. Political Economy*, 98(6), 1307-1324, doi:10.1086/261736 (PGA Tour), and the companion "The incentive effects of tournaments revisited: evidence from the European PGA Tour", *ILR Review*, 43(3), 74-S-88-S, doi:10.1177/001979399004300305 (both Crossref confirmed in validation; finding that higher purses lower scores is from standard account); and from experiments [standard account]. Review: Connelly, B. L., Tihanyi, L., Crook, T. R. & Gangloff, K. A. (2014). Tournament theory: thirty years of contests and competitions. *J. Management*, 40(1), 16-47, doi:10.1177/0149206313498902 (Crossref confirmed; online 2013). Blunt: field tests are in settings where output is observable and the payoff is exactly the prize, which is not a typical workplace.

**Translation.** This is the clearest *status competition* operator: a game on rank with a closed-form equilibrium. Operand: ordered set of agents with a rank-dependent payoff. A direct bridge to social-preference extensions (envy, relative concerns, 21).

## 8. Organisational justice

**Citations.** Colquitt, J. A. (2001). On the dimensionality of organizational justice: a construct validation of a measure. *J. Applied Psychology*, 86(3), 386-400. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.86.3.386 (confirmed). Colquitt, J. A., Conlon, D. E., Wesson, M. J., Porter, C. O. L. H. & Ng, K. Y. (2001). Justice at the millennium: a meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research. *J. Applied Psychology*, 86(3), 425-445. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.86.3.425 (confirmed title, venue and the five authors in Crossref). Colquitt, J. A., Scott, B. A., Rodell, J. B., Long, D. M., Zapata, C. P., Conlon, D. E. & Wesson, M. J. (2013). Justice at the millennium, a decade later: a meta-analytic test of social exchange and affect-based perspectives. *J. Applied Psychology*, 98(2), 199-236. doi:10.1037/a0031757 (confirmed, authors confirmed).

**Vocabulary.** Distributive (fairness of outcomes, Adams's ratio), procedural (voice, consistency, bias suppression, accuracy, correctability: Leventhal 1980), interpersonal and informational (the "interactional" pair; Bies & Moag 1986). Group-value model (Lind & Tyler 1988): procedures signal standing in the group. [standard account for the sources named]

**Equations.** None beyond the Adams ratio for distributive justice.

**Empirical standing.** Confirmatory factor analyses favour four dimensions over two or three (Colquitt 2001) [from abstract via search]. The 2013 meta-analysis, 493 independent samples [abstract read, PubMed 23458336], finds the links from justice to task performance and citizenship behaviour mediated by social exchange quality (trust, commitment, perceived organisational support, leader-member exchange), but this mediation did not appear for counterproductive behaviour; affect (positive and negative) also mediates [from abstract via search]. The 2001 meta-analysis covered 183 justice studies [via search]. Per-dimension correlations were not retrieved. Blunt: justice is measured by perceptions on surveys, so the correlation with outcomes also carries same-source and mood effects.

**Translation.** Justice perceptions are an operand attached to an agent-authority dyad. They behave like a variable that the authority controls through procedures and that turns into trust and then discretionary effort. The measurable structure is: three or four parallel fairness channels combining into one exchange-quality state. Equity (section 1) is the distributive channel.

## 9. Psychological contracts and breach

**Citations.** Rousseau, D. M. (1989). Psychological and implied contracts in organizations. *Employee Responsibilities and Rights Journal*, 2(2), 121-139. doi:10.1007/BF01384942 (confirmed). Zhao, H., Wayne, S. J., Glibkowski, B. C. & Bravo, J. (2007). The impact of psychological contract breach on work-related outcomes: a meta-analysis. *Personnel Psychology*, 60(3), 647-680. doi:10.1111/j.1744-6570.2007.00087.x (confirmed title, venue, year). Rousseau's 1995 book *Psychological Contracts in Organizations* (Sage) is standard but not resolved.

**Concept.** The employee's belief about the reciprocal obligations between self and employer, perceived promissory; *breach* is the perception that the employer has failed to fulfil one; *violation* is the felt emotional reaction. Transactional versus relational contracts. [standard account]

**Equations.** None.

**Standing.** Zhao et al., modelled on affective events theory: breach → affect (violation, mistrust) → attitudes (satisfaction, commitment, intent to leave) → effectiveness (turnover, citizenship, in-role performance). Reported pattern: strong association with violation and mistrust; strong negative with satisfaction; moderate negative with commitment; moderate positive with intent to leave; modest negative with in-role performance and citizenship; related to all outcomes except actual turnover; affect mediates [via search summary of abstract; the "51 samples" figure I had in mind was not confirmed]. Blunt: breach is a perception, measured by survey; causal direction is untested by most of the samples.

**Translation.** A contract is a stored set of expected operators between two agents (promises). Breach is a violation of the *expected value* of an operator, which makes the effect a prediction-error drive (cross-ref the expectation models in 16 and the curiosity/fear survey 17). This is the natural place for an "expected action" operand.

## 10. Leadership as an operator on followers' drives

**Citations.**
- Judge, T. A. & Piccolo, R. F. (2004). Transformational and transactional leadership: a meta-analytic test of their relative validity. *J. Applied Psychology*, 89(5), 755-768. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.89.5.755 (confirmed). 626 correlations from 87 sources; overall validity of transformational leadership ρ = .44, which "generalized over longitudinal and multisource designs"; contingent reward .39; laissez-faire −.37; transformational correlates .80 with contingent reward and −.65 with laissez-faire; contingent reward had stronger relations than transformational on several criteria; transformational failed to predict leader job performance once the other dimensions were controlled [abstract read, PubMed 15506858]. The ρ = .44 is the source's figure, a corrected mean correlation pooled across criteria (follower satisfaction and motivation, leader effectiveness, group performance: criterion list from standard account), not a causal effect.
- Gerstner, C. R. & Day, D. V. (1997). Meta-analytic review of leader-member exchange theory: correlates and construct issues. *J. Applied Psychology*, 82(6), 827-844. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.82.6.827 (confirmed). Dulebohn, J. H. et al. (2012). A meta-analysis of antecedents and consequences of leader-member exchange. *J. Management*, 38(6), 1715-1759. doi:10.1177/0149206311415280 (confirmed title, venue, pages; year in Crossref is 2011 online, 2012 print).
- Hogg, M. A. (2001). A social identity theory of leadership. *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 5(3), 184-200. doi:10.1207/S15327957PSPR0503_1 (confirmed title and venue). Claim: as group identification rises, followers weight leader *prototypicality* (how well the leader represents the group) over leader task-skill. [standard account]
- Bass, B. M. (1985) *Leadership and Performance Beyond Expectations*; Graen & Uhl-Bien (1995) for LMX; neither resolved here.

**Equations.** None in any of these. Hogg states testable propositions verbally. LMX is a dyadic quality (low/high exchange) per leader-follower pair.

**Standing.** Blunt: the headline ρ = .44 is a correlation of ratings of leader behaviour with ratings of effectiveness, much of it same-source (the authors report it holding up in multisource designs, which softens but does not remove this). Judge and Piccolo's own numbers show that "transformational" overlaps contingent reward at .80, so the construct is not well separated. Dulebohn and Gerstner-Day give moderate correlations with satisfaction, commitment and performance, but LMX has the classic problem that a high-quality relationship may be an outcome of good performance rather than a cause. Hogg's prototypicality predictions have lab and some field support [standard account; not checked].

**Translation.** Leader as operator: three different forms. (a) *Transformational* — acts on followers' goals and identification (a change of the operand's goal vector). (b) *Transactional/contingent reward* — a conditional payoff, the section 5 operator. (c) *LMX* — an edge weight on a dyad, which is an algebra-friendly object (a labelled relation, as in 01 and 03). Hogg's account turns the group's norm into the operand against which the leader is evaluated. Nothing is composed or has a closed form.

## 11. Voice, exit, loyalty, silence and psychological safety

**Citations.**
- Hirschman, A. O. (1970). *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. Harvard University Press. Cross-ref 10. Not resolvable via Crossref.
- Edmondson, A. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(2), 350-383. doi:10.2307/2666999 (confirmed).
- Frazier, M. L., Fainshmidt, S., Klinger, R. L., Pezeshkan, A. & Vracheva, V. (2017). Psychological safety: a meta-analytic review and extension. *Personnel Psychology*, 70(1), 113-165. doi:10.1111/peps.12183 (confirmed title, venue, volume, pages and all five authors; Crossref gives 2016 online).

**Hirschman [standard account].** When quality of a firm or organisation declines, members can exit (leave) or voice (complain, try to change it); loyalty makes exit costly and so activates voice. Qualitative; he gives informal conditions (the comparative effectiveness of exit and voice depends on competition and loyalty). No theorems stated in his text. Formalisations exist later.

**Edmondson (1999) [abstract read].** Team psychological safety is "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking"; in a multimethod field study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company it is associated with learning behaviour, team efficacy is not once safety is controlled, and learning behaviour mediates between safety and team performance. Context support and team-leader coaching are the antecedents modelled.

**Frazier et al. 2017.** 136 independent samples, over 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups; ranks antecedents by effect size, tests whether psychological safety predicts task performance and citizenship beyond positive leader relations and work engagement, checks research design and national culture as moderators, and compares individual and group levels [from the abstract]. A summary reports that antecedents (leadership, team design) and outcomes (learning, performance, engagement) are confirmed with moderate strength, and that most studies are cross-sectional, so causal claims are not warranted [secondary summary]. Specific coefficients not retrieved.

**Standing.** Blunt: psychological safety has a convincing measure and consistent correlations, but its construct overlaps with trust and leader relations, and the causal direction safety → performance is supported mainly by correlation. Silence (Morrison, E. W. & Milliken, F. J. (2000). Organizational silence: a barrier to change and development in a pluralistic world. *Academy of Management Review*, 25(4), 706-725, doi:10.2307/259200, Crossref confirmed in validation) as the mechanism by which fear suppresses voice is theory plus survey evidence.

**Translation.** The fear drive (cross-ref 17) is an operand at the *team* level, set by leader behaviour and shared perception, and whose effect is to suppress an observable (voice, information). Hirschman supplies the choice structure over three actions (exit, voice, stay-loyal) and the interplay: loyalty raises the price of exit and converts a leaving agent into a speaking one. Psychological safety as a scalar attached to a group, with transmission via the leader, is the single best fit in this cluster for "a group-level state that modulates individual expression of drive".

## What exists vs what does not

**Exists.**
- Explicit organisational formulas: Adams's ratio, the MPS product, the Lazear-Rosen first-order condition (derived, with closed-form prizes), the Akerlof-Kranton utility form, the Bénabou-Tirole signalling model (derived propositions, no closed-form contract in general).
- A large set of meta-analyses (justice, breach, safety, leadership, work design, rewards) giving effect sizes between perceived states and outcomes.
- A coherent *qualitative* theory of what an incentive does to a relation: it is read as information (price, bad news, breach, signal of distrust).
- Dyadic constructs ready to be edges: equity, LMX, psychological contract, trust.

**Does not exist (as first written, overstated; corrected in validation).**
- ~~A dynamic model in which a drive evolves under organisational operators. The formulas above are static.~~ The formulas *surveyed above* are static (Bénabou-Tirole has only a two-period element, Prop. 8). But formal, dynamic models of individual work motivation do exist and were missed: the self-regulation / control-theory line of Vancouver and colleagues, which writes goal-striving and goal-choice as coupled discrepancy-reducing loops simulated over time and fitted to data. Vancouver, J. B., Weinhardt, J. M. & Schmidt, A. M. (2010). A formal, computational theory of multiple-goal pursuit: integrating goal-choice and goal-striving processes. *J. Applied Psychology*, 95(6), 985-1008, doi:10.1037/a0020628 [abstract read: a dynamic computational model reproduces the reversal in which relative goal discrepancy predicts resource allocation positively early and negatively near the deadline]. Also Vancouver, Weinhardt & Vigo (2014), *OBHDP* 124(1), 56-74, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2013.12.002 (adds learning); Vancouver & Purl (2017), self-efficacy, *JAP* 102(4), 599-616, doi:10.1037/apl0000177; Ballard, Yeo, Loft, Vancouver & Neal (2016), the MGPM*, *JAP* 101(9), 1240-1265, doi:10.1037/apl0000121; Ballard, Vancouver & Neal (2018), deadlines, *JAP* 103(11), 1242-1264, doi:10.1037/apl0000304; Vancouver, Wang & Li (2020, online 2018), a dynamic computational model of the integrated model of work motivation, *Organizational Research Methods* 23(2), 238-274, doi:10.1177/1094428118780308 (all Crossref confirmed; contents beyond the 2010 abstract from standard account). These are drive-like state variables (discrepancy, expectancy, self-efficacy) with explicit update rules, i.e. exactly the dynamic form this survey said was absent. What is still true is narrower: none of them takes pay, fairness, leadership or safety as the input operator, and they are individual-level.
- Composition rules among the operators: how pay, fairness, leadership and safety combine on one worker. Humphrey et al. report variance shares (additive, motivational then social then context) but no interaction structure.
- Causal identification at scale. Most large meta-analyses are cross-sectional, single-source survey data. The best causal evidence (crowding experiments, day-care field study, tournament sports data) is narrow.
- Group-level drives with equations. Psychological safety is a group-level state but has no dynamics. Nearest miss: DeShon, R. P., Kozlowski, S. W. J., Schmidt, A. M., Milner, K. R. & Wiechmann, D. (2004). A multiple-goal, multilevel model of feedback effects on the regulation of individual and team performance. *J. Applied Psychology*, 89(6), 1035-1056, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.89.6.1035 (Crossref confirmed; content from standard account): parallel individual and team goal-regulation loops with feedback deciding which level gets effort. It is a conceptual dynamic model tested empirically, not a set of equations, so the claim stands in its narrow form.
- A replication-clean organisational law. Herzberg failed, MPS multiplication did not beat additive, overpayment equity is weak, crowding is conditional.

**For the algebra.** Take from here: (1) the idea that an incentive operator has two actions, one on payoff and one on the agent's belief about the principal; (2) the dyadic variables (equity, LMX, contract expectation) as edge labels; (3) the non-compensatory product as one candidate combination rule; (4) tournaments as the rank-competition operator with a worked equilibrium. Do not take from here: any numeric coefficient as a law.
