# Prior work 42: dynamic and formal models, drive by drive (status, belonging, care, play, sex, autonomy, fairness, meaning, aggression, disgust)

Date: 2026-10-29. Survey for the social-algebra strand. Aldred's clarification: curiosity, fear and desire were examples; the aim is a new or updated **equation set covering the full range of human and group drives**. This file asks, for each remaining major social drive: is there a formal model of its **dynamics** (state variable, set point or accumulation, satiation or decay, trigger, coupling), and how good is the evidence? Cross-references: 06, 09, 29 (winner-loser, hierarchy in animals), 15 (SDT, taxonomies), 17 (curiosity, fear, desire, plus ostracism and disgust entries), 18 (uncertainty-identity), 20 (dyadic coupling), 21 (inequity aversion, psychological games), 22 (social homeostasis, Panksepp, testosterone), 24 (significance quest), 25 (parasite-stress), 31 (ethology), 33 (attachment, effectance, play development).

**Method and honesty note.** (Validation 2026-10-29: abstracts were read for the works tagged `[abstract read]`, and the Bonabeau equations were read in Stauffer's restatements; see the log at the end.) This session verified bibliographic records against Crossref (titles, authors, years, DOIs). No full paper was read at source. Every substantive claim below is therefore `[from abstract/standard account]` (my background knowledge of the literature, checked only at the level of "this paper exists and has this title"). Equations are given as the field states them and **must be checked against the primary text before anything is built on them**; where I am unsure of a form I say "form not stated here" rather than guess. DOIs marked "confirmed" resolved at Crossref this session; "not located" means a Crossref lookup did not return it.

**Headline.** Across ten drives, only two have anything resembling a **dynamics law** with state variable and update rule: status (Bonabeau-type self-organising hierarchy; an animal-derived model, never validated on humans, and its phase transition turned out to be model-dependent: see 1.2) and fairness-as-evolved-strategy (Nowak et al. 2000, replicator dynamics, not a within-person drive law). Belonging has the best **verbal staged dynamics** (Williams) and a solid homeostatic base (22). Play has an empirical **deprivation-rebound** regularity and the 50:50 hypothesis (not supported in its wolf test), but no equation. Everything else is static utility, verbal process diagram, or typology. This is the honest state of the field and should be priced into the algebra: for most social drives the algebra would be **proposing** the dynamics, not importing them.

## 1. Status, dominance, prestige

### 1.1 Anderson, Hildreth & Howland 2015: is status a fundamental motive?
**Citation.** Anderson, C., Hildreth, J. A. D., & Howland, L. (2015). Is the desire for status a fundamental human motive? A review of the empirical literature. *Psychological Bulletin*, 141(3), 574-601. DOI 10.1037/a0038781 (confirmed).
- **Their vocabulary.** Status as respect and admiration in others' eyes; "desire for status" as a motive; fundamental-motive criteria (universality, functionality, automaticity, independence of other motives).
- **Drives/operands.** One drive: status desire. State variable: **the person's perceived standing in others' eyes** (ordinal, relative), not an accumulating deprivation level.
- **Operators/equations.** None [from abstract/standard account]. It is a review; its output is a criteria checklist and a list of consequences (status affects wellbeing, health, behaviour). 22 already records it; not repeated.
- **Derived predictions.** Status gains raise subjective wellbeing, and loss of status is distressing, independent of material gain; people will pay for status. Directional only.
- **Empirical standing.** A review, so as strong as its sources; much of the underlying social psychology is cross-sectional or lab-based. No dynamic parameters (rate of status adaptation, set point) extracted.
- **Translation.** Operand: `status(i)` as an **ordinal rank position** within a group, plus a felt-need scalar. Whether the need adapts to level (hedonic treadmill) is not established.

### 1.2 Bonabeau, Theraulaz & Deneubourg 1995: self-organising hierarchy
**Citation.** Bonabeau, E., Theraulaz, G., & Deneubourg, J.-L. (1995). Phase diagram of a model of self-organizing hierarchies. *Physica A*, 217, 373-392. DOI 10.1016/0378-4371(95)00064-e (confirmed). Follow-up: Bonabeau, Theraulaz & Deneubourg (1999), Dominance orders in animal societies: the self-organization hypothesis revisited, *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology*, DOI 10.1006/bulm.1999.0108 (confirmed).
- **Their vocabulary.** Agents on a lattice, "winner/loser effect", "dominance order", "egalitarian vs hierarchical phase", phase transition in the control parameters.
- **Drives/operands.** Agents with a scalar **dominance weight** (w_i) that rises on winning and falls on losing. Agents move at random on a lattice; a fight occurs on meeting.
- **Operators/equations** (corrected in validation; abstract [read], model equations [read in Stauffer 2003 and Schulze & Stauffer 2004, arXiv cond-mat/0208388 and cond-mat/0405697, which restate it], not in the 1995 paper itself, which is paywalled). Agents fill a fraction ρ of a square lattice and diffuse; when i tries to move onto k's site they fight. i wins with probability **Q = 1 / (1 + exp(η (h_k − h_i)))**, where h ("force") counts past victories minus past defeats. The winner takes the contested site. **Forgetting**: at every time step all h are multiplied by (1 − f) (f = 0.1 in Stauffer's runs), so memory of fights decays exponentially. η plays the role of an inverse temperature: how deterministic fights are. The survey earlier described "fixed increment to winner and loser"; in the restated model it is ±1 per fight, with the decay as the relaxation term. The paper's own result (abstract [read]): **with forgetting, the formation of hierarchy shows subcritical or supercritical bifurcations as density ρ is varied, the nature of the transition depends on η, and there is a dynamical tricritical point in the ρ-η plane.** "Stable linear hierarchy" was the survey's gloss; the order parameter is inequality in win probabilities, not linearity.
- **Derived predictions.** Hierarchy is an emergent phase, not a built-in; below a critical density forgetting outpaces fights and the group stays egalitarian; η decides whether the transition is continuous or discontinuous. Numerical and mean-field, not a proof.
- **The transition is not robust (added in validation; this matters for borrowing it).** Sousa & Stauffer (2000), Reinvestigation of self-organizing social hierarchies, *Int. J. Mod. Phys. C* 11, 1063-1066, DOI 10.1142/s0129183100000894 (confirmed) found the 1995 transition to be **an artifact of non-stationarity**; with a rule giving stationarity the sharp transition vanished (as summarised by Stauffer 2003 [read]). Stauffer (2003), *Int. J. Mod. Phys. C* 14, 237-239, DOI 10.1142/s0129183103004425 (confirmed) recovers a sharp, first-order transition (near ρ ≈ 0.32 for f = 0.1) only by adding a **feedback from group inequality**: η replaced by σ, the standard deviation of win probabilities across the population [read]. Schulze & Stauffer (2004), *Advances in Complex Systems* 7, 289-294, DOI 10.1142/s0219525904000184 (confirmed) add individual abilities and find the phase diagram little changed [read, abstract]. So the egalitarian-to-hierarchical phase transition depends on a modelling choice; the update rule itself is sound.
- **Empirical standing.** The 1995 paper is theoretical (statistical physics); the survey's earlier "fitted to ants, wasps and fish qualitatively" is not supported by the abstract and is removed. The winner/loser-effect assumption is supported in many animals, but the specific meta-analytic claim "winner effects modest, loser effects stronger" could not be traced to a source and is moved to Unconfirmed. Mesterton-Gibbons, Dai & Goubault (2016), Modeling the evolution of winner and loser effects: a survey and prospectus, *Math. Biosci.* 274, 33-44, DOI 10.1016/j.mbs.2016.02.002 (confirmed record) is the modelling review. **Never validated on human groups.** Hemelrijk's DomWorld is the primate successor: e.g. Hemelrijk, Wantia & Isler (2008), Female dominance over males in primates: self-organisation and sexual dimorphism, *PLoS ONE* 3, e2678, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0002678 (confirmed record; not read).
- **Translation.** This is the one real **state + update + coupling** status model. Operand `w_i`; operator `fight(i,j)` returns winner and updates both weights. Directly usable as the status sub-algebra, flagged "animal-derived" and with the phase-transition claim treated as model-dependent (Sousa & Stauffer 2000).

### 1.3 Human hierarchy: dominance versus prestige, and reinforcement
- **Henrich & Gil-White 2001.** The evolution of prestige: freely conferred deference as a mechanism for enhancing the benefits of cultural transmission. *Evolution and Human Behavior*, 22, 165-196. DOI 10.1016/s1090-5138(00)00071-4 (confirmed). Distinguishes **prestige** (deference freely given to a skilled model whose behaviour others want to copy) from **dominance** (deference extracted by threat). No equations in the sense of dynamics; verbal evolutionary argument [from abstract].
- **Cheng, Tracy, Foulsham, Kingstone & Henrich 2013.** Two ways to the top: evidence that dominance and prestige are distinct yet viable avenues to social rank and influence. *J. Personality and Social Psychology*, 104, 103-125. DOI 10.1037/a0030398 (confirmed). Empirical: in group tasks both dominant and prestigious members attain influence, via different routes; dominant members are less liked [from abstract]. Gives the algebra **two status operands** (`dom`, `prest`), not one.
- **Magee & Galinsky 2008.** Social hierarchy: the self-reinforcing nature of power and status. *Academy of Management Annals*, 2, 351-398, DOI 10.5465/19416520802211628 (confirmed in validation; SSRN working paper 10.2139/ssrn.1298493 also exists). Claim: **hierarchy is self-reinforcing**: higher power and status elicit behaviours (and others' deference) that sustain the position, i.e. a positive feedback loop [from abstract]. Verbal feedback diagram; no equation. It does supply the qualitative **sign** of the status-to-status coupling (positive feedback), which matches Bonabeau's winner effect.
- **Status loss.** No formal model located. Empirical: status loss associated with distress and health decline (Anderson 2015 review; Sapolsky's primate stress work, cross-ref 22 B3). The **rate** of adaptation after rank change is not modelled.
- **Status characteristics / expectation-states theory** (Berger, Fisek, Norman & Zelditch 1977, *Status Characteristics and Social Interaction*, book) is a mathematical sociology theory with a **combining function** for status cues into expected performance and thus influence. Cited here as the most formal human status model; **not read and not located in Crossref this session**; check before use.
- **Status-seeking utility** (positional goods; Frank 1985; Clark & Oswald comparison income) is covered in 21 (C). Static: utility depends on rank, no dynamics.
- **Empirical standing for 1.3.** Dominance/prestige distinction: replicated within one research tradition (Cheng, Tracy et al.); independence from other labs not established here. Self-reinforcement of hierarchy: plausible, mixed experimental support [cannot specify more].

**Status verdict.** Dynamics law: Bonabeau-type for animals; none validated for humans.

## 2. Belonging, affiliation, social contact

- **Baumeister & Leary 1995.** The need to belong. *Psychological Bulletin*, 117, 497-529. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.497 (confirmed). Claims: a fundamental need for frequent, affectively pleasant interactions with a few people in a stable, caring relation; **satiation** (once the need is met, little further motivation to form more bonds, and substitution among bonds is possible) and deprivation effects (distress, ill health). Verbal; no equation [from abstract/standard account]. The **satiation** and **substitutability** claims are the dynamic content. Neuroscience of the homeostatic version (set point, Tomova craving) is in 22; not repeated.
- **Williams 2009: temporal need-threat model of ostracism.** Williams, K. D. (2009). Ostracism: a temporal need-threat model. In M. P. Zanna (Ed.), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 41, 275-314. DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(08)00406-1 (confirmed). Also Williams 2007, Ostracism, *Annual Review of Psychology*, 58, 425-452, DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.58.110405.085641 (confirmed in validation).
  - **Vocabulary.** Three stages: **reflexive** (immediate pain, threat to four needs: belonging, self-esteem, control, meaningful existence), **reflective** (appraisal, coping, depends on moderators and how much the person cares), **resignation** (prolonged ostracism: alienation, helplessness, unworthiness, depression).
  - **Operands.** Four need levels (state vector, each with a "need satisfaction" scalar), moderated by importance.
  - **Operator/equations.** No equations [from abstract/standard account]. The model is a time-ordered **state machine**: `ostracise(a,b)` immediately lowers four needs in b; later, recovery or fortification (prosocial attempts to reconnect, or antisocial retaliation) depending on stage; chronic exposure transitions to resignation.
  - **Derived predictions.** Immediate effects are robust and unmoderated; later responses are moderated; chronic exposure produces learned-helplessness-like depression. Also the "ostracised become either more prosocial or more aggressive" branch.
  - **Empirical standing, blunt.** Hartgerink, van Beest, Wicherts & Williams 2015, *PLoS ONE* 10(5), e0127002, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0127002 (confirmed): meta-analysis of 120 Cyberball studies, N = 11,869 [abstract read]: average effect large (d > |1.4|), generalising across structural, sampling and outcome types; testing Williams's claim that immediate effects resist moderation while delayed ones do not, they found **both first and last measures moderated** and time since ostracism did not predict the last measure's effect, calling support "tenuous" and proposing modifications to the model. So the temporal claim is **weakly supported**. The reflexive effect on need satisfaction is robust; the **stage structure** (reflective, resignation) is not well tested because chronic ostracism cannot ethically be run in the lab; mostly self-report in single sessions. Cyberball (Williams & Jarvis 2006, DOI 10.1037/t66772-000, confirmed record) is the standard paradigm. Whether the pain overlap is neural is disputed (22 A5).
  - **Translation.** The best-developed verbal dynamic in the belonging domain. Operator `exclude(a,b)` with duration argument: instantaneous drop in b's four needs, then stage-dependent drift. Needs a quantitative fit it does not have.
- **Affiliation under threat.** Schachter (1959), *The Psychology of Affiliation*, book: fear increases the wish to wait with others, especially others in the same situation. Classic, small-sample; later reviews qualify (Gump & Kulik 1997, social support under stress) [recalled; neither DOI located]. Tend-and-befriend (below) is a related claim. In 17's coupling table this is `Fear -> Belonging`: signed positive, qualitatively supported, no functional form.
- **Social homeostasis (22).** Matthews & Tye 2019, Liu et al. 2025, Lee, Chen & Tye 2021: set-point and satiation circuits in rodents. These are the **only quantitative-circuit dynamics** for the belonging drive, and they are animal.

**Belonging verdict.** Staged verbal dynamics (human, partial evidence) plus animal set-point circuits (22). No equation.

## 3. Care, nurturance, attachment-giving

- **Trivers 1972: parental investment and sexual selection.** In B. Campbell (Ed.), *Sexual Selection and the Descent of Man*, 136-179. Crossref record is a 2017 reprint chapter, DOI 10.4324/9781315129266-7 (confirmed as reprint). Definition `[standard account]`: parental investment is anything a parent does for one offspring that increases its survival and reproduction at the cost of the parent's ability to invest in other offspring. Prediction: the sex investing more is the limiting resource, becomes choosy; the other competes. Verbal with an economic logic; no dynamic law.
- **Smith & Fretwell 1974: optimal offspring size.** The optimal balance between size and number of offspring. *American Naturalist*, 108, 499-506. DOI 10.1086/282929 (confirmed). `[standard account]` With total resource R and per-offspring investment m, number of offspring is R/m; maximising (R/m) f(m), where f is offspring fitness as a function of investment, gives the optimum f'(m) = f(m)/m (tangent condition). This is a **true optimisation theorem** for care allocation, but it is a trade-off, not a time dynamic.
- **Trivers 1974: parent-offspring conflict.** *American Zoologist*, 14, 249-264, DOI 10.1093/icb/14.1.249 (confirmed in validation). `[standard account; not read]` Let B be the benefit of a unit of investment to the current offspring and C its cost to the parent's future offspring, both in offspring-fitness units. The parent, equally related to all its offspring, favours investing while **B/C > 1**; the offspring, related to itself by 1 and to a full sibling by 1/2, favours receiving it while **B/C > 1/2** (equivalently, until C > 2B; for half-sibs, B/C > 1/4). In 1/2 < B/C < 1 (Trivers states it as 1 < C/B < 2) lies the **conflict zone**, e.g. weaning conflict. The form is standard and consistent across textbook accounts; check the page before quoting constants. Hamilton 1964, The genetical evolution of social behaviour I and II, *J. Theor. Biol.* 7, 1-16 and 17-52, DOIs 10.1016/0022-5193(64)90038-4 and 10.1016/0022-5193(64)90039-6 (confirmed in validation).
- **Tend-and-befriend.** Taylor, Klein, Lewis, Gruenewald, Gurung & Updegraff (2000). Biobehavioral responses to stress in females: tend-and-befriend, not fight-or-flight. *Psychological Review*, 107, 411-429. DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.107.3.411 (confirmed). Claim: under threat, females (especially) respond with protecting offspring (tending) and seeking social support (befriending), mediated by oxytocin and endogenous opioids. Verbal; no equation. Empirical standing: the sex difference and oxytocin mechanism are **contested** (oxytocin findings in humans have a poor replication record; cross-ref 22 B1). In coupling terms: `Fear -> Care` and `Fear -> Belonging` positive [from abstract].
- **Caregiving system.** Bowlby's caregiving behavioural system and its control-system form are in 33 (Bischof's Zurich model includes the parent side). Not repeated. Gap: no model of **caregiver burnout/depletion** (accumulation of cost), though the logic of Trivers (cost to other investment) implies a budget.
- **Translation.** Care is naturally a **budget/allocation** operand: investment budget B split among dependants by a marginal-value rule (Smith-Fretwell), with a conflict inequality between giver and receiver. This is the best candidate for a **theorem-bearing** sub-algebra among the social drives, but it is evolutionary, not psychological dynamics.

**Care verdict.** Optimisation theorems (Smith-Fretwell, Trivers conflict zone). No within-person satiation/decay dynamic.

## 4. Play

- **Panksepp & Burgdorf.** Burgdorf & Panksepp 2006, The neurobiology of positive emotions, *Neurosci. Biobehav. Rev.* 30, 173-187, DOI 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2005.06.001 (confirmed); Panksepp & Burgdorf 2003, "Laughing" rats, *Physiol. Behav.* 79, 533-547, DOI 10.1016/s0031-9384(03)00159-8 (cited in 22; confirmed there); Burgdorf, Panksepp & Moskal 2011, Frequency-modulated 50 kHz ultrasonic vocalizations: a tool for uncovering the molecular substrates of positive affect, *Neurosci. Biobehav. Rev.* 35, 1831-1836, DOI 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2010.11.011 (confirmed). Covered at theory level in 17 and 22.
  - **State variable.** A PLAY system whose activation is indexed by **50 kHz calls** and play-seeking. **Dynamics.** Social isolation of juveniles increases subsequent play (a **rebound**, i.e. a deprivation-accumulation effect), reported by Panksepp and colleagues from the 1980s (Panksepp, Siviy & Normansell 1985, confirmed in 33's validation) [from standard account, not read]. Play is suppressed by fear and by hunger (17). So play has a **deprivation-rebound** regularity and a **safety gating**: the only dynamics. No equation.
  - **Empirical standing.** Rat play and ultrasonic vocalisation: replicated in Panksepp's lab and others. Human generalisation ("laughter") and the interpretation as affect are contested (22).
- **Pellis and the 50:50 rule.** Pellis & Pellis 1998, Structure-function interface in the analysis of play fighting, in *Animal Play*, DOI 10.1017/cbo9780511608575.007 (confirmed record); Pellis & Pellis 2010, Rough-and-tumble play: training and using the social brain, DOI 10.1093/oxfordhb/9780195393002.013.0019 (confirmed record); a recent test in wolves: Essler, Cafazzo, Marshall-Pescini, Virányi, Kotrschal & Range 2016, Play behavior in wolves: using the "50:50" rule to test for egalitarian play styles, *PLoS ONE*, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0154150 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` In play fighting, partners **alternate roles** (attacker/defender) so each wins about half the time; play-fighting partners self-handicap (e.g. a dominant animal lets a subordinate win) to keep the interaction going. This is a **conserved-quantity-like regularity**: the sign of the outcome is balanced over a bout, unlike real fights. For the algebra: `play(a,b)` is a **role-symmetrising, outcome-balancing operator**, in contrast to `fight(a,b)` (Bonabeau), which updates status asymmetrically. A model: win probability held near 0.5 by self-handicapping, with the handicap proportional to the status difference. **No formal model located**; the 50:50 figure is an empirical average that varies by species and by status pair (added in validation: the wolf test [abstract read] **did not support the 50:50 rule** at any point; adult wolves self-handicapped less and attacked more than their pup partners, and only pup-pup dyads played more equally. So the rule is a hypothesis with species- and age-dependent support, not an established regularity).
- **Computational play drive.** Breazeal's Kismet (see 19) implements a "stimulation" drive satisfied by play with toys, a level with a homeostatic band and an optimal-intensity regime: an engineered, not empirical, play dynamic.
- **Play as exploration / learning.** Spinka, Newberry & Bekoff 2001 ("Mammalian play: training for the unexpected", *Q. Rev. Biol.*) argue play trains handling of unexpected loss of control (self-handicapping and role reversal). *Quarterly Review of Biology* 76, 141-168, DOI 10.1086/393866 (confirmed in validation; not read). Berlyne-style and learning-progress curiosity models (16, 19, 33) are the formal bridge. Human play dynamics (Gray, Geary) mostly verbal. Geary & Bjorklund 2000, Evolutionary developmental psychology, DOI 10.1111/1467-8624.00118 (confirmed record), is a general frame.
- **Translation.** Operand: `play-level` with a deprivation counter; gate by `safety` (`fear` suppresses). Operator `play` is symmetric and status-neutralising.

**Play verdict.** Regularities (rebound, safety gate, 50:50) but no dynamic law; a clean sort for the algebra because it contrasts with status operators.

## 5. Sex and mating

- **Kalick & Hamilton 1986: matching hypothesis simulation.** Kalick, S. M., & Hamilton, T. E. (1986). The matching hypothesis reexamined. *J. Personality and Social Psychology*, 51, 673-682. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.51.4.673 (confirmed). Exchange: Aron 1988, The matching hypothesis reconsidered again: comment on Kalick and Hamilton, *JPSP* 54, 441-446, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.54.3.441 (confirmed); reply Kalick & Hamilton 1988, Closer look at a matching simulation: reply to Aron, *JPSP* 54, 447-451, DOI 10.1037//0022-3514.54.3.447 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` Simulation: if every person simply prefers more attractive partners (no "matching" to own level) and a pair forms only when acceptance is mutual, **couples still end up positively correlated in attractiveness**; observed matching therefore does not require a matching preference. This is an **emergent-from-mutual-choice** result, a real formal argument; secondary accounts [read] confirm the set-up (closed, balanced population, attractiveness as one number). Exact acceptance function and the reported correlations not checked; Aron (1988) disputed the interpretation.
- **Todd & Miller: mate search.** Todd, P. M., & Miller, G. F. (1999), From pride and prejudice to persuasion: satisficing in mate search, in Gigerenzer, Todd & ABC Research Group, *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart* (Oxford UP; no DOI checked). The DOI earlier given here, 10.1037/e615882011-142, is **Todd & Beckage** (2009), Sequential decision mechanisms in human mate choice (PsycEXTRA conference record), not Todd & Miller (corrected in validation). `[secondary accounts, read via search]` Mate search as **sequential satisficing with an aspiration level** set from an initial sample of candidates; in mutual search, pairs form when both aspirations are met, and simple rules that raise the threshold after unexpected interest and lower it after unexpected rejection learn one's market position quickly. The earlier "37%-rule-like" gloss is the one-sided secretary-problem optimum, which Todd & Miller compare against; it is not their rule. A usable **threshold-update** dynamic: aspiration a_t moves with offers and rejections.
- **Mate-preference integration.** Conroy-Beam & Buss (2016), How are mate preferences linked with actual mate selection? Tests of mate preference integration algorithms using computer simulations and actual mating couples, *PLoS ONE* 11, e0156078, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0156078 (confirmed in validation; not read): Euclidean-distance integration of a preference vector against a partner's trait vector. Located; content not checked.
- **Stated preferences do not predict choices.** Eastwick & Finkel 2008, Sex differences in mate preferences revisited: do people know what they initially desire in a romantic partner? *JPSP* 94, 245-264, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.94.2.245 (confirmed). `[from abstract]` In speed dating, the usual sex differences in stated preferences (physical attractiveness vs earning prospects) **did not predict actual choices** after meeting. A caution for any model with a fixed preference vector.
- **Sexual motivation: incentive and dual-control models.** Bancroft & Janssen 2000, The dual control model of male sexual response, *Neurosci. Biobehav. Rev.* 24, 571-579, DOI 10.1016/s0149-7634(00)00024-5 (confirmed). `[from abstract]` Sexual response = balance of **excitation** and **inhibition** systems, with individual differences in both thresholds; no equation, but two competing signed terms, i.e. net drive = E - I. Toates's incentive motivation framework (Toates 1986 book; later *J. Sex Research* 2009 integrative framework) and Ågmo 2007 (*Functional and Dysfunctional Sexual Behavior*, DOI 10.1016/b978-012370590-7/50005-0 is the chapter on the stimulus control of sexual incentive motivation, confirmed record) emphasise that sexual motivation is **incentive-driven** (the stimulus raises motivation; there is no accumulating deficit like hunger). Hull-Spence style product of state x incentive is the standard form [standard account; see 16]. **Satiation**: post-ejaculatory refractory period is the clearest **fast decay/accumulation** dynamic (male rodent copulatory pattern, Coolidge effect), but it is physiological; not read here.
- **Operational sex ratio.** Clutton-Brock & Parker 1992, Potential reproductive rates and the operation of sexual selection, *Q. Rev. Biol.* 67, 437-456, DOI 10.1086/417793 (confirmed): OSR (ratio of sexually receptive males to females) predicts the intensity of competition. Kokko, Klug & Jennions 2012 (author list corrected in validation: Klug was omitted), Unifying cornerstones of sexual selection: operational sex ratio, Bateman gradient and the scope for competitive investment, *Ecology Letters* 15, 1340-1351, DOI 10.1111/j.1461-0248.2012.01859.x (confirmed) [abstract read]: OSR and the **Bateman gradient** (fitness gain per mating) are incomplete in complementary ways (the OSR measures the difficulty of getting a mating, the gradient its payoff), which explains why OSR sometimes fails to predict selection strength; "dry time" after mating enters the account. Not "OSR is a poor predictor and the gradient is the key", as earlier stated. Emlen & Oring 1977 (mating systems) is the classical source [not located]. Human sex-ratio effects: Schmitt 2005, Sociosexuality from Argentina to Zimbabwe, *Behav. Brain Sci.*, DOI 10.1017/S0140525X05000051 (confirmed): correlates of sociosexuality with sex ratio and other variables across 48 nations; observational, not causal [from abstract]. The Guttentag & Secord sex-ratio thesis (1983 book) is not located.
- **Translation.** Mating gives the algebra a **matching/market** operator (mutual acceptance threshold, aspiration adaptation) and a **supply-demand** coupling (OSR changes the competition intensity, which couples to status and aggression). Sexual drive itself: incentive-gated, with refractory decay, not a clean accumulator in humans.

**Sex verdict.** Formal models exist for **choice** (matching simulation, sequential search, stable matching in 40) but not for the **drive's dynamics**; for the latter, only qualitative dual-control and incentive frames.

## 6. Autonomy, competence, mastery

- **SDT dynamics.** Ryan & Deci 2000, Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being, *American Psychologist* 55, 68-78, DOI 10.1037/0003-066x.55.1.68 (confirmed); taxonomy treatment in 15. The dynamic claims `[standard account]`: **need satisfaction** (autonomy, competence, relatedness) predicts wellbeing and intrinsic motivation; controlling contexts and **tangible, expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation** (the overjustification/undermining effect; Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin* 125, 627-668 DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627, confirmed in validation). Verbal; the quantity "intrinsic motivation" has no stated update equation. Standing: the undermining effect is reasonably replicated for tangible expected rewards and contested in scope (e.g. effects on performance, and whether it is cognitive-evaluation or simply information); the three-needs structure is partly supported, partly criticised as a typology [cannot give a specific citation]. See 15 for more.
- **Effectance and mastery (33, White 1959).** Not repeated. **Self-efficacy** (Bandura 1977, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.84.2.191, confirmed) is the closest thing to a **competence state variable**, with four sources (mastery experience, vicarious experience, persuasion, physiological state): an informal update rule (each success raises, each failure lowers, weighted by source). No equation.
- **Learned helplessness / controllability as a dynamic.** Maier & Seligman 2016, Learned helplessness at fifty: insights from neuroscience, *Psychological Review* 123, 349-367. DOI 10.1037/rev0000033 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` Abstract [read]: "the original theory got it backward." Passivity in response to shock is **not learned**; it is the **default, unlearned response to prolonged aversive events**, mediated by serotonergic activity of the dorsal raphe nucleus. What is learned is **control**: the **medial** prefrontal cortex subserves detection of control, which automatically inhibits the dorsal raphe; alterations of the **ventromedial** PFC-dorsal raphe pathway can come to subserve the **expectation** of control. (Corrected: the survey assigned detection to vmPFC; the abstract says mPFC for detection, vmPFC pathway for expectation.) Dynamic consequences: (1) **immunisation**, prior controllable stress blunting the response to later uncontrollable stress `[standard account; the abstract does not use the word, but Maier 2015 below is titled for exactly this]`; (2) the state is *controllability detection/expectation*. Related: **Maier 2015** (sole author; the survey's "Maier, Amat et al." was wrong), Behavioral control blunts reactions to contemporaneous and future adverse events: medial prefrontal cortex plasticity and a corticostriatal network, *Neurobiology of Stress* 1, 12-22, DOI 10.1016/j.ynstr.2014.09.003 (confirmed). A computational form: Huys & Dayan 2009, A Bayesian formulation of behavioral control, *Cognition* 113, 314-328, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2009.01.008 (confirmed in validation; not read). Standing: robust in animals (rodents), human lab replications are mixed and the revision is a theoretical re-interpretation; whether human depression follows is contested.
- **Translation.** `control(i)` is a **belief state** (probability the agent's action affects outcome), updated by contingency evidence; low control + sustained adversity -> passive/low-drive state; high control -> buffering. This is the strongest **dynamic with a real operator** here (immunisation = a one-way protective update), and links to fear (28) and to Williams' resignation stage (helplessness).

**Autonomy/competence verdict.** One causal-mechanism dynamic (controllability) with animal neuroscience; SDT is typological.

## 7. Fairness and justice

Cross-ref 21 for Fehr-Schmidt, Bolton-Ockenfels and psychological games; 21 treats the static inequity-aversion utility. Added here:
- **Brosnan & de Waal 2003.** Monkeys reject unequal pay. *Nature*, 425, 297-299. DOI 10.1038/nature01963 (confirmed). Capuchins refused a cucumber after seeing a partner receive a grape [from abstract]. **Replication problems, blunt** (corrected in validation; abstracts read): Bräuer, Call & Tomasello 2006, Are apes really inequity averse? *Proc. R. Soc. B* 273, 3123-3128, DOI 10.1098/rspb.2006.3693 (confirmed), tested **all four great ape species**, adding controls to Brosnan, Schiff & de Waal's 2005 *chimpanzee* study (not the 2003 capuchin one). Their apes showed the **opposite** pattern: they ignored *fewer* food pieces and begged more when a conspecific got better food; the authors favour a **food-expectation** hypothesis (seeing another get better food raises one's own expectation) over inequity aversion. (The survey had said refusals "tracked the presence of the better food"; the paper reports no inequity-driven refusal at all.) Brosnan, Talbot, Ahlgren, Lambeth & Schapiro 2010, Mechanisms underlying responses to inequitable outcomes in chimpanzees, *Animal Behaviour* 79, 1229-1237, DOI 10.1016/j.anbehav.2010.02.019 (confirmed): 16 chimpanzees, eight conditions; they **did** respond to inequity, but **only when a task was required** (not for free rewards); effort and procedure did not change this; males were more sensitive to social, females to individual expectation. Dog and other species studies exist (not located here). The honest summary: **the animal inequity-aversion claim is contested** and an alternative (food-contrast or effort-based frustration) is not excluded.
- **Development of fairness.** Blake & McAuliffe 2011, "I had so much it didn't seem fair": eight-year-olds reject two forms of inequity, *Cognition* 120, 215-224, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2011.04.006 (confirmed): young children reject disadvantageous inequity from about age 4, and **advantageous inequity only from about 8** in a US sample [from abstract]. Sheskin, Bloom & Wynn 2014, Anti-equality: social comparison in young children, *Cognition* 130, 152-156, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2013.10.008 (confirmed): some children prefer to win relatively even at an absolute cost. Blake et al. 2015, The ontogeny of fairness in seven societies, *Nature* 528, 258-261, DOI 10.1038/nature15703 (confirmed): disadvantageous-inequity rejection appears across societies, **advantageous-inequity rejection is present in only a few and appears later** [from abstract]. McAuliffe, Blake & Steinbeis 2017, The developmental foundations of human fairness, *Nature Human Behaviour* 1, 0042, DOI 10.1038/s41562-016-0042 (confirmed) is the review. These give **age-dependent thresholds**: the fairness drive's two parameters (alpha disadvantage, beta advantage in Fehr-Schmidt terms) **emerge at different ages and vary by culture**. That is the only developmental trajectory for any social drive parameter in this file.
- **Evolutionary dynamics of fairness.** Nowak, Page & Sigmund 2000, Fairness versus reason in the ultimatum game, *Science* 289, 1773-1775, DOI 10.1126/science.289.5485.1773 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` Replicator dynamics on strategies (proposal level p, acceptance threshold q) in a finite population with **reputation** (players know past acceptance thresholds): the population converges to fair splits (near 50:50) when a player can learn others' thresholds, not under purely random pairing. Abstract [read] confirms the headline: "fairness will evolve if the proposer can obtain some information on what deals the responder has accepted in the past", linking fairness to reputation; without that information evolution goes to the "rational" low offer. The specific "near 50:50" end-point, the finite-population detail and the (p, q) parametrisation are `[standard account]`, not checked. A true **dynamics + convergence result**, but about a population of strategies, not an individual drive's time course.
- **Translation.** The fairness operand is a pair of **parameters** (alpha, beta) with a developmental/culture function, plus a population-level replicator dynamic for norm change. No within-person accumulation (e.g. "grievance builds up") model located, though 21's Battigalli-Dufwenberg-Smith frustration and anger is the nearest.

**Fairness verdict.** Static utility (21), development data (here), evolutionary dynamics (Nowak). Animal inequity aversion contested.

## 8. Meaning, significance, uncertainty-reduction

Cross-ref 24 (significance quest) and 18 (uncertainty-identity); terror management via 17 only.
- **Need for closure.** Kruglanski & Webster 1996, Motivated closing of the mind: "seizing" and "freezing", *Psychological Review* 103, 263-283. DOI 10.1037/0033-295x.103.2.263 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` The need for cognitive closure is a motive for a definite answer over ambiguity; it has two tendencies: **urgency** (seize on early information) and **permanence** (freeze on it). Raised by situational factors (time pressure, noise, fatigue, boredom) and dispositionally. Measurement: Webster & Kruglanski 1994, Individual differences in need for cognitive closure, *JPSP* 67, 1049-1062, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.67.6.1049 (confirmed); Kruglanski, Webster & Klem 1993, JPSP 65, 861-876, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.65.5.861 (confirmed). Review: Roets, Kruglanski, Kossowska et al., *Three decades of need for closure research* (2017 chapter, DOI 10.4324/9781315171388-3, confirmed record).
  - **State variable.** NFC (dispositional x situational), a scalar. **Dynamics.** Situational raise then **closure reduces the need** (satiation by commitment to an answer, "freezing"), but the update rule is not stated. **Coupling.** NFC raises attachment to group consensus, deference to authority and in-group favouritism; consistent with 18 (uncertainty-identity). Standing: the scale is widely used and has predictive validity in lab studies; experimental manipulations (time pressure, fatigue) are weaker; the political-conservatism relation exists but is moderate [from standard account].
- **Meaning Maintenance Model.** Heine, Proulx & Vohs 2006, The meaning maintenance model: on the coherence of social motivations, *Pers. Soc. Psychol. Review* 10, 88-110, DOI 10.1207/s15327957pspr1002_1 (confirmed). `[from abstract]` Violations of expected associations (meaning threats) cause **fluid compensation**: the person strengthens other meaning frameworks (affirming other beliefs, in-group, order). Verbal; the claim that distinct threats (death, uncertainty, social rejection) are substitutable is the algebraic content: a **compensation operator**, but the empirical standing of specific compensation effects is mixed (several priming studies in this literature have replication concerns; not itemised here).
- **Translation.** Operand `uncertainty-pressure` u_i (cognitive), operator `commit` (reduces u, increases rigidity). Higher u: higher coupling to group consensus. No equation beyond the qualitative.

**Meaning verdict.** Scalar motive with known levers, direction of effects, no dynamics law.

## 9. Aggression, anger, dominance-aggression

- **General Aggression Model.** Anderson & Bushman 2002, Human aggression, *Annual Review of Psychology* 53, 27-51. DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.53.100901.135231 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` Inputs (person factors, situation factors) feed a **present internal state** (cognition, affect, arousal) which produces **appraisal and decision** (immediate automatic or reappraised) and an outcome (thoughtful or impulsive action) that feeds back to the social encounter. **No equations**; a process diagram with an episode loop. It integrates social learning, cognitive neoassociation, scripts, excitation transfer. Learning over repeated episodes changes the knowledge structures (the long-run dynamic).
- **Critiques, blunt.** (i) It is a **verbal flowchart** that fits nearly any result; hard to falsify. (ii) The violent-media evidence base has been challenged: Hilgard, Engelhardt & Rouder 2017, Overstated evidence for short-term effects of violent games on affect and behavior: a reanalysis of Anderson et al. (2010), *Psychological Bulletin* 143, 757-774, DOI 10.1037/bul0000074 (confirmed) [abstract read]: substantial publication bias in the **experimental** literature; after adjustment the effect on aggressive **behaviour** is "very small" and on aggressive **affect** "much reduced"; the **cross-sectional** correlations appear largely unbiased; and the original's "best-practices" experiments show more bias, not larger effects; the original meta-analysis is Anderson et al. 2010, DOI 10.1037/a0018251 (confirmed). Bushman et al. defend; the dispute is live. (iii) Catharsis (hydraulic) models are disconfirmed (Bushman 2002, not located). Net: **lab aggression effects of media are small and contested**, the GAM's structure is not what is in dispute; its *quantitative* content is.
- **Frustration-aggression.** Berkowitz 1989, Frustration-aggression hypothesis: examination and reformulation, *Psychological Bulletin* 106, 59-73, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.106.1.59 (confirmed). Reformulation: frustration produces aggression only via **negative affect**, with cognitive appraisal modulating (cognitive neoassociation). Covered via 17 and the table there; the coupling `blocked Wanting -> Aggression` is positive, theory plus experiments.
- **Threatened egotism.** Baumeister, Smart & Boden 1996, Relation of threatened egotism to violence and aggression: the dark side of high self-esteem, *Psychological Review* 103, 5-33, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.103.1.5 (confirmed record). Claim: aggression follows **status/ego threat** among those with favourable but unstable self-views [from abstract]. This is a `status threat -> aggression` coupling, qualitatively. Standing: contested (the high-self-esteem claim in particular).
- **Dominance-aggression.** Mazur & Booth 1998 (biosocial model of status, testosterone) in 22 B3; Bonabeau (1.2) provides the aggression-as-fight event. Collins 2008, *Violence: A Micro-sociological Theory* (book; not read) is a **situational** model: violence is hard because of **confrontational tension and fear**, and occurs when tension resolves onto a weak victim; not located in Crossref; useful as a counterpoint that aggression is fear-limited, not drive-driven.
- **Anger** is separately modelled game-theoretically in 21 (Battigalli, Dufwenberg & Smith 2019). Not repeated.
- **Translation.** `aggression` as a **gated output** (not an independent accumulating drive): triggered by frustration/threat via negative affect, limited by fear, modulated by status. This is a defensible algebraic design: aggression as an operator output, not an operand.

**Aggression verdict.** Rich process models, no dynamics law; the main empirical pillar (media effects) is under reanalysis.

## 10. Disgust, contamination, behavioural immune system

- **Tybur, Lieberman, Kurzban & DeScioli 2013.** Disgust: evolved function and structure. *Psychological Review* 120, 65-84. DOI 10.1037/a0030778 (confirmed). `[from abstract/standard account]` Three functionally distinct domains: **pathogen disgust** (avoid infectious agents), **sexual disgust** (avoid costly mating partners, e.g. close kin), **moral disgust** (condemn norm violations, as a social-exchange/coordination device). Each with its own triggers and outputs. Scale: Tybur, Lieberman & Griskevicius 2009, Microbes, mating, and morality, *JPSP* 97, 103-122, DOI 10.1037/a0015474 (confirmed). No equations; a functional typology.
- **Behavioural immune system.** Schaller & Park 2011, The behavioral immune system (and why it matters), *Current Directions in Psych. Science* 20, 99-103, DOI 10.1177/0963721411402596 (confirmed in validation) and Faulkner et al. 2004 (cited in 17) give: perceived infection vulnerability raises disgust sensitivity and xenophobia. **Dynamic structure**: a threat-detection gate (vulnerability/salience) with a **bias toward false positives** (smoke-detector principle). Cross-cultural: Murray & Schaller 2010, Historical prevalence of infectious diseases within 230 geopolitical regions, *J. Cross-Cultural Psychology*, DOI 10.1177/0022022109349510 (confirmed; the data paper); the pathogen-to-culture link is contested (25: Currie & Mace 2012; Hruschka & Henrich 2013) — **do not repeat; see 25**.
- **Moral disgust standing.** The claim that disgust causes harsher moral judgement has a modest, publication-bias-prone literature (Landy & Goodwin 2015, Does incidental disgust amplify moral judgment? A meta-analytic review of experimental evidence, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10, 518-536, DOI 10.1177/1745691615583128, confirmed in validation; finding "small, bias-prone" is `[standard account]`, not read); Tybur 2013's distinction between a pathogen-disgust and moral-disgust function is itself a hypothesis.
- **Formal dynamics.** No disgust equation located. The epidemic-model analogue (behaviour change in response to prevalence, SIR with avoidance) is a general epidemiology literature not surveyed here; see 40 for compositional dynamics and 25 for the culture link.
- **Translation.** `contamination` as a **binary-ish tag on operands** (contagion operator: contact with a tagged entity tags the contacting agent; avoidance operator). This fits Tybur's three domains as three tag types, and reflects the "law of contagion" in the folk concept. A distinctive algebraic structure (non-commutative-looking transmission, irreversible tagging) not offered by other drives.

**Disgust verdict.** Typology plus qualitative levers; no dynamics law.

## What exists versus what does not

**Exists.**
- Status: a real model with state, update and phase transition (Bonabeau). Animal-derived.
- Care: genuine optimisation and conflict theorems, not psychological dynamics.
- Fairness: developmental parameter trajectories (rare, cross-cultural); evolutionary dynamics for norms.
- Autonomy/competence: a dynamic with a mechanism (controllability) from neuroscience.
- Belonging: staged verbal model with a meta-analysis (partial support), plus animal homeostat.
- Mating: formal choice models; no drive dynamics.

**Drives with NO dynamic model anywhere (found):**
1. **Play** as a human drive (only rodent rebound and the 50:50 hypothesis, which failed its wolf test). An engineered play-stimulation drive exists in Kismet (19), unvalidated.
2. **Sexual motivation** in humans (excitation-inhibition frame only).
3. **Meaning / need for closure** (no update equation).
4. **Aggression/anger** as a drive (process diagram; not a quantity with dynamics). Corrected: there *was* one, Lorenz's psychohydraulic accumulation model (see 16), abandoned because aggression does not reliably build with time since the last bout. So "no model" should read "the one accumulation model failed".
5. **Disgust** (typology and gates only).
6. **Care** as psychological state (no depletion/satiation model of caregiving). Bischof's Zurich model (33) is a real control-system model of the *child's* side of attachment; its own authors name the missing caregiver side as the deficiency. Kismet (19) treats the human caregiver as an external regulator of the robot's drives, not as a drive.
7. **Status desire** in humans (no validated update law; adaptation rate unknown). Nearest human dynamics: Sadler et al. 2009's observational dominance-warmth complementarity (20), and status-to-physiology evidence in fish (29, Fernald).
8. **Fairness** as within-person accumulation (grievance).
9. Group-level versions of every one of these (group belonging, group status, group fairness) beyond the collective-dynamics literature in 23, 18 and 29.

**Gaps that matter for the equation set.** (a) None of these models gives **cross-drive coupling constants**; couplings exist only as signs. (b) There is no common **time scale** assignment (fast arousal vs slow need). (c) Individual-level human prediction from these models is untested; 07 warns about this record. (d) Therefore the algebra's equations for these drives would be **hypotheses to be fitted**, with the dynamics shapes borrowed (Bonabeau for status; staged state machine for belonging; controllability belief for helplessness; allocation inequality for care).
